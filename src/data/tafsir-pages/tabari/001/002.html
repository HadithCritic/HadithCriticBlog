Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds.
Arabic & English
01

On praise and gratitude

The meaning of Praise be to God is: thanks to God alone, to the exclusion of everything else that is worshipped besides Him and everything He has created (1), for the blessings He bestowed (2) on His servants that number cannot count and that none but Him can encompass in number, in the soundness of the faculties for obeying Him, and in enabling the limbs of the bodies of those charged with duties to perform His obligations, together with the provision He spread out for them in their worldly life and the pleasant living with which He nourished them, without their having any claim (3) on Him for it, and together with what He alerted them to and called them to, of the means leading to everlasting permanence in the abode of residence, in lasting bliss. To our Lord belongs the praise for all of that, first and last. The report has come (4) from Ibn ʿAbbās and others with the interpretation we gave of the words of our Lord, majestic is His praise and hallowed be His names: Praise be to God.

03

The language of al-ḥamd

There is no dispute among those who know the languages of the Arabs in judging as correct the saying “Praise be to God, in thanks” (al-ḥamdu lillāhi shukran). It is thus evident (5), since this is sound among all of them (6), that praise (7) can be uttered in the place of thanks, and thanks can be put in the place of praise; for if that were not so, it would not be permissible to say: “Praise be to God, in thanks.” It would be excluded from the speaker's words: “Praise be to God” as a verbal noun of “I thank” (ashkuru); for if shukr did not have the meaning of ḥamd, it would be an error to derive from ḥamd a verbal noun other (1) than its meaning and other than its wording (2).

Question

What is the reason for the entry of alif and lām into al-ḥamd? Why was it not said: ḥamdan lillāhi rabbi l-ʿālamīn?

al-Ṭabarī responds

The entry of alif and lām into al-ḥamd has a meaning that the speaker's “ḥamdan lillāh” (3), without alif and lām, does not convey. That is because their entry into al-ḥamd [announces] (4) that its meaning is: all praises and complete thanks belong to God. Had the two been dropped, it would indicate only that the speaker's praise belongs to God to the exclusion of all praises, since the meaning of the speaker's “ḥamdan lillāh,” or [“ḥamdun lillāh”] (5), is: I praise God with praise. But the interpretation of the speaker's Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds when reciting Sūrat Umm al-Qurʾān is not “I praise God”; the interpretation is rather what we described before, that all praises belong to God by virtue of His divinity and His bestowal on His creation of the blessings He bestowed on them, which have no equal (6) in religion and this world, the present and the future. For this reason the reading of the reciters and scholars of the community has been continuous in the nominative for al-ḥamd in Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds, rather than the accusative, which would indicate that the meaning of the one who recites it so is: I praise God with praise.

Were a reciter to recite it with the accusative (7), in my view he would distort its meaning, and deserve punishment for reciting it so, if he deliberately recites it so while knowing its error and the corruption of its interpretation.

Question

What is the meaning of His words Praise be to God? Did God praise Himself, majestic is His praise, and extol Himself, and then teach it to us so that we say it as He said it and described Himself with it? If that is so, what is the reason for His words, Exalted is His mention, You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help, when He, mighty is His mention, is the one worshipped and not a worshipper? Or (1) is this from the saying (2) of Gabriel, or of Muḥammad, the Messenger of God ﷺ? In that case it would be void for this to be God's speech.

al-Ṭabarī responds

Rather, all of this is the speech of God, majestic is His praise; but He, majestic is His mention, praised Himself and extolled Himself with what He is [deserving of] (3), then taught that to His servants and made its recitation obligatory for them, as a test and trial from Him of them. So He said to them: Say: Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds, and say: You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.

His words (4) You alone we worship are among what He, majestic is His mention, taught them to say and to profess in its meaning, and this is joined to His words Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds; it is as if He said: Say this, and this.

Question

Where are His words “Say,” such that the interpretation of this would be what you claimed?

al-Ṭabarī responds

We have shown in what preceded that (5) it is the habit of the Arabs, when they know the place of the word and do not doubt (6) that its hearer knows from what they voiced of their speech what they omitted, to omit what is rendered unnecessary by the apparent part of their speech, particularly if the omitted word is a saying or has the sense (1) of a saying, as the poet said (2): And I know that I shall become a grave (3) (ramsan) (4), when the swift camels (5) set out and he does not set out / The askers (6) said: [For whom did you dig] (7)? / And the informers (8) said to them: Wazīr.

He means by this: and the informers (8) said to them: The dead man is Wazīr. So he dropped “the dead man,” since he had said what indicates it. Likewise the words of another (9): [And you saw your husband in the battle] (10), having slung on a sword and a spear He knew that a spear is not slung on, [and that he only] (1) meant: and carrying a spear. But since the meaning was known, he relied on what appeared of his speech instead of expressing what he omitted from it. They also say to the traveller when they bid him farewell: “Accompanied, in good health!” [By this is meant: Travel accompanied, in good health! And they omit] (2) “travel” and “set out”, because its meaning is known even if its mention is dropped. Likewise what was omitted from God's words, Exalted is His mention: Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds: since His words, majestic is His mention, You alone we worship made known what He meant by Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds of the sense of His command to His servants, the indication of what appeared of the saying made it unnecessary to state what was omitted.

We have also related the report (3) we mentioned at the outset, in the interpretation (4) of God's words Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds, from Ibn ʿAbbās, that he used to say: Gabriel said to Muḥammad: Say, O Muḥammad: Praise be to God, Lord of the Worlds. And we showed that Gabriel only taught Muḥammad ﷺ what he was commanded to teach him, and this report announces the soundness of what we said in the interpretation of that (5).

04

The meaning of “Lord”

The explanation of the interpretation of God's name Allāh, in In the name of God, has already been given, so there is no need for us to repeat it in this place (6). As for the interpretation of His words Lord: in the speech of the Arabs, rabb has several meanings (1). The master who is obeyed among them (2) is called rabb. Of this is the verse of Labīd b. Rabīʿa (3): And they destroyed one day the rabb of Kinda and his son, and the rabb of Maʿadd, between Khabt (4) and ʿArʿar (5). By “the rabb of Kinda” he means: the master of Kinda. And of this is the verse of al-Nābigha of Banū Dhubyān (6): She hastens to al-Nuʿmān until she reaches him; [a ransom for you] (7) be my new (8) and inherited (9) wealth, from a rabb. And the man who sets a thing right is called a rabb (10). Of this is the verse of al-Farazdaq b. Ghālib (11): They were like a foolish ghee-maker who, when she stored her ghee (12), stored it in a hide that had not been cured (marbūb).

By this he means: in a hide that had not been set right (muṣlaḥ).

By his words afḍat ilayka he means: my ribāba (4) reached you, and you became the one who takes charge (tarubbu) of my affair and sets it right, when I left the ribāba of others among the kings (5) who were over me before you, who neglected my affair and left off looking after it. They are the rubūb, of which the singular is rabb; and the owner of a thing is called its rabb. The meaning of rabb also varies in other respects, but these return to one of these three aspects. So our Lord, majestic is His praise, is the master who has no peer (6) and no like in the like (7) of His sovereignty; the one who sets right the affairs of His creation with the blessings He has lavished on them; and the owner to whom belong creation and command. A report [similar to what] (8) we said in the interpretation of His words, majestic is His praise, Lord of the Worlds, has come from Ibn ʿAbbās.

05

The meaning of “the Worlds”

al-ʿālamūn is the plural of ʿālam, and al-ʿālam is a plural that has no singular from its own wording, like al-anām, al-rahṭ, [and al-jaysh] (4), and similar nouns that are set down for a collective with no singular from its wording. Al-ʿālam is a name for the classes of nations, and each class of them is a ʿālam, and the people of each generation of each class are the ʿālam of that generation and that time; so humankind is a ʿālam, and each people of a time among them is the ʿālam of that time; the jinn are a ʿālam, and likewise the other kinds of creation, each kind of which is the ʿālam of its time (5). For that reason it was made plural and ʿālamūn was said. Its singular is a plural because the ʿālam of every time of these is the ʿālam of that time. Of this is the verse of al-ʿAjjāj (6): Khindif (7) are the head of this ʿālam. So he made them the ʿālam of their time.

This view that we have stated is the view of Ibn ʿAbbās and Saʿīd b. Jubayr, and it is the meaning of the statement of the generality of the exegetes.

About the edition

Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (d. 310 AH), Jāmiʿ al-bayān ʿan taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān. The English of this surah is from the supplied English translation, Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī = Jāmiʿ al-bayān ʿan taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān (version 1422 AH / 2001 CE, first edition).

The supplied source identifies the Dār Hajar edition and its editors but does not name the English translator.

Translation passages are presented by verse; paragraph, report and quotation groupings are editorial. The English wording is preserved.

The editor's notes are those of ʿAbd Allāh al-Turkī’s edition (Dār Hajr, 2001), cited by volume, page and note number. Verse numbering follows the Kufan count; the Fātiḥa verse wording follows the supplied English translation of al-Ṭabarī.

How this page is set out: the commentary is read into blocks (the commentator's own explanation, transmitted reports with their chains, quoted verses, objections and answers, cited poetry) and grouped into sections. These divisions, their titles and the highlighted verse wording are editorial; the words are unchanged, except that a backtick standing for the letter ʿayn is set as ʿ. Qur'anic wording the translator marked with braces is highlighted; turn highlights off to see the braces.
