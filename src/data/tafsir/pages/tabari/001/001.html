Arabic & English
01

The opening formula

God, exalted be His mention and hallowed be His names, instructed His Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, by teaching him to put the mention of His most beautiful names before all his actions, and charged him to describe himself with them before all his important concerns. What He instructed and taught him of this He made a custom for all His creation to follow, and a path for them to walk, (4) in opening the beginnings of their speech and at the heads of their letters, their books, and their needs, so that the significance of what appears in the speaker's words In the name of God sufficed for what is hidden of his intent, which is omitted. That is because the bāʾ in In the name of God requires a verb to govern it, and no verb is expressed with it. The hearer of the one who says In the name of God therefore needs nothing beyond knowing the speaker's intent, without the speaker stating that intent in words, since every speaker who says it at the start of a matter has brought his speech to it, either together with it or before it without separation, something that spares his hearer from needing an explicit indication of what he began his saying with it for.

The hearer's sufficiency with this, without the speaker expressing what is omitted, resembles his sufficiency when he hears someone asked: “What did you eat today?” and he replies: “Food.” He has no need to repeat “I ate” with “food,” because the question put to him about what he ate has already made clear to him that this is the meaning. It is therefore understood that when a speaker says In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful and then begins reciting a surah, his following In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful with the recitation of the surah (1) announces (2) the meaning of his words In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful, and it is understood from it that he means: I recite in the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. Likewise his saying “In the name of God” when he rises to stand, or sits, or does any of his other actions announces (3) the meaning he intends by “In the name of God,” and that by his saying “In the name of God” he meant: I stand in the name of God, I sit in the name of God.

And so with all other actions.

What we have said in interpreting this is the meaning of the words of Ibn ʿAbbās, which Abū Kurayb related to us:

Transmitted explanation 01

ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās

Transmission wording
ʿUthmān b. Saʿīd told us: Bishr b. ʿUmāra told us: Abū Rawq told us, from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās, who said:
The first thing with which Gabriel came down to Muḥammad ﷺ, was that he said: O Muḥammad, say: I seek refuge in the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing from Satan the accursed. Then he said: Say: In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. He said: Gabriel said to him (1): In the name of God, O Muḥammad. He means: Recite with the remembrance of God your Lord, and stand and sit with the remembrance of God.

If someone says to us: If the interpretation of God's words In the name of God is what you have described, and what governs the bāʾ in In the name of God is what you mentioned, how can In the name of God be said in the sense of: I recite in the name of God, or: I stand [in the name of God] (2), or: I sit in the name of God? You know that everyone who recites the Book of God recites it with God's help and success, and that everyone who stands, sits, or does any deed does so through God, his standing, sitting and deed being by God. And why was it not said, since that is so: “By God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful,” and not In the name of God? For the speaker's words “I stand and sit by God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful,” or “I recite by God,” are clearer in meaning to the hearer than “In the name of God,” since the words “I stand,” or (3) “I sit in the name of God” lead the hearer to suppose that his standing and sitting are by something other than God.

It is said to him, and success is with God: What is intended by that meaning is other than what you imagined to yourself. The meaning of In the name of God is rather: I begin with the naming of God and the remembrance of Him before everything; or: I recite by naming Him (4); or: I stand and sit by my naming God and remembering Him. [It does not mean] (5) that he intends by his saying In the name of God: I stand by God, or: I recite by God, such that the speaker's words “I recite by God,” or “I stand,” or “I sit by God” would be closer to the correct way than In the name of God.

Question

If the matter is as you describe, how was In the name of God said, when you know that ism is a noun, and that tasmiya (naming) is a verbal noun from your saying sammaytu?

al-Ṭabarī responds

The Arabs sometimes produce verbal nouns in varied nouns, without their regular form, as they say: akramtu fulānan karāma. The verbal noun of “afʿaltu,” when brought out on the pattern of its verb, is ifʿāl. Likewise: ahantuhu hawānan, and kallamtuhu kalāman. The verbal noun of “faʿʿaltu” is tafʿīl. Of this is the verse of the poet (1): Is it ingratitude after you turned death away from me, and after your gift of the hundred pasturing camels?

He means: iʿṭāʾuka (your giving). And the verse of another (2) poet: If this miserliness from you is a trait of character, then in my long (ṭūlī) hoping for you I have been an Ashʿab. He means: in (3) my prolonging (iṭālatī) my hope in you. And the verse of another (4): O Ẓulaym (1), your striking down a man who offered a greeting of salutation is an injustice. He means: iṣābatakum (your striking). Witnesses of this kind are many, and what we have mentioned suffices for one granted the understanding of it. Since (2) [the matter is as] (3) we have described, in that the Arabs frequently bring verbal nouns out on a pattern other than that of their verbs (4), and their putting them in the forms of nouns is attested and widespread, the soundness of what we said in interpreting the speaker's words In the name of God is evident (5): that (6) its meaning, when he begins an action or a saying, is: I begin with the naming of God before my action or before my saying.

Likewise the meaning of the words of one who says, when he begins reciting the Qurʾān, In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful, is: I recite beginning with the naming of God, or: I begin my recitation with the naming of God. So the noun was put in place of the verbal noun of naming, as kalām was put in place of taklīm, and ʿaṭāʾ in place of iʿṭāʾ. The report from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās has been transmitted with the same interpretation we have given.

Abū Kurayb related to us: ʿUthmān b. Saʿīd told us: Bishr b. ʿUmāra told us: Abū Rawq told us, from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās, who said:
The first thing with which (7) Gabriel came down to Muḥammad ﷺ, was that he said: O Muḥammad, say: I seek refuge in the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing from Satan the accursed. Then he said: Say: In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. Ibn ʿAbbās said: In the name of God: Gabriel says to him: O Muḥammad, recite with the remembrance of God your Lord, and stand and sit with the remembrance of God.
al-Ṭabarī’s assessment

This interpretation from Ibn ʿAbbās announces the soundness of what we said, that what is intended (1) by the words of one who begins his recitation with In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful is: I recite by naming God and remembering Him, and I begin the recitation by naming God with His most beautiful names and His exalted attributes. It also (2) makes plain the falsity of the claim of one who asserted that its meaning, from its speaker, is: By God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful, the first (3) of everything. Moreover, servants were commanded only to begin at the outset of their affairs by naming God, not by reporting His greatness and attributes, like what they were commanded of naming over slaughtered animals and game, at eating and drinking, and in all their actions. So likewise what they were commanded of naming Him at the opening of the recitation of God's revelation, and at the heads of their letters and books. There is no disagreement among all the scholars of the community that if someone, when slaughtering one of the grazing animals, said “By God” and did not say “In the name of God,” he has contravened, by leaving out the saying “In the name of God,” what is prescribed for him at slaughter. It is thus known that he did not mean by “in the name of God”: “by God,” as the claimant said, that the ism of God in the words of God In the name of God, Most Gracious, Most Merciful is God; for if it were as he claimed, it would follow that one who says “By God” at slaughtering his sacrifice has said what is prescribed for him to say over the sacrifice. And the consensus of all that one who says this has left what is prescribed for him to say over his sacrifice if he did not say “In the name of God” is clear proof of the falsity of what was claimed of the interpretation of the words “in the name of God,” namely that “by God” is meant by it, and that the name of God is God. This is not a place for lengthy exposition of the name, whether it is the named, or other than it, or an attribute of it, such that we should prolong the book by mentioning it. It is only a place for clarifying the name that is annexed to God, Mighty and Majestic: is it a noun, or a verbal noun meaning naming?

Question

What do you say about the verse of Labīd b. Rabīʿa (1): Until the year, then the name of peace upon you both; and whoever weeps a full year has made his excuse? One advanced in knowledge of the language of the Arabs has interpreted it as meaning: “then peace upon you both,” and that “the name of peace” is peace (2).

al-Ṭabarī responds

If that were permissible and its interpretation as he interpreted it sound, it would be permissible to say: I saw the name of Zayd, I ate the name of food, I drank the name of drink. The consensus of all the Arabs that this is impossible announces the falsity of the interpretation of one who interpreted Labīd's “then the name of peace upon you both” as meaning “then peace upon you both,” and (3) of his claim that the insertion of the noun there and its annexation to peace is permissible only because the name of the named is the named itself. Those who hold the view we have related are asked: Do you allow it in Arabic to say “I ate the name of honey,” meaning “I ate honey,” as you permit “ismu l-salāmi ʿalayka” (1), when you mean “al-salāmu ʿalayka” (1)?

Question

If they say yes, they have left the language of the Arabs, and have allowed in their language what all the Arabs call an error in their language. If they say no, they are asked for the difference between the two, and whatever they say about one they will be bound to say the same about the other. If someone says to us: What then is the meaning of Labīd's verse in your view?

al-Ṭabarī responds

It admits two interpretations, both different from what the person whose view we related said. One of them: that al-Salām is one of the names of God, so it is possible that Labīd meant by his words “then ismu l-salāmi ʿalaykumā”: then hold fast to the name of God and His remembrance after that, and leave my remembrance and weeping over me, by way of ighrāʾ (urging). He put the noun in the nominative because he placed (3) the word that carries the sense of urging after it. The Arabs do this when they put the urging word later and put what is urged first, although they may also put it in the accusative while it comes later. Of this is the verse of the poet (4): O drawer (1) of my bucket, take it (dūnaka)!

I have seen people praising you. He urged with “dūnaka” although it is placed last; the meaning is: dūnaka dalwī (here, take my bucket). So likewise Labīd's words “until the year, then ismu l-salāmi ʿalaykumā” mean: then ʿalaykumā ismu l-salāmi, that is: hold to the remembrance of God, and leave my remembrance and grief for me; for one who has wept a year over a dead man has made his excuse. This is one of its two aspects. The other aspect is: then my naming of God over you both. This is as one says of something he sees that pleases him: “ismu llāhi ʿalayka” (the name of God be upon you), warding off evil from it by that; it is as if he said: then the name of God be upon you both against evil. The first aspect resembles Labīd's meaning more closely of the two. And it is said to the one who directs Labīd's verse to the meaning “then peace upon you both”: Do you consider what we said in these two aspects permissible, or one of them, or something other than what you said about it?

If he says: No, he shows the extent of his knowledge of the varied usages of Arab speech, and spares his opponent from debating him. If he says: Yes, it is said to him: What is your proof that what you claimed of the interpretation is the correct one, rather than what I said it admits, such that we are obliged to concede it to you? There is no way to that.

02

The name Allāh

As for the interpretation of God's words God, it accords with what has been reported to us from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās: He is the One whom everything worships (yaʾlahuhu) (1) and every creature serves.

Transmitted explanation 04

ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās

Transmission wording
This is because Abū Kurayb related to us: ʿUthmān b. Saʿīd told us: Bishr b. ʿUmāra told us: Abū Rawq told us, from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās, who said:
God is the One who holds divinity and the right to servanthood over all His creation (2).
Question

Does this have a base in the pattern “faʿala yafʿalu,” from which this name was formed (3)?

al-Ṭabarī responds

Not by hearing from the Arabs, but by inference.

Question

What shows that ulūhiyya is worship, that ilāh is the one worshipped, and that it has a base in “faʿala yafʿalu”?

al-Ṭabarī responds

There is no dispute among the Arabs in judging as correct, without disagreement, the saying of one who describes a man devoted to worship and seeking what (4) is with God, mighty is His mention: taʾallaha fulān.

He means: at my worship and my seeking God by my deed. There is no doubt that taʾalluh, of the pattern tafaʿʿul, is from alaha yaʾlahu, and that the meaning of alaha, when it is uttered, is: he worshipped God. A verbal noun of it has also come that shows that the Arabs have used from it the pattern “faʿila yafʿalu” without augmentation.

04

A question of language

Question

If it is allowable to say of one who worshipped God: alahahu, on the interpretation of Ibn ʿAbbās and Mujāhid, how should it be said when the reporter wishes [to report] (2) God's rightful claim to that over His servant?

al-Ṭabarī responds

As for transmission, we have no transmission on it (3), but what is required follows the analogy of what came in the report from the Messenger of God ﷺ, which Ismāʿīl b. al-Faḍl related to us:

Attributed report 08

Abū Saʿīd

Transmission wording
Ibrāhīm b. al-ʿAlāʾ told us: Ismāʿīl b. ʿAyyāsh told us, from Ismāʿīl b. Yaḥyā, from Ibn Abī Mulayka, from one who related it to him, from Ibn Masʿūd, and Misʿar b. Kidām, from ʿAṭiyya al-ʿAwfī, from Abū Saʿīd, who said: The Messenger of God ﷺ, said:
“Jesus was handed over by his mother to the kuttāb to be taught. The teacher said to him: Write: Allāh. Jesus said to him: Do you know what Allāh is? Allāh is the God of the gods (ilāh al-ālihah)”
al-Ṭabarī’s assessment

— that it be said: God, majestic is His praise, is the god of the servant, and the servant is the one who worships Him (alahahu). And that the saying of the speaker “Allāh” belongs to the kind of speech (4) whose origin is al-ilāh.

Question

How can that be so, given the difference between their two forms (1)?

al-Ṭabarī responds

Just as it can be that His words But as for me, He is God, my Lord Q 18:38 have as their origin: lākin anā, huwa Allāhu rabbī. As the poet says (2): You shoot me with a glance, as if to say that you are the one wronged, and you hate me, but as for me (lākinnā), I do not hate you (3). He means: lākin anā, I do not hate you. He dropped the hamza of anā, so the nūn of anā met the nūn of lākin, which is unvowelled, and it was assimilated into the nūn of anā, and the two became one doubled nūn. Likewise Allāh: its origin is al-ilāh.

The hamza, which is the fāʾ of the noun, was dropped, so the lām which is the ʿayn of the noun met the additional lām that entered with the additional alif, which is unvowelled, and it was assimilated into the other, which is the ʿayn of the noun, and the two became in pronunciation a single doubled lām, as we described regarding God's words: But as for me, He is God, my Lord.

05

The two names of mercy

As for al-Raḥmān, it is of the pattern faʿlān, from raḥima (4), and al-Raḥīm is of the pattern faʿīl from it. The Arabs often build nouns from “faʿila yafʿalu” on faʿlān, as they say from ghaḍiba: ghaḍbān; from sakira: sakrān; and from ʿaṭisha: ʿaṭshān. Likewise their saying raḥmān from raḥima, because its “faʿila” form (1) is raḥima yarḥamu. And raḥīm was said, even though the ʿayn of its “faʿila” form (2) is vowelled with kasra, because it is praise, and it is the custom of the Arabs to carry noun patterns, when they contain praise or blame, onto faʿīl, whether the ʿayn of the verb form is vowelled with kasra or with fatḥa, as they said from ʿalima: ʿālim and ʿalīm; and from qadara: qādir and qadīr. That is not a building of those on the pattern of their verbs, because the pattern from “faʿila yafʿalu” and “faʿala yafʿilu” is fāʿil; had al-raḥmān and al-raḥīm come out on (3) the pattern of their verbs, their form would have been al-rāḥim.

Question

Since al-raḥmān and al-raḥīm are two names derived from raḥma (mercy), what is the point of repeating them, when one of them conveys the meaning of the other?

al-Ṭabarī responds

The matter is not as (4) you supposed; each of the two words has a meaning that the other does not convey.

Question

What is the meaning in which each is distinct, so that one does not convey the meaning of the other?

al-Ṭabarī responds

As for the Arabic-language aspect, there is no dispute among those who know the languages of the Arabs that the saying al-raḥmān departs further from the noun patterns of “faʿila yafʿalu” than the saying al-raḥīm; and there is no disagreement among them that every noun that has a base in “faʿila yafʿalu” and then departs further from its base of “faʿila yafʿalu,” the one described by it is preferred over the one described by the noun built on its base of “faʿula yafʿulu,” when the naming is by way of praise or blame. So this is what the saying al-raḥmān contains of additional meaning over the saying al-raḥīm, in language. As for the aspect of report and tradition, the interpreters differ (1) on it (2).

07

Weighing the explanations

Question

Which of these two interpretations is more correct in your view?

al-Ṭabarī responds

Each of them has in our view a sound way out, so there is no ground for asking which of the two is more correct. That is because the meaning in naming God al-Raḥmān, as distinct from the meaning in naming Him al-Raḥīm, is that by the name al-Raḥmān He is described as having mercy that extends to all His creation, and by the name [al-Raḥīm He is described] (1) as having mercy specific to some of His creation, whether in all circumstances or in some. There is no doubt, then, that the specificity in describing Him as al-Raḥīm does not change its meaning, whether in this world, in the Hereafter, or in both. Since what we have said is sound, and God, majestic is His praise, has singled out His believing servants in this present world with the gracious favour He showed (2) them in granting them success in obeying Him, in believing in Him and in His messengers, in following His command and avoiding His acts of disobedience, from which He withheld help from the one who associated partners with Him, disbelieved, opposed what he was commanded, and committed acts of disobedience against Him; and since He, majestic is His praise, has also made what He has prepared for those who believe in Him in the future abode in His gardens, the lasting bliss and the manifest triumph, a part of His mercy in this world and the Hereafter, alongside what He has extended to them and to the unbelievers in this world, of bounty and kindness to all of them, in the expansion of provision, the subjection of the clouds to bring rain, the bringing forth of plants from the earth, the soundness of bodies and minds, and the other countless blessings in which believers and unbelievers share, our Lord, majestic is His praise, is the Most Gracious of all His creation in this world and the Hereafter, and the Most Merciful of the believers in particular, in this world and the Hereafter. As for the mercy with which He encompassed all of them in this world, by which He is raḥmān (1) to them, it is what we have mentioned together with its counterparts, which none of His creatures can count, as He, majestic is His praise, said: If you count God's blessing, you will not be able to enumerate it Q 14:34; 16:18.

As for the Hereafter, the mercy with which He encompassed all of them there, by which He is Most Gracious to them, lies (2) in His treating all of them equally, majestic is His mention, in His justice and judgement, so that He wrongs none of them by the weight of an atom, and if there is a good deed He multiplies it (3), and gives each soul in full what it has earned. That is the meaning of His general mercy over all of them in the Hereafter, by which He is Most Gracious in the Hereafter. As for what He specified for the believers in this present world of His mercy, by which He is Most Merciful to them in it, as He, majestic is His mention, said: And He is ever Merciful to the believers Q 33:43, it is the gracious favour in their religion that we described, which He granted to them and withheld from those of the unbelievers whom He abandoned. As for what He specified for them in the Hereafter, by which He is Most Merciful to them and not to the unbelievers, it is what we described above (5) of the bliss and honour, beyond what hopes can reach, that He prepared for them and not for others.

Further transmitted views

Transmitted explanation 11

ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās

Transmission wording
The other view on its interpretation is what Abū Kurayb related to us: ʿUthmān b. Saʿīd told us: Bishr b. ʿUmāra told us: Abū Rawq told us, from al-Ḍaḥḥāk, from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās, who said:
al-Raḥmān is the faʿlān form from raḥma (mercy), and it belongs to the speech of the Arabs. He said: Most Gracious, Most Merciful: the tender, the gentle toward whom He wishes to show mercy, and the remote, the severe toward whom He wishes to be harsh; and likewise all His names (1).
al-Ṭabarī’s assessment

This interpretation from Ibn ʿAbbās shows that that by which our Lord is raḥmān is that by which He is raḥīm, even though the word al-Raḥmān has a meaning that the word al-Raḥīm lacks. This is because he made the meaning of al-Raḥmān that of the one who is tender (2) toward the one to whom He is tender, and the meaning of al-Raḥīm that of the one who is gentle toward the one to whom He is gentle. The view that we related on the interpretation of this from the Prophet ﷺ, and mentioned from al-ʿArzamī (3), is closer to its interpretation than this view that we related from Ibn ʿAbbās, although this view agrees with it in meaning, in that al-Raḥmān has a meaning that al-Raḥīm lacks, and that al-Raḥīm has an interpretation other than that of al-Raḥmān. The third view on the interpretation of this is what ʿImrān b. Bakkār al-Kalāʿī related to me:

Transmitted explanation 12

ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī

Transmission wording
Yaḥyā b. Ṣāliḥ told us: Abū al-Azhar Naṣr b. ʿAmr al-Lakhmī, of the people of Palestine, told us: I heard ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī say:
It was “al-Raḥmān”; when al-Raḥmān was cut away (ukhtuzila) from His name, it became “al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm” (4).
al-Ṭabarī’s assessment

What ʿAṭāʾ meant by this, God willing, is that al-Raḥmān was one of the names of God by which none of His creatures was named; when the liar Musaylima named himself by it, and that is his “cutting away” of it, meaning his taking it from God's names for himself, God, majestic is His praise, announced that His name is al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm; so that by this He would distinguish for His servants His name from the name of those who had taken His names for themselves, since no one other than Him, majestic is His mention, is named al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm, such that these two names are joined for him. Some of His creatures are named (1) either Raḥīm or Raḥmān, but Raḥmān Raḥīm has never been joined for anyone but Him, and the two are not joined for anyone besides Him. So the meaning of this saying of ʿAṭāʾ is that God, majestic is His praise, distinguished His name from the name of any other of His creatures by following al-Raḥmān with al-Raḥīm, whether the two differ in meaning or agree. What ʿAṭāʾ said is sound in meaning; it is rather possible that He, majestic is His praise, reserved for Himself being named with both together, to set it apart from His creatures, in order to make His servants know, by the mention of the two together, that He alone is intended by their mention, to the exclusion of anyone else among His creatures, along with the meaning in the interpretation of each of them that the other lacks.

09

The order of the names

Some of the dull-witted have claimed that the Arabs did not know al-Raḥmān and that it was not in (2) their language, and that for this reason the polytheists said to the Prophet ﷺ: And what is the Raḥmān? Shall we prostrate to what you command us? Q 25:60, in denial of this name. In his view it was as if (3) it were impossible that polytheists deny what they knew to be true, or (4) as if he had not recited from the Book of God the words of God: Those to whom We gave the Book know him (meaning Muḥammad ﷺ) as they know their own sons Q 2:146. Yet they were at the same time rejecting him and denying his prophethood. So it is known from this that they used to dispute the reality of what had been established as sound with them and what they knew with certainty. A verse has also been recited from one of the ignorant of the Jāhiliyya (5): Behold, that [girl has struck her hajīn] (1) / Behold, may al-Raḥmān, my Lord, cut off her right hand.

And Salāma b. Jandal al-Saʿdī said (2): You rushed upon us, our two rushes (3) upon you; and whatever al-Raḥmān wills, He binds and looses. Some who have weak knowledge of the interpretation of the exegetes, and little transmission of the sayings of the early authorities among the commentators (4), have also claimed that the figurative sense (majāz) of al-Raḥmān is “possessor of mercy,” and of al-Raḥīm is “the one who shows mercy.” Then he said: They sometimes derive the two words from one word with the meaning being one, owing to the breadth of speech among them. He said: They did the same in saying nadmān and nadīm. Then he cited as witness [the verse of Burj] (5) b. Mushir al-Ṭāʾī: Many a drinking companion (nadmān) who makes the cup sweeter, I served him when the stars had set (6). And he cited verses similar to it concerning nadīm and nadmān. He thus distinguished between the meaning of al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm in the interpretation, in saying: al-Raḥmān is the possessor of mercy, and al-Raḥīm is the one who shows mercy; although he left aside explaining the interpretation of their two meanings (7) on its soundness, and then illustrated (8) this with two words [that come with one meaning], so he returned to what he had made two meanings and made it an example of what has a single meaning, despite the difference of the words.

There is no doubt that the possessor of mercy is the one for whom mercy has been established and for whom it is sound that it is an attribute, and that the one who shows mercy is described as one who will show mercy, or has shown mercy and that act is complete, or is in the midst of it; and in that there is then no indication (2) that mercy is an attribute of his, as there is an indication that it is his attribute when he is described as possessor of mercy. So where is the meaning of al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm, on his interpretation, in relation to the meaning of two words derived from a single word, with differing wording and the same meanings? But a statement that has no firm foundation to rely on has its flaw plain to see.

Question

Why did He put His name Allāh before His name al-Raḥmān, and His name al-Raḥmān before His name al-Raḥīm?

al-Ṭabarī responds

Because it is the habit of the Arabs, when they wish to report about one who is reported on, to put his name first and then to follow it with his attributes and epithets; and this is what is required by rule, that the name be placed before its epithet and attribute, so that the hearer knows about whom the report is. Since that is so, and God, majestic is His mention, has names that He forbade His creatures to be named by, reserving them to Himself apart from them, such as Allāh, al-Raḥmān and al-Khāliq, and names that He permitted them to use of one another, such as al-Raḥīm, al-Samīʿ, al-Baṣīr, al-Karīm and similar names, it was required that His names that belong to Him alone, apart from all His creation, come first, so that the hearer knows to whom the praise and glorification are directed; and that these be followed by His names by which others also are named, after the one addressed or the hearer knows to whom what follows of the meanings is directed. So God, majestic is His mention, began with His name Allāh, because divinity belongs to none other than Him, majestic is His praise, in any respect, neither by way of being named by it nor by way of meaning. For we have shown that the meaning of “Allāh,” majestic is His praise, is the meaning (1) of the one worshipped, and there is no one worshipped but Him, majestic is His praise; and that God has forbidden being named by it, even if the one who takes the name intends what (2) the one named Saʿīd (happy) intends while he is wretched, or the one named Ḥasan (handsome) while he is ugly.

Do you not see that God, majestic is His praise, said in more than one verse of His Book: Is there a god with God? Q 27:60–64? He thus regarded it as monstrous in one who professes it. And He, Exalted, said, in singling Himself out (3) with Allāh and al-Raḥmān: Say: Call upon God or call upon the Raḥmān; whichever you call upon, to Him belong the most beautiful names Q 17:110. Then He followed that (4) with His name al-Raḥmān, since He had also forbidden His creation to be named by it, although some of His creatures may deserve to be named by some of its meanings. That is because it is permissible to describe many of those below God among His creatures with some attributes of mercy, whereas it is impermissible for anyone beside Him to deserve any part of divinity; so for that reason al-Raḥmān came second (5) to His name Allāh. As for His name al-Raḥīm, we have mentioned that it is among the names by which it is permissible to describe others, and mercy is among His attributes, majestic is His mention; so, the matter being as we described, it fell into the positions of epithets of names, which follow (6) them after the names have come first.

This is the reason for putting His name Allāh before His name al-Raḥmān, and His name al-Raḥmān before His name al-Raḥīm. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī also said of al-Raḥmān what we have said, that it is among the names of God by which servants were forbidden to be named.

Transmitted explanation 13

al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī

Transmission wording
Muḥammad b. Bashshār related to us: Ḥammād b. Masʿada told us, from ʿAwf (1), from al-Ḥasan, who said:
al-Raḥmān is a prohibited name (2).
al-Ṭabarī’s assessment

Moreover, the consensus of the community in prohibiting all people from being named by it makes it unnecessary to cite the saying of al-Ḥasan and others as proof of what we have said in this matter.

Abū Jaʿfar Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (d. 310 AH), Jāmiʿ al-bayān ʿan taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān. The English of this surah is from the supplied English translation, Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī = Jāmiʿ al-bayān ʿan taʾwīl āy al-Qurʾān (version 1422 AH / 2001 CE, first edition).

The supplied source identifies the Dār Hajar edition and its editors but does not name the English translator.

Translation passages are presented by verse; paragraph, report and quotation groupings are editorial. The English wording is preserved.

The editor's notes are those of ʿAbd Allāh al-Turkī’s edition (Dār Hajr, 2001), cited by volume, page and note number. Verse numbering follows the Kufan count; the Fātiḥa verse wording follows the supplied English translation of al-Ṭabarī.

How this page is set out: the commentary is read into blocks (the commentator's own explanation, transmitted reports with their chains, quoted verses, objections and answers, cited poetry) and grouped into sections. These divisions, their titles and the highlighted verse wording are editorial; the words are unchanged, except that a backtick standing for the letter ʿayn is set as ʿ. Qur'anic wording the translator marked with braces is highlighted; turn highlights off to see the braces.
