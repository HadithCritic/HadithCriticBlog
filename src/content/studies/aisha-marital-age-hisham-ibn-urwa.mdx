If you have read the [primer on isnad bundles](/studies/how-to-read-an-isnad-bundle/), you know the shape to look for: a fan of corroborated strands converging on one transmitter, and a single uncheckable strand beneath him. Joshua Little's Oxford doctoral thesis applies that lens, at book length and with unusual rigor, to what is arguably the most discussed hadith outside Muslim scholarship: the report of Aisha's age at her marriage to the Prophet.

## The hadith

The version most readers know is Bukhārī's:

> Muḥammad b. Yūsuf related to us: "Sufyān related to us, from Hishām, from his father, from ʿĀʾishah, that the Prophet married her when she was a girl of six years, and she was taken to him when she was a girl of nine, and she lived with him nine [years]."

Versions of this report appear across the canonical collections and far beyond them: in Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, the Musnad of Ibn Ḥanbal, and dozens of other works, with the ages six (or seven) and nine remarkably stable across them. You can read the canonical versions in Silsilah: [bukhari:5134](/silsilah/find/?q=bukhari:5134), [muslim:1422](/silsilah/find/?q=muslim:1422), [abudawud:2121](/silsilah/find/?q=abudawud:2121), [ibnmajah:1877](/silsilah/find/?q=ibnmajah:1877).

By medieval standards the matter is closed: the chains are ṣaḥīḥ, the report sits in both Ṣaḥīḥs, and that is that. Little's question is the structural one: when *every* available version is collected, more than the canonical handful, what does the whole transmission picture say?

## The method

Little's tool is isnad-cum-matn analysis (ICMA), the method associated with Harald Motzki and refined by a generation of scholars since. The procedure, in brief:

1. Collect every version of the hadith in every surviving source, with its full chain.
2. Draw the combined bundle and identify the apparent common links, the points where strands genuinely converge.
3. Independently, compare the *texts* of the versions. If the versions transmitted from a given figure share distinctive wording that other lines lack, the convergence is corroborated from two directions at once: the chains say the versions passed through him, and the wording agrees.
4. Reconstruct the redaction each common link actually circulated, then date and place it using his biography.

The first chapter of the thesis is itself a methodological survey, a defense of a rigorous form of ICMA against both traditionalist confidence and the more corrosive forms of revisionist skepticism. That chapter alone functions as a literature review of the entire dating debate, from Schacht through Juynboll, Motzki, and their critics.

## The bundle

Little collated the versions and the picture that emerged is the textbook shape. Strand after strand, across regions and collections, runs back to one man: Hishām b. ʿUrwa b. al-Zubayr, a Medinan transmitter, great-grandson of the first caliph Abū Bakr through his grandmother Asmāʾ, and grand-nephew of Aisha herself. Below Hishām the chain is a family single strand: Hishām, from his father ʿUrwa, from Aisha.

The matn evidence backs the picture: the versions descending from Hishām share their distinctive skeleton (married at six, consummated at nine, the doll-play motifs in some lines), in exactly the patterned way ICMA predicts for a genuine common-link redaction. Little's conclusion at this stage: all versions of the marital-age hadith likely derive from a single archetype, an ur-hadith, and that archetype is Hishām's.

## The geography

Here is the thesis's sharpest finding. Hishām was a Medinan, and he spent most of his life in Medina, where ʿUrwa's circle and its students were. But the marital-age hadith does not surface in the Medinan corpus of his earlier career. The transmitters who carry it from him are overwhelmingly Iraqi: Kufans like Sufyān al-Thawrī, Abū Muʿāwiya, and ʿAlī b. Mushir, Basrans like Ḥammād b. Salama. Hishām moved to Iraq toward the end of his life (he died there in 146-147/763-765), and that is where, and when, the report enters circulation.

Why would Hishām circulate such a report in Iraq? Little's proposal is contextual: mid-8th-century Iraq was the home of sharpening proto-Shīʿī polemic against Aisha. A report establishing her extreme youth at marriage carried a clear apologetic point in that environment: she was the Prophet's only virgin bride, married before anyone else could have touched her, a status her partisans wielded in her defense. On Little's reading, the hadith was created and disseminated by Hishām as a reaction to local polemics against his great-aunt, with the family chain through his father supplying the authority.

## Naturalization and dives

A report this useful did not stay in one bundle. Little traces how, as the hadith spread across the early Abbasid caliphate, some scholars reworked its content and others replaced its isnad altogether, substituting local or familial chains that bypassed Hishām, exactly the diving behavior the primer describes. Regional centers thereby naturalized the report as their own, and the apparent corroboration multiplied. Drawn as a diagram, these strands attach below the common link, and on Juynboll's adage their depth betrays their lateness.

## Reception

The final movement of the thesis follows the proto-Sunnī hadith critics. They did engage with the marital-age versions: some lines were rejected, others criticized. But Little finds their acceptance of the core report proceeded without a thorough, systematic investigation of its provenance and transmission, in keeping with their one-strand-at-a-time toolkit. The hadith was canonized, in both Ṣaḥīḥs, on the strength of chains whose structural shape, one man, one family line, one late Iraqi context, their methods were not designed to see.

## What the thesis concludes, and what it does not

Little's positive claims are precise. The marital-age hadith, as a *report*, originates with Hishām b. ʿUrwa in mid-8th-century Iraq, roughly 120 years after the marriage it describes. Below Hishām, the family strand to ʿUrwa and Aisha is unverifiable, and the geographic and polemical evidence tells against it. That is a finding about the transmission history of a text, reached by methods any reader can audit on the diagrams above.

What the thesis does not do is replace the report with an alternative biography: it dates the memory, not the marriage. Its force lies elsewhere, in the demonstration that the most thoroughly authenticated report in the medieval sense can still be, on structural analysis, one man's late composition. The implications for the corpus at large, where single strands and family isnads are the norm rather than the exception, are the standing question of this whole field, and of this site.
