Every study in this section relies on one method, and the method has two halves that the name states plainly: *isnād-cum-matn*, the chain together with the text. Read the chains and you learn how a tradition was distributed. Read the texts and you learn whether that distribution is real. Neither half settles anything alone. This primer builds the isnad picture first, because it is the easier half to draw, then shows why the picture is not enough, then adds the text analysis that turns a suggestive diagram into an argument about who said what, and when.

The structural vocabulary is Gautier Juynboll's, from the general introduction to his *Encyclopedia of Canonical Hadith*. The synthesis of chain and text into a single dating procedure is the work of Harald Motzki, Gregor Schoeler, and Andreas Görke in the 1990s, sharpened more recently by Joshua Little. Where these scholars disagree with one another, and they do, the disagreements are mostly about how much the method can prove, not about how it is performed. We flag them as they arise.

## A hadith has two parts

A hadith is a *matn*, the text of what was said or done, carried by an *isnād*, the chain of people who claim to have passed it down. After the isnad became the universal device for authentication, presence in the canonical collections, above all the two Ṣaḥīḥs of Bukhārī and Muslim, was generally treated as a guarantee. The attribution to the Prophet was accepted at face value.

The modern method refuses that face value, and it refuses it for a specific reason. Medieval criticism judged chains one at a time, narrator by narrator, with the biographical dictionaries: was each man reliable, did each pair of lifetimes overlap. It almost never asked the structural question. When *all* the chains supporting a tradition are drawn as one picture, what does the picture say about where the tradition came from? Juynboll's encyclopedia is built on a medieval work that makes the picture drawable, Mizzī's *Tuḥfat al-ashrāf*, which inventories every isnad of every canonical tradition across 19,626 numbered items.

## The single strand

Start with the simplest case: one collector, reached by one chain of single individuals, running back to the Prophet.

For a single strand to be history, every link must have happened exactly as claimed, between exactly those two people, with no one else hearing the report along the way. Juynboll's verdict is that postulating historicity for a transmission "achieved at the hands of one single individual to another single individual" requires an act of faith no dispassionate historian can supply. A single strand may be true. The point is that nothing about it can be checked. The whole method exists to find structures that can.

## The spider

Now a trap, and the trap is that it is built to look like the real thing. Sometimes many strands appear to support one tradition and the fan looks as convincing as a bundle's, until you notice *where* the strands actually touch. Juynboll named the shape after the harvest spider, the daddy-longlegs: a small body, and long thin legs splaying out from it.

Set this beside the bundle and the dive diagrams and the silhouette is almost the same: a stub down to the Prophet, a fan of legs up to the collectors. That resemblance is the whole danger. The difference is in the legs. Each leg of a spider is a single strand, and the transmitters peopling them are so often obscure that the biographical lexicons can manage only *majhūl*, unknown. Where a bundle's legs join at partial common links who really taught several students, a spider's legs join nowhere except at the body, which is to say nowhere real. Juynboll reads the shape as back-projection: later collectors, each wanting their own route to a text already in circulation, devised chains downward into the past, and those chains happen to cross at a famous name. The crossing point transmitted nothing. It is an artifact of the drawing. In Mizzī's material, Juynboll notes, spiders comfortably outnumber genuine bundles. Hold on to the spider. It is the precise thing the text analysis later catches.

## The bundle and the common link

Here is the structure worth having. The example is Juynboll's own showcase, "deeds are appraised by their intentions", the first hadith in Bukhārī's Ṣaḥīḥ.

<IsnadBundle href="/silsilah/bukhari/1/?v=1#h1" hrefLabel="Open bukhari:1 in Silsilah" />

A bundle has a signature: a single strand from the Prophet up to one man, then a genuine fan after him. That man is the **common link** (CL). The transmitters above him who themselves have several pupils are **partial common links** (PCLs), and they are what separates a bundle from a spider. The corroboration is observable in independent collections rather than merely claimed. Juynboll grades the result by counting: three or more credible PCLs make a CL; two make a *seeming* CL, written (S)CL; one with only single strands otherwise makes a seeming CL, written SCL.

The reading rule follows. Transmission becomes historically tenable at the point where independent strands genuinely converge, the knot. So in the bundle above, the fan tells us Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Anṣārī (d. 144/761) really did spread this tradition, and the lone strand beneath him, through al-Taymī, ʿAlqama, and ʿUmar to the Prophet, is precisely the part the structure cannot vouch for. The common link is the earliest *demonstrable* origin of the wording. Whether he is more than that, whether he composed the wording or merely passed it on, is the question the isnad alone cannot answer, and we return to it once the texts are in hand.

## Dives

Bundles rarely stay clean, because collectors competed. A transmitter who received a tradition by the ordinary route might still want a more impressive one: older, shorter, or simply his own. The solution was a dive, a new strand launched past the common link to touch the structure lower down.

Juynboll's chronological rule: shallow dives are older than deep ones. A dive onto the common link's informant was an early move; a dive onto the companion, or onto a freshly supplied alternative companion, came later. He notes with some relish that medieval hadith science had technical terms for exactly these strands, *mutābiʿāt* for the shallow kind and *shawāhid* for the deep kind, and counted them as corroboration. In his reading they are the opposite of corroboration. They are the visible trail of competitive authentication, Bukhārī and Muslim themselves among the competitors.

## Why the picture is not enough

If you stop here, you have Juynboll's method, and it has a serious weakness that he never fully answered. Michael Cook put it most cleanly: a pattern that *looks* like a bundle can be manufactured without any genuine common link at all. Suppose one man invents a wording and ascribes it to an old authority. A second man takes the wording, drops the inventor from his chain, and cites the old authority directly. A third does the same. Now three transmitters cite one matn from one source, and on paper they fan out from him exactly like real PCLs. Cook called this the spread of isnāds. The same mechanism, applied to a single feature rather than a whole tradition, is contamination: a phrase migrates from one tradition into a neighbouring one and leaves a chain that says it was always there.

This is the spider problem again, generalised. A pile of strands converging on a name proves only that the name was worth borrowing. Dense citation, Juynboll's main authenticity criterion, cannot by itself distinguish a man who taught a hundred students from a man whose prestige attracted a hundred forgeries. To tell those apart you need a second witness, one the forgers did not think to coordinate. That witness is the text.

## Reading the matns together

So you collate. Lay every surviving version of the tradition side by side and read the *texts*, not the chains: the wording, the order of clauses, what each adds and omits, the distinctive turns of phrase. Then group the versions into textual families by what they share. Then ask the one question the whole method turns on.

**Do the textual families line up with the branches of the isnad?**

The logic, in Little's hypothetical: a common link circulates a report whose core we can call A. One PCL paraphrases it as AB, and his own students, paraphrasing in turn, produce AB1 and AB2. A second PCL renders the same core as AC, and his students AC4 and AC5. A third gives AD, and so on. The result is that the versions cluster: each PCL's transmissions look more like each other than like any other PCL's, and underneath all of them sits the shared A. Now run the explanation backwards. If you find exactly that pattern in the real collections, the shared core is most economically explained as the common link's wording, and each branch's distinctive material as the addition of its PCL.

Why does correlation carry weight that dense citation did not? Because, as Motzki argued and Herbert Berg restated, forgery and correlation pull in opposite directions. Mass fabrication, borrowing, contamination, spreading isnads: these can produce almost any arrangement of texts and chains, and so they tend toward noise. A genuine transmission process, by contrast, produces this one pattern over and over, because each transmitter leaves the same kind of fingerprint on everything he passes on. When the chain structure and the text structure agree, and agree in detail, coincidence and conspiracy both become expensive explanations.

This rests on a premise worth making explicit: that early transmission was *riwāya bi-l-maʿnā*, transmission by sense rather than by rote. If men had repeated traditions word for word, there would be no idiosyncratic wordings to cluster, and the test would have nothing to read. The variation is not noise in the method. It is the signal.

## What the synthesis lets you say

Once chain and text agree, the analysis does real work in four directions.

It **confirms a transmitter**. A PCL or CL is genuine when a distinctive sub-tradition clusters around him, and the elements shared across that cluster can be assigned to him as his redaction. This is what lets Motzki and his successors drop two of Juynboll's habits: the blanket suspicion of single strands, since a single strand can be vindicated when it carries a distinctive matn that corroborates a PCL, and the rule of three direct citations, since two independent lines carrying demonstrably independent texts can establish a common link on their own.

It **reconstructs an ur-text**. Schoeler likened the procedure to editing a manuscript: you infer the relationships among versions from their shared and divergent readings, and where all branches descend from one wording, that wording is the archetype. The difference is that the variants here are mostly oral, products of paraphrase, mishearing, and elaboration rather than copyists' slips, so the work is better called tradition criticism than textual criticism. The shared core across the PCL branches is the common link's protoversion; branch-specific material is later.

It **exposes fraud**, and this is the part that makes the method falsifiable rather than merely suggestive. If the texts under a supposed PCL resemble *other* PCLs' texts more than each other, the node is a spider, the target of several independent false ascriptions, exactly the case the isnad picture could not catch. If a version's text is nearly identical to a tight PCL family but its chain claims a wholly separate route, the independent chain is almost certainly a dive: a real text fitted with a fresh isnad. The correlation that confirms genuine transmission, by its absence, convicts the fabricated kind.

It **catches contamination and editing**. A wording that belongs to one branch but surfaces once, out of place, in another is best read as a leak between traditions. A feature carried by only one PCL, absent from all the others who heard the same source, is best read as that PCL's own addition, not as something the others all happened to drop. Interpolation is generally the more economical story than coordinated omission, and traditions tend to accrete rather than shed.

## What it dates, and what it cannot reach

The payoff is a date, but a particular and limited kind of date. The common link's lifetime is a *terminus a quo*: the tradition, in the form we can reconstruct, was in circulation by then, and the common link is at minimum its formulator. Juynboll's formula is that the saying the common link ascribes to the Prophet "is in reality his own, or if somebody else's, he was the first to put it into so many words", and that he is responsible for the strand back to the Prophet as well. On this much Motzki agreed.

The honest limit lies below the common link, and Little has pressed it hardest. His defence of the method depends on transmission growing *more* precise over time, sloppy in the mid-to-late eighth century when common links handed their material to their PCLs, then tightening around 800 as writing spread and the vague *ʿan* gave way to verbatim *ḥaddathanā*. That gradient is what lets later variants survive intact to be read. But it cuts the other way too. If transmission was loose at the common link's level and looser still before him, then whatever the common link received from earlier figures was already heavily reworked, and usually beyond recovery. Little calls his own result a pyrrhic victory: the method reaches the common link with some confidence and the pre-common-link layer with very little. What a common link *is* (Schacht's outright creator, Motzki's first systematic collector, the formulator that Juynboll and Little both settle on) remains the field's live disagreement, but it is a disagreement about a figure in the eighth century, not the seventh.

## A worked example: the hadith of ʿĀʾisha's marital age

Little's doctoral study runs the whole procedure on one famous tradition, the report that the Prophet married ʿĀʾisha bint Abī Bakr when she was very young. He collated every available version, diagrammed the bundles, clustered the matns, and tested one against the other.

The chains across the corpus converge on a single common link: Hishām b. ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr (d. 146-147/763-765), ʿĀʾisha's great-nephew, transmitting from his father ʿUrwah from ʿĀʾisha. The texts cooperate with that reading. The versions group into branches that match Hishām's students, each branch carrying its own phrasing over a shared core, which is the correlation the method looks for. Crucially, the strands that present an *independent* route to the report do not carry independent texts; their wording tracks Hishām's families too closely to be independent, which marks them, on the test above, as dives and local re-ascriptions rather than separate testimony.

From there the analysis turns to the who, when, where, and why that the biographies supply once the common link is fixed. Hishām's redaction appears to surface only after he left Medina for Iraq late in life, which places the tradition's circulation in Kufa somewhere between roughly 754 and 765. Little reads its content against that setting and argues it answers a local need: proto-Shīʿī hostility toward ʿĀʾisha was active in the Kufan milieu, and a report that fixes her early and singular place in the Prophet's household reads as a defence of her by her own kinsman. After Hishām, the report spread and diversified across the early Abbasid world, with some collectors reworking its wording or swapping the original isnad for local and familial chains that naturalised it in their own regions. The proto-Sunnī critics who later sorted the corpus accepted some versions and rejected others, but, on Little's account, without the systematic provenance analysis that would have traced them all to one eighth-century origin.

Two cautions belong with this example. First, every step is an argument, not a datum: the common-link identification is the strong part, resting on the chain-and-text correlation; the motive read from Kufan sectarian politics is the more interpretive part, and Little presents it as such. Second, the conclusion is recent and not uncontested. What the case shows for our purposes is the shape of a finished isnād-cum-matn analysis: a reconstructed wording, a named formulator, a date and place, and a candidate reason, each tied to evidence that another scholar can redraw and dispute.

## How scholars read the method

The procedure above did not arrive whole. It is the current settlement in a long argument over whether common links can date anything at all, and the argument is worth knowing, because each step of the method is a concession won or lost in it. Little's thesis reconstructs that argument in detail. What follows is its shape.

Joseph Schacht invented common-link analysis in 1950. For him the common link was the originator: the man who put a doctrine into circulation and fabricated the chain back to the Prophet to authorise it. The strands fanning out below him were the tradition's real history, and the single strand reaching up to the Prophet was his invention. The common link therefore dated the tradition, and Schacht trusted the dating for a specific reason. His common links clustered in one generation, the generation of al-Zuhrī around the middle of the eighth century, which was exactly when, on other evidence, hadiths were being produced in bulk. The figure fit the period. (Schacht called him "N.N." and drew the Prophet at the top of the page; neither convention survived him.)

Michael Cook, with Patricia Crone and Norman Calder, took the method apart across the late 1970s and 1980s, and did it with Schacht's own observation. Schacht had noticed that isnads spread: a doctrine already in circulation picks up fresh chains as rival groups each capture it and supply their own authorities. But if isnads spread, Cook argued, a common link can be manufactured. Convergence on a famous name need not mean the name transmitted anything. It can mean only that the name was worth borrowing, with later transmitters diving onto him and quietly dropping their real sources. Crone's verdict was that the spread of isnads invalidates the method outright, since it works in some cases and fails in others and so cannot date on its own. Cook's was wrier: the common links must mean something, but he did not claim to know what.

Gautier Juynboll spent a career answering this without giving up the common link. His answer is the apparatus this primer opened with. He named the single strand, the spider, and the dive; he split Schacht's crude common link into graded kinds; and he held that dense citation defeats the spread of isnads. A spider, where single strands merely cross at a name, is precisely the manufactured pattern Cook described, and Juynboll granted that spiders outnumber real bundles. But a genuine bundle, with three or more partial common links each citing the figure independently, was on his view too well attested to fake. The common link is then the formulator of the wording and the maker of the strand to the Prophet. One point here is easy to miss. Juynboll did not conclude that the Prophet said nothing. He allowed that the texts might descend from Muhammad through informal channels and held only that the chains cannot prove it. That distinction settles one of the later quarrels.

Harald Motzki, with Gregor Schoeler and Andreas Görke, found Juynboll's answer at once too strict and too weak. Too weak, because the three-citation test is open to the very problem it was meant to solve: a run of shallow dives can yield three "independent" citations as easily as real teaching can, so counting strands does not by itself beat the spread of isnads. Too strict, because the blanket distrust of single strands rested on intuition rather than argument, and a single strand can be perfectly genuine with its corroborating lines simply lost. Their move was to stop leaning on the chains alone and bring in the texts, which is the isnād-cum-matn analysis. Where the texts cluster by branch, the correlation does the work that citation-counting could not, and a single strand carrying a distinctive text can be vindicated rather than dismissed. Motzki read the common link more cautiously than Schacht: the first systematic collector and formulator, not the necessary creator of the idea.

The synthesis drew its own critics. Christopher Melchert has pressed two objections. The first is a paradox: the method needs transmission loose enough for distinctive wordings to arise, yet tight enough for them to survive and still correlate, and it is not obvious how it can be both. The second is harder. A coordinated set of dives and borrowings could in principle produce the very correlation the method treats as proof, so the step from correlation to authenticity is an assumption rather than a result. Herbert Berg, following Motzki, gives the standing reply to the second. Fabrication and borrowing can run in any direction and so tend toward noise, whereas genuine transmission keeps producing this one pattern, which makes it the better explanation even if not the only conceivable one.

From the conservative side, Jonathan Brown argues that Juynboll's skepticism fails Occam's razor. To accept it, Brown writes, is to believe in a forgery so vast and so well hidden that thousands of mutually hostile scholars, across seven centuries and three continents, fabricated the corpus and never exposed one another, which is harder to credit than that the Prophet now and then said what is ascribed to him. Little's reply is that the objection trades on a confusion. The provenance of a text and the provenance of its chain are separate questions: one can hold that the chains are formally fabricated and still hold that many of the texts descend, informally, from the Prophet. Since that was Juynboll's own position, the choice Brown forces is a false one.

Joshua Little sits at the skeptical end of the synthesis. He accepts the correlation argument and then tightens what it licenses. The method, on his account, is falsifiable: where the texts under a supposed common link refuse to cluster, the figure is exposed as a spider rather than confirmed as a source. And it reaches the common link with some confidence but the period before him with very little, because the same loose-then-tightening transmission that lets later variants survive also guarantees that whatever the common link received was already reworked past recovery. The gain is real and bounded, which is why he calls his strongest results a pyrrhic victory.

What unites these scholars is larger than what divides them. All of them, Schacht's critics included, accept that the transmission picture is where the argument must be fought. They part over how much the picture carries: whether a common link dates a tradition, whether correlation proves transmission, whether the figure at the knot is a creator, a collector, or a fiction. None of this is closed. The reason to lay the diagrams and the texts side by side is that these are disagreements over shared evidence, which can be redrawn and rechecked, not competing acts of faith.

## What to keep

Keep the pictures and keep the collation. Whatever one concludes from them, the structures and the textual families are real and reproducible: anyone with Mizzī, or with the [Silsilah database](/silsilah/), can rebuild a bundle, and anyone with the collections can lay the matns side by side. The studies in this section will mark, case by case, where an author lands on the questions above. The premise they share is this primer's, that a claim about a hadith's origin has to be argued from the whole transmission picture, chain and text together, never from one polished chain.
