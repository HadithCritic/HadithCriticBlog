# The Abbasid Mahdī: Black Banners, Khurāsān, and the Capture of Hāshimite Hope

## Opening Thesis

The three preceding chapters traced Mahdī clusters whose central work was theological transformation of political defeat. The Kaysānī cluster converted the failure of Mukhtār's revolt and the death of Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya into deferred occultation. The Zubayrid cluster converted the killing of Ibn al-Zubayr at the violated House into the eschatological reversal of the swallowed army at al-Bayḍāʾ. The ʿUmar II cluster, structurally different from both, did not transform a defeat but preserved a moment of semantic instability in which the title *mahdī* was still being negotiated around a successful pious-restorer caliph. The cluster treated in this chapter is different again. It is the first layer in which Mahdī expectation becomes the explicit instrument of an organized revolutionary daʿwa that actually succeeded — and then, having succeeded, became dynastic propaganda for the regime it produced.

The Abbasid revolution of 130–132/747–750 is the decisive break-point. Before it, Mahdī material had collected around figures who had been defeated. After it, Mahdī material collected around a regime that ruled. The Abbasid daʿwa machinery — the secret missionary network in Khurāsān, the call to an unnamed *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*, the seventy initial responders, the twelve naqībs, the black banners and black clothing of the Musawwida — is historically documented in the Sunnī and Shīʿī chronicle tradition (Balādhurī, Ṭabarī, Yaʿqūbī, Masʿūdī, Dīnawarī, Jahshiyārī, Ibn al-Jawzī) with a level of organizational detail unavailable for any previous Mahdī movement. The matching hadith and fitan material — the eastern black-banner reports through Ibn Masʿūd, the Thawbān Mahdī reports through Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ and ʿAbd al-Razzāq, the Abū Hurayra "banners planted at Īliyāʾ" reports through Rushdīn b. Saʿd and al-Zuhrī, the explicit Banū al-ʿAbbās reports preserved in Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād — uses the same vocabulary, geography, color-symbolism, and mobilization command as the daʿwa it sacralizes. The match is too specific and too multidimensional to be incidental.

<ClaimBox title="Analytical Framework">
<p>Following Motzki, Schacht, and Juynboll, the methodological question is not whether the Prophet predicted black banners from Khurāsān. The methodological question is what these reports were doing at the moment of their earliest historical visibility — overwhelmingly the late Umayyad and early Abbasid periods — and which transmitter networks carried them. The answer, traced across the eight major matn families and the historical control material, is that the Abbasid black-banner cluster is best read as the prophetic-form afterlife of the Abbasid daʿwa. Some of the underlying motifs may have circulated in broader Hāshimite, Alid, or eastern-army apocalyptic environments earlier than the daʿwa itself. But the cluster as we have it — the cluster of reports that command joining the banners, that name *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* as their figure, that route eastern conquest through Khurāsān to Īliyāʾ — was assembled, expanded, and propagated in a transmission environment that overlaps geographically and chronologically with the Abbasid revolutionary network. The Abbasid movement did not invent the black-banner imagery; it captured and redirected an existing Hāshimite-eschatological field by attaching its own organizational machinery to it.</p>
</ClaimBox>

The chapter's central analytical task is therefore double. The first task is to map the eight matn families and their isnād structures against the historical daʿwa material, demonstrating the correspondence between hadith vocabulary and revolutionary machinery. The second task is to identify, for each report family, who the Mahdī figure is — explicitly named, deliberately unnamed, retrospectively reassigned, or ambiguously available for multiple Hāshimite candidates to claim. The candidate-identification question is not a side issue. It is the cluster's organizing political problem. The reports that say a man from Ahl al-Bayt will receive rule after the eastern banners win, the reports that name *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* among the black banners, the reports that distinguish the Khurāsānī army from the Mahdī it searches for, and the reports that explicitly name Banū al-ʿAbbās as the banner-bearers do not all identify the same figure. The cluster's variability on this point is precisely what made it useful to the Abbasid daʿwa, whose own foundational slogan — *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad wa-lā yusammī aḥadan* — was a programmatic refusal to name the candidate. The narrative ambiguity of the Mahdī across the report families is the same ambiguity the daʿwa weaponized.

The pro-banner and anti-banner halves of the cluster also require analytical separation. Where the pro-banner material commands joining the eastern army even crawling over snow and identifies *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* among them, the anti-banner material — preserved by Nuʿaym alongside the pro-banner reports — calls the same banners *fitna*, *misguidance*, and *unbelief*, attacks the *Musawwida* and black clothing as garments of fire, describes the revolutionary cadres as people who "*call to truth but are not its people*," and warns the faithful to "*stay on the ground*" rather than join. The contestation is the cluster's defining feature. It documents that the same symbolic system the Abbasid regime used as its public legitimating apparatus was being attacked, almost in real time, by counter-traditions whose specificity (kunyas, village nisbas, loose hair, "people of the state") is too tightly fitted to the Khurāsānī revolutionary cadres to be generic anti-rebel polemic.

## I. The Historical Daʿwa: al-Riḍā, Khurāsān, and the Unnamed Candidate

The historical control layer for this cluster is unusually rich. The Abbasid daʿwa is not merely inferred from later reports; it is documented in the chronicle tradition with circumstantial detail that includes specific names of organizers and missionaries, dated transitions in the movement's leadership, geographic instructions, recruitment numbers, internal organizational categories (the seventy, the twelve naqībs), and recorded mobilization slogans. Three principal early historians — Balādhurī, Ṭabarī, and Ibn al-Jawzī (himself working from earlier sources) — converge on the same organizational kernel. Their convergence is the analytical anchor against which the matching hadith and fitan material is read.

The passage links, in a single sentence, the four organizational decisions that define the historical daʿwa: (i) the transfer of Kaysānī-Hāshimid authority into the Abbasid line through the Abū Hāshim succession narrative, (ii) the recognition of Kūfa as the Shīʿī base, (iii) the strategic prioritization of Khurāsān as the recruitment ground, and (iv) the foundational slogan — *call to al-riḍā from Āl Muḥammad and do not name anyone*. The last instruction is the cluster's organizing political principle. The daʿwa's strength was precisely that it did not name its candidate. The slogan *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad* could absorb Alid sympathizers (who would hear it as referring to a descendant of ʿAlī), Kaysānī partisans (who would hear it as referring to the heir of Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya's authority transmitted through Abū Hāshim), Zaydī expectations, and broader Hāshimid sentiments — all simultaneously, without committing the movement to a specific candidate until victory made specification possible.

Ṭabarī's mechanics matter for the present argument because they are precisely what the matching hadith reports presuppose. The reports that command joining the eastern banners are not commanding joining a generic apocalyptic army; they are commanding joining a movement that operated with named missionaries, dated recruitment campaigns, structured organizational categories, and a deliberate naming policy. The line *amarahu an yadʿū ilā al-riḍā wa-lā yusammī aḥadan* is the operational principle of which the hadith reports' anonymous "man from Ahl al-Bayt" and "*Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*" are the prophetic-form expression. The reports do not name the Mahdī because the daʿwa they sacralize did not name him.

The cumulative historical control material therefore establishes nine organizational facts as the basis against which the hadith material is read: (1) the secret missionary network was sent specifically to Khurāsān; (2) the recruitment was deliberately framed as a call to *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*; (3) the candidate was strategically unnamed; (4) the daʿī was given a description of justice as the recruiting argument; (5) the recruitment proceeded in secrecy; (6) the initial cell consisted of seventy responders; (7) twelve naqībs were selected from the seventy as an organizational core; (8) the movement adopted black banners and black clothing as its public symbol; (9) the military execution of the revolution was led by Abū Muslim al-Khurāsānī, who took bayʿa from the Khurāsānī army specifically as *Hāshimiyya*. Each of these nine facts has a matching feature in the hadith and fitan material treated below.

**Candidate Identification — Group A.** The historical control layer does not identify a single Mahdī figure. It identifies a movement whose principle was non-identification. The most that can be said is that authority passed institutionally through a specific Abbasid line: Muḥammad b. ʿAlī al-ʿAbbāsī as the receiving organizer, Ibrāhīm al-Imām as his designated successor before the open revolution, Abū al-ʿAbbās al-Saffāḥ as the actual first Abbasid caliph after the revolution succeeded, and al-Manṣūr as the consolidator. The slogan *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad* was strategically broader than any one of these candidates and was designed to absorb Alid sympathy and Kaysānī inheritance simultaneously. The "Mahdī figure" of the historical daʿwa is best understood as a deliberately constructed institutional ambiguity rather than a person.

## II. The Eastern Banner Hadith: Ahl al-Bayt Suffering and the People from the East

The first major matn family in the hadith corpus is preserved through the Kūfan chain Ibn Masʿūd → ʿAlqama → Ibrāhīm → Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād, with proliferation downstream into the canonical and pre-canonical Sunnī collections. The matn structure is consistent across the variants. The Prophet sees young men from Banū Hāshim, weeps, predicts affliction for his household, foretells the arrival of a people from the East with black banners who will ask for justice, be denied, fight, prevail, and ultimately deliver authority to "a man from my household" who will fill the earth with justice. The closing command is the most operationally significant element: "*Whoever among you reaches that should go to them, even crawling over snow.*"

  arabic="بينا نحن عند رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم إذ أقبل فتية من بني هاشم، فلما رآهم النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم اغرورقت عيناه وتغير لونه. قال فقلت له: ما نزال نرى في وجهك شيئا نكرهه. قال: إنا أهل البيت اختار لنا الله الآخرة على الدنيا، وإن أهل بيتي سيلقون بعدي بلاء وتشريدا وتطريدا، حتى يأتي قوم من قبل المشرق معهم رايات سود، يسألون الحق فلا يعطونه، فيقاتلون فيضرون، فيعطون ما سألوا فلا يقبلونه، حتى يدفعوا إلى رجل من أهل بيتي فيملؤها قسطا كما ملؤوها جورا. فمن أدرك ذلك منكم فليأتهم ولو حبوا على الثلج."

The report's organizational fit with the historical daʿwa is precise at five distinct points. The Banū Hāshim youths in the opening scene match the Hāshimiyya as the public family-frame of the daʿwa. The Ahl al-Bayt suffering matches the Umayyad persecution of Hāshimid notables that the daʿwa cited as its historical grievance. The eastern direction matches Khurāsān. The black banners match the Musawwida's public color-symbol. The closing command — *fal-yaʾtihim wa-law ḥabwan ʿalā al-thalj* — matches the daʿwa's recruiting injunction to join the movement under conditions of hardship. The "man from my household" who receives authority after the eastern army wins matches the *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad* slogan's anonymous Hāshimid candidate.

**Ibn Mājah, Sunan, no. 4082** preserves the canonical Sunan witness through the same chain (ʿUthmān b. Abī Shayba → Muʿāwiya b. Hishām → ʿAlī b. Ṣāliḥ → Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād → Ibrāhīm → ʿAlqama → Ibn Masʿūd). The matn preserves the same opening, the same eastern-banner element, the same handover to a man from Ahl al-Bayt, and the same crawling-over-snow command. The Sunan placement made this the canonical Sunnī form for the broader Mahdī-coded eastern-banner expectation.

**Ibn Abī Shayba's Musnad no. 308**, the Bazzār, Abū Yaʿlā, Ṭabarānī, and al-Ḥākim parallels all preserve essentially the same matn through the same Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād transmission line. The cluster is therefore concentrated on Yazīd as the Common Link below the family memory and is propagated through standard Kūfan channels.

**Source-critical note on Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād.** Yazīd is the diagnostic transmitter for this matn family. His standing in the Sunnī rijāl tradition is mixed: he is generally graded as weak (*ḍaʿīf*), with documented Shīʿī sympathies, and is identified as a Kūfan transmitter whose late-life condition was assessed as deteriorating. The relevant analytical observation is not that his weakness disqualifies the report from authentication — that is not the source-critical question at issue — but that his transmitter profile matches the matn's pro-Ahl al-Bayt content. A Kūfan transmitter with Shīʿī sympathies is exactly the kind of figure through whom an eastern-banner, pro-Ahl al-Bayt matn would proliferate in the late Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. The report's transmission profile is therefore consistent with its content. No claim of partisan fabrication is being made; the more limited claim is that Yazīd is the figure through whom the matn becomes historically visible in the second century, and his transmitter environment is the environment in which the matn's vocabulary made political sense.

**Candidate Identification — Group B.** The matn explicitly leaves the figure unnamed: *rajulun min ahli baytī*. The closing handover is doctrinally important. The eastern army does not itself install the Mahdī; it wins the war, is offered what it asked for, refuses it, and then transfers authority to a man from the Prophet's household. The structural distinction is significant for the candidate question. The eastern army is the preparer, not the Mahdī. The Mahdī is the figure who receives authority from the army. In Abbasid reception this maps directly onto the operational reality of the daʿwa: Abū Muslim and the Khurāsānī army win the war; authority is then transferred to the Abbasid imam (Abū al-ʿAbbās al-Saffāḥ at the moment of victory). The matn's deliberate vagueness — "a man from my household" — is exactly what the daʿwa's *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad* slogan required. The report is *available* for Abbasid claim without being explicitly Abbasid. Earlier circulation may have permitted Alid or Kaysānī readings; the Abbasid revolution then captured the available matn and made it propaganda for the regime that actually received the eastern army's bayʿa.

## III. The Thawbān Black-Banner Mahdī Reports

The second major matn family is preserved through the chain Thawbān → Abū Asmāʾ al-Raḥabī → Abū Qilāba → Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ, with downstream proliferation through Sufyān al-Thawrī, ʿAbd al-Razzāq, and into the canonical and post-canonical collections. The Thawbān family is more developed than the Ibn Masʿūd family. It contains an expanded matn structure that adds, before the eastern banners element, a "treasure" scene in which three figures — each *ibn khalīfa* — fight at "your treasure" without any of them obtaining it. The matn then proceeds to the eastern black banners, the unmatched killing they inflict, and the closing command, which in the expanded form goes further than Group B by giving the figure an explicit title: *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*.

The cluster also preserves a short command form that omits the treasure scene and gives only the eastern-banners-go-to-them command. The relationship between the expanded and short forms is the cluster's most analytically interesting source-critical question, and is addressed below.

The Sunan matn does three things the Group B reports do not. First, it identifies the figure explicitly as *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* — both title (*Mahdī*) and theological designation (*caliph of God*). Second, it changes the command from joining "them" (the army) to pledging allegiance "to him" (the figure), distinguishing the army from the Mahdī more sharply than Group B. Third, it adds the treasure-and-three-sons-of-caliphs scene as the immediate precondition for the eastern banners. The narratorial aside *thumma dhakara shayʾan lā aḥfaẓuhu* — "then he mentioned something I do not remember" — is a transmissional honesty marker that gestures toward content the chain had partially lost.

Bayhaqī's preservation is diagnostically important for two reasons. First, the Bayhaqī version explicitly names Khurāsān (*min Khurāsān*) where the Ibn Mājah version says only "from the East." This is the most direct hadith-level instance of the daʿwa's geographic specificity entering the matn. Second, Bayhaqī appends the critical methodological note:

تفرد به عبد الرزاق عن الثوري.

"ʿAbd al-Razzāq alone transmitted it from al-Thawrī."

And, of the parallel route, Bayhaqī writes:

وروي من وجه آخر عن أبي قلابة وليس بالقوي.

"It is transmitted from another route from Abū Qilāba, but it is not strong."

Bayhaqī's caution is a piece of source-critical evidence preserved at the moment of canonical reception. The expanded matn rests on a single ʿAbd al-Razzāq transmission from al-Thawrī; the parallel route from Abū Qilāba is judged weak. Bayhaqī does not reject the report — he preserves it — but the explicit note documents that even the early Sunnī critical tradition recognized the expanded form's narrow transmission base. The implication for the present argument is that the expanded Thawbān form may be the developed and expanded version of a shorter underlying kernel, with the elaboration concentrated in the ʿAbd al-Razzāq generation. This is precisely the developmental sequence treated below.

**al-Bazzār, al-Ḥākim al-Mustadrak, Ibn ʿAsākir Tārīkh Dimashq, and Ibn Kathīr al-Nihāya** preserve the expanded matn through the broad Khālid → Abū Qilāba → Abū Asmāʾ → Thawbān nexus. The reception is stable; the matn proliferates across the standard Sunnī fitan and biographical literature.

**Candidate Identification — Group C.** The matn explicitly names the figure *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*. The title *Khalīfat Allāh* is theologically significant: it is the strongest possible eschatological-political designation, identifying the figure not merely as a Mahdī from the household but as God's caliph on earth. In Abbasid usage this title acquires particular resonance. The Abbasid regnal-name system included an Abbasid caliph who took the regnal title *al-Mahdī* (Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad b. al-Manṣūr, r. 158–169/775–785), and Abbasid panegyric explicitly applied the *Khalīfat Allāh* title to the Abbasid caliph. The expanded Thawbān matn is therefore well positioned for Abbasid reception in two distinct ways: (i) as a prediction of the Khurāsānī-led delivery of authority to the Abbasid imam (al-Saffāḥ as the first beneficiary, al-Manṣūr as consolidator), and (ii) as a prediction of the specific Abbasid caliph al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr, reread retrospectively in his favor once he took the regnal title. The most precise statement that can be made at the level of the extant transmission is that the matn is structurally available for both readings: it identifies the figure as Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī, leaves him otherwise unnamed, and places him in the immediate aftermath of the eastern army's victory. The Abbasid reception preserved in Nuʿaym and in the broader fitan tradition reads it as a prophecy fulfilled by the Abbasid house.

## IV. Khurāsān to Īliyāʾ and the Kaʿb-Style Conquest Apocalypse

The third matn family is preserved through Abū Hurayra → Qabīṣa b. Dhūʾayb → al-Zuhrī → Yūnus b. Yazīd → Rushdīn b. Saʿd → ʿAbd Allāh b. Yūsuf → Muḥammad b. Isḥāq → Bayhaqī. The matn is short and structurally distinct from Groups B and C: it omits both the Ahl al-Bayt suffering and the Mahdī-installation elements and concentrates on a single conquest motif — black banners coming from Khurāsān whose advance cannot be stopped until they are planted at Īliyāʾ (Jerusalem).

<HadithBlock
  arabic="تخرج رايات سود من خراسان لا يردها شيء حتى تنصب بإيلياء."
  translation="“Black banners will come out from Khurāsān; nothing will repel them until they are planted at Īliyāʾ.”"
  source="**Bayhaqī, Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa.** Chain as above."

Bayhaqī's critical note on this matn is one of the most important methodological observations preserved in the entire black-banner corpus:

تفرد به رشدين بن سعد عن يونس بن يزيد. ويروى قريب من هذا اللفظ عن كعب الأحبار ولعله أشبه والله أعلم.

"Rushdīn b. Saʿd alone transmitted it from Yūnus b. Yazīd. Something close to this wording is transmitted from Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, and that is perhaps more likely. God knows best."

Bayhaqī's caution does three things at once. First, it documents that the matn rests on a single Rushdīn b. Saʿd transmission from Yūnus b. Yazīd, which makes Rushdīn the Common Link and concentrates the cluster's evidential weight on a transmitter whose standing in the rijāl tradition is mixed (he is generally graded weak, with documented memory problems). Second, it documents that a similar wording is preserved as a saying of Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, the Yemenite-Jewish-convert source for much early apocalyptic material in the Islamic tradition. Third, it openly states that the Kaʿb attribution is "perhaps more likely" than the Prophetic attribution — a strikingly direct acknowledgment by an early Sunnī authority that some Mahdī material may better fit Kaʿb-style apocalyptic lore than secure Prophetic transmission.

The Kaʿb crossover is analytically significant for the entire black-banner cluster, not just for this matn. Kaʿb is the principal early carrier of Israelite-style apocalyptic geography in which Jerusalem (Bayt al-Maqdis / Īliyāʾ) is the eschatological terminus, the conquering army is theologically purified, and the unstoppable advance is divinely guaranteed. Bayhaqī's note suggests that at least one strand of the black-banner cluster — the Khurāsān-to-Īliyāʾ form — is best read as a Kaʿb-mediated apocalyptic motif that received Prophetic attribution late, possibly through Rushdīn's transmission environment. The implication is that the cluster as a whole drew on at least two distinct streams: a Prophetic-form pro-Ahl al-Bayt mobilization tradition (Groups B and C) and an Israelite-style conquest apocalypse mediated through Kaʿb (Group E). The Abbasid daʿwa was able to use both because both supported the same revolutionary geography: black banners from the East, unstoppable advance, divine guarantee.

**Ibn ʿAsākir, Tārīkh Dimashq** and **Ibn Kathīr** preserve the same matn through the same Rushdīn / al-Zuhrī chain, with Ibn Kathīr explicitly repeating Bayhaqī's caution about the Kaʿb origin. The reception preserves the methodological warning.

**Candidate Identification — Group D.** The matn does not identify a Mahdī figure. The banners themselves are the agents; their terminus is Jerusalem, not the installation of a figure. The cluster's function is conquest-apocalyptic rather than Mahdī-installation. For Abbasid propaganda the matn serves a different purpose than the Thawbān expanded form: it sacralizes the unstoppable Khurāsānī military advance without committing to any specific identification of the figure who receives power at the end of it. The matn is therefore politically useful in a complementary way — Group C identifies the figure, Group E identifies the geography. The two together provide a complete propaganda package, but the components are doing different work.

## V. The Short Thawbān Command and the Developmental Sequence

The fourth matn family is a short command form that omits the treasure scene and gives only the eastern-banners-go-to-them command with the same *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* identification. The short form is preserved through two distinct chains, one weak and one mawqūf, and is significant for the developmental question of how the Thawbān cluster expanded.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="إذا رأيتم الرايات السود قد جاءت من قبل خراسان، فأتوها؛ فإن فيها خليفة الله المهدي."
  translation="“When you see the black banners coming from Khurāsān, go to them, for among them is the caliph of God, the Mahdī.”"
  source="**Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, Musnad, no. 22387\.** Chain: Aḥmad → Wakīʿ → Sharīk → ʿAlī b. Zayd → Abū Qilāba → Thawbān → Prophet."
/>

The chain is methodologically weak: Sharīk (with his late-life memory issues) and ʿAlī b. Zayd b. Judʿān (graded weak in the rijāl tradition) together make this an unreliable bypass route. The matn's interest does not lie in the chain's strength but in the fact that the short command — go to the banners, the Mahdī is among them — circulated independently of the expanded treasure form. The short command is structurally simpler and could plausibly represent an earlier kernel that was later expanded with the *yaqtatilu ʿinda kanzikum* prologue.

**Bayhaqī, Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa** preserves the ʿAlī b. Zayd short form in the same wording, with the explicit critical note *wa-ruwiya min wajhin ākhara ʿan Abī Qilāba wa-laysa bi-l-qawī*. The weak bypass is acknowledged at the level of reception.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="ورواه عبد الوهاب بن عطاء عن خالد الحذاء عن أبي قلابة عن أبي أسماء عن ثوبان موقوفا قال: إذا رأيتم الرايات السود خرجت من قبل خراسان فآتوها فإن فيها خليفة الله المهدي."

The mawqūf preservation is the cluster's most diagnostically valuable single piece of evidence. The same matn that appears as Prophetic in Aḥmad's Musnad appears as a Companion-level saying through a related route. Following Schacht's classic observation, this is the textual profile of a saying that may have circulated as a Companion-level statement before being raised to Prophetic status in some transmission lines. The developmental sequence is hypothetical but textually supported:

1. **Stage 1** — Short command kernel circulating as mobilization slogan: "When you see the black banners from Khurāsān, go to them; the Mahdī is among them." This is the recruiting language of the daʿwa itself, plausibly attached to Thawbān as a Companion-level saying.  
2. **Stage 2** — Mawqūf preservation in the Khālid → Abū Qilāba → Abū Asmāʾ → Thawbān chain (preserved by Bayhaqī via ʿAbd al-Wahhāb b. ʿAṭāʾ).  
3. **Stage 3** — Marfūʿ elevation: the same matn is raised to the Prophet in parallel transmissions (Aḥmad via Sharīk → ʿAlī b. Zayd; some early Khālid transmissions).  
4. **Stage 4** — Expanded form: the treasure-and-three-sons-of-caliphs prologue is added, producing the developed Ibn Mājah / Bayhaqī Thawbān matn (preserved primarily through ʿAbd al-Razzāq → al-Thawrī → Khālid).

The sequence is not certain — the source-critical evidence is consistent with it but does not strictly entail it — but it accounts for the textual variation more parsimoniously than alternative readings. The short command is structurally simpler and matches the daʿwa's actual recruiting language; the expanded form adds narrative elements (the three sons of caliphs at the treasure) that look like a prologue retrofitted to anchor the recruitment slogan within a richer prophetic-narrative frame.

**Candidate Identification — Group D (short).** The matn says the Mahdī is *in them* (*fīhā Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*) — that is, in the eastern army, among its members. This is a stronger identification than Group B (which transfers authority to a man from Ahl al-Bayt without locating him in the army) and slightly different from Group C (which has the army win and *then* the Mahdī appear or arrive). The short form's ambiguity is operationally significant for the daʿwa: the Mahdī could be Abū Muslim's commander, the Abbasid imam traveling with the Khurāsānī cadres, or the figure to whom the army would deliver authority. The matn allows all three readings simultaneously. In Abbasid reception the practical referent is the Abbasid house, whose representatives traveled with and were protected by the Khurāsānī army during the revolution.

## VI. Explicit Abbasid Black-Banner Reports

The fifth major family makes the Abbasid identification openly. These reports name *Banū al-ʿAbbās*, name the Abbasid state, identify the Persians as the army-base of the movement, and treat black banners as the dynasty's legitimating symbol. The transmission environment for this material is concentrated in **Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād's Kitāb al-Fitan**, supplemented by Ibn ʿAsākir's Tārīkh Dimashq, with Kaʿb al-Aḥbār appearing repeatedly as the cited source. The Kaʿb attribution recurs significantly: where the earlier Groups B–D had to be read against an Abbasid background that the matn left implicit, Group F places the Abbasid identification on the surface.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="حدثنا أبو المغيرة عن ابن عياش عمن حدثه عن كعب قال: تظهر رايات سود لبني العباس حتى ينزلوا الشام، ويقتل الله على أيديهم كل جبار عنيد أو عدو لهم. يرابط بساحتهم آدم خمسة وأربعين صباحا، فيدخلها سبعون ألفا شعارهم فيها: أمت أمت. ثم تضع الحرب أوزارها، فيمكث ملكهم تسع في سبع، ثم ينتكث أمرهم بعد ثلاث وسبعين سنة."

The Kaʿb attribution is decisive for reading this matn. The Abbasid revolution is presented openly as the fulfillment of a Kaʿb-style apocalyptic prediction. The text is doing three things at once: it sacralizes the Banū al-ʿAbbās banners as God's instrument, it predicts the dynasty's military success against its enemies, and it predicts an eventual collapse "after seventy-three years." The number-language is suggestive: the Abbasid dynasty's foundation in 132/750 plus seventy-three years would place its predicted unraveling around 205/820 — well within the period of the early ʿAbbāsid civil wars (the fitna between al-Amīn and al-Maʾmūn, 195–198/811–813) and the broader crises of early ninth-century rule. Whether this is post-eventum prediction or schematic apocalyptic numerology cannot be settled on internal evidence alone, but the report's circulation through Nuʿaym in the early ninth century places its visible reception in exactly the period to which its numbers point.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="وسيكون لبني العباس راية، فمن تبعها رشد، ومن تخلف عنها هلك، ولم يخرج الإمام منهم إلى غيرهم."

The matn does three things explicitly: it makes following the Abbasid banner the criterion of guidance, it makes refusing it the cause of destruction, and it locks the imamate within the Abbasid family ("the imam will not go out from them to anyone else"). This is direct dynastic propaganda, indistinguishable in function from the panegyric prose that accompanied the Abbasid court's self-presentation.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="قال ابن عباس: إذا رأيتم الرايات السود تجيء من قبل المشرق فأكرموا الفرس فإن دولتنا فيهم."

The matn is striking because it explicitly links four elements that the historical record links: black banners, the East, the Persians, and "our state" (*dawlatunā*). The Persian dimension is the daʿwa's actual social base — the Khurāsānī army, the mawālī recruits, the Persianate cadres who carried the revolution — and the report's use of *dawlatunā* identifies the Abbasid state as the speaker's referent. The Ibn ʿAbbās attribution is post-eventum at this level: the eponymous ancestor of the Abbasid house is being made to predict the dynasty he was claimed by. The matn is one of the cluster's clearest examples of pro-Abbasid prophetic-form retrojection.

The transfer narrative of the Abbasid daʿwa — Abū Hāshim b. This is the cluster's most direct example of the Abbasid capture of Kaysānī authority.

**The historical Musawwida material**, scattered across Ṭabarī, Balādhurī, Yaʿqūbī, and Dīnawarī, confirms that the black color-symbol was the public visual identity of the Abbasid movement at the moment of its open revolution. The army wore black; the banners were black; the recruits were called the Musawwida (the black-clothed ones). The hadith vocabulary *al-rāyāt al-sūd* therefore was not abstract apocalyptic imagery but the precise public symbol of a specific historical army.

**Candidate Identification — Group F.** This is the cluster's clearest identification group. The Mahdī figure here is not an individual but the *Abbasid house collectively*. The matn says "Banū al-ʿAbbās" without further specification, identifies the banner as the dynasty's banner, and locks the imamate within the Abbasid family. The candidate is therefore the Abbasid imam at the moment of fulfillment — Abū al-ʿAbbās al-Saffāḥ at the original revolution, al-Manṣūr at consolidation, and potentially al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr in later reception. The specificity of the dynastic identification is what distinguishes Group F from the deliberately vague Groups B–D. Where the earlier families left the figure available for multiple Hāshimid readings, Group F closes the ambiguity by naming the family.

## VII. Khurāsān Searches for the Mahdī

The sixth family integrates Khurāsān into the broader Sufyānī-Mahdī apocalyptic narrative. Where Groups B–D presented the eastern army as the Mahdī's installer and Groups E–F made it the Abbasid dynasty's military base, Group G presents the Khurāsānī forces as actively *searching for* and *supporting* a Mahdī who is figured as someone else — a distinct figure not collapsed into either the army or the Abbasid line. The shift is significant for the cluster's later development because it preserves the possibility of an Abbasid-era Mahdī expectation that is *not* simply the Abbasid imam.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="عن علي قال: يظهر السفياني على الشام، ثم يكون بينهم وقعة بقرقيسيا حتى يشبع طير السماء وسباع الأرض من جيفهم، ثم يفتق عليهم فتق من خلفهم، فيقبل طائفة منهم يدخلوا أرض خراسان، وتقبل خيل السفياني في طلب أهل خراسان، فيقتلون شيعة آل محمد بالكوفة، ثم يخرج أهل خراسان في طلب المهدي."
  translation="ʿAlī said: “The Sufyānī will prevail over Syria. The Sufyānī integration is the diagnostic feature. The Sufyānī figure of the later Sunnī and Shīʿī fitan literature is a Syrian (Umayyad) anti-Mahdī, and his presence in this matn places the report within an apocalyptic framework that postdates the original Abbasid victory. The Khurāsānī forces here are not the conquering Musawwida of 130–132/747–750; they are a later eschatological army searching for a Mahdī figure who has not yet appeared. The matn is best read as a late integration of Khurāsānī revolutionary geography into a post-Abbasid Mahdī apocalypse — that is, a layer in which the Khurāsānī army that historically delivered the Abbasid revolution has been recast as the army that will deliver the final Mahdī.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="ثم يطلبون أهل خراسان في كل وجه، ويخرج أهل خراسان في طلب المهدي، فيدعون له وينصرونه."

The phrase *fa-yadʿūna lahu wa-yanṣurūnahu* — "they call for him and support him" — is structurally important because it preserves, in the Mahdī-search idiom, the same vocabulary the historical daʿwa used: *daʿwa*, *al-daʿūn*, supporters (*anṣār*). The Khurāsānī army does not merely wait; it *calls for* and *supports* the Mahdī. This is the daʿwa logic projected into the eschatological future. Whatever the matn's original date — late Umayyad, early Abbasid, or post-Abbasid — its vocabulary is unmistakably the daʿwa's vocabulary, repurposed for a future Mahdī expectation that has come to exceed the original Abbasid fulfillment.

**Candidate Identification — Group G.** The Mahdī of this group is distinct from the Abbasid house. The Khurāsānī forces *search for* him, *call for* him, and *support* him; they do not deliver authority to the sitting Abbasid imam. This is the cluster's clearest example of a Mahdī expectation that survives the Abbasid revolution and looks beyond it. The matn is structurally available for several possibilities: a future eschatological Mahdī unrelated to the Abbasid line, a Mahdī figure who emerges in later anti-Abbasid revolts (some of which used the Mahdī title), or a generalized post-Abbasid Hāshimite Mahdī. The most precise statement that can be made is that Group G preserves a Mahdī figure who is *not* the Abbasid imam and whose appearance is structurally delayed beyond the Abbasid victory.

## VIII. Counter-Reports and Anti-Abbasid Memory

The cluster's most diagnostically important methodological feature is the preservation, alongside the pro-banner material, of an anti-banner counter-tradition that attacks the same symbols the pro-banner reports sacralize. The counter-reports are not late polemic against the developed Sunnī Mahdī doctrine; they are early second-century material that targets the Abbasid daʿwa and its symbols with specificity. Their preservation alongside the pro-banner material in compilations like Nuʿaym's *Kitāb al-Fitan* documents that the black-banner corpus was contested in real time and that the pro-Abbasid reading was not uncontested in the period of the cluster's earliest visibility.

<HadithBlock
  arabic="إذا خرجت الرايات السود فإن أولها فتنة وأوسطها ضلالة وآخرها كفر."
  translation="“When the black banners come out, their beginning is fitna, their middle is misguidance, and their end is disbelief.”"
  source="**Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād** preserves the fitna-misguidance-unbelief counter-report:"
/>

The matn is a direct semantic inversion of the pro-banner command. Where the pro-banner reports say "*go to them even crawling over snow*," this counter-report says they are *fitna* at their inception, *misguidance* in their middle phase, and *disbelief* at their end. The three-stage structure (*awwaluhā / awsaṭuhā / ākhiruhā*) reads as an explicit periodization of the Abbasid revolution and the dynasty it produced: the revolution itself as fitna, the consolidating reign as misguidance, the later phase as unbelief. The matn does not commit to a specific historical break-point but the structure presupposes a movement that lasts long enough to have a beginning, middle, and end — that is, a dynasty rather than a single uprising.

The matn is the cluster's most direct counter-propaganda. It names the dynasty (*Banū al-ʿAbbās*) and attacks two of the dynasty's signature acts: the division of the community (the Abbasid revolution and its aftermath) and the imposition of black clothing (the Musawwida identity). The closing curse — *clothed by God in garments of fire* — is the symbolic inversion of the black-garment pro-banner imagery. The matn is unmistakably anti-Abbasid; the Prophetic attribution is plausibly post-eventum, generated within the counter-Abbasid environment that the pro-banner material was simultaneously claiming for itself.

The matn is the counter-reports' most specific attack and the cluster's most precise piece of socio-historical description. Five descriptive features identify the target with uncomfortable accuracy:

1. *Aṣḥāb al-dawla* — "the people of the state." This is the standard Abbasid self-designation; *dawla* in the early Abbasid period referred specifically to the new regime.  
2. *Lā yafūna bi-ʿahdin wa-lā mīthāq* — "they do not fulfill covenant or pact." This matches the early Abbasid record of broken promises to Alid allies, most notoriously the killing of Abū Salama al-Khallāl and the elimination of Alid claimants who had supported the daʿwa.  
3. *Yadʿūna ilā al-ḥaqq wa-laysū min ahlihi* — "they call to the truth but are not its people." This is the precise counter-formula to *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*: the daʿwa called to a true claim, but the actual beneficiaries were not the people of that claim.  
4. *Asmāʾuhum al-kunā wa-nisbatuhum al-qurā* — "their names are kunyas, their nisbas are villages." This is a remarkably specific socio-historical observation. The Khurāsānī revolutionary cadres operated under kunya-pseudonyms during the secret daʿwa phase (Abū Muslim's name itself is a kunya), and their nisbas were typically tied to villages rather than tribes (the Khurāsānī mawālī base).  
5. *Shuʿūruhum murkhāt ka-shuʿūr al-nisāʾ* — "their hair hangs loose like women's hair." This matches the documented hair-style of Abū Muslim's Khurāsānī army.

The specificity is too tight to be generic anti-rebel polemic. The matn is targeting the Abbasid revolutionary cadres at their socially identifying features. Its preservation in the same compilation (Nuʿaym's Fitan) as the pro-banner material documents that the contestation was preserved in real time within the same fitan compilations that the dynasty's apologists were also using.

**Candidate Identification — Group H.** The counter-reports do not identify a Mahdī figure. They identify a *target*: the Abbasid revolutionary movement, its dynasty, its cadres, and its symbols. The function is to delegitimate the pro-banner reading by attacking the same symbolic system the pro-banner reports sacralize. The matn's final clause — *thumma yuʾtī Allāh al-ḥaqq man yashāʾ*, "then God will give the truth to whom He wills" — defers Mahdī fulfillment beyond the Abbasid revolution entirely. The truth is not with the people of the state; it will come, but to a recipient the report leaves unspecified.

## IX. The Mobilization Mechanism: How the Call Actually Worked

The cluster's analytical force depends on the convergence between the historical daʿwa machinery and the hadith mobilization commands. Group I material — the operational vocabulary of the daʿwa itself — is preserved across the historical chronicles and matches, term for term, the recruitment language of the hadith reports. The convergence is not generic. It is specific vocabulary attached to specific organizational steps.

The anonymous-call principle (*yadʿū ilā al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad wa-lā yusammī aḥadan*) corresponds to the hadith reports' refusal to name the figure. The justice-description principle (*wa-waṣafa min al-ʿadl ṣifa*, "and he described a quality of justice") corresponds to the eastern army's *yasʾalūna al-ḥaqq* — they ask for the right. The secret-call principle (*fa-daʿā sirran*) corresponds to the late-developmental visibility of the cluster: the recruiting language existed in the daʿwa decades before it acquired Prophetic attribution. The seventy-and-twelve principle (*fa-lammā ṣārū sabʿīn akhadha minhum ithnay ʿashara naqīban*) does not have a direct hadith parallel — the organizational categories were operational rather than narrative — but it documents that the movement was structured rather than spontaneous, which the hadith reports presuppose. The black-color identity (*Musawwida*, *libs al-sawād*, *al-rāyāt al-sūd*) is the most visible vocabulary overlap: the banners of the hadith reports are the banners of the historical army.

The Khurāsānī geography is the strongest individual convergence. The historical sources name Khurāsān specifically as the daʿwa's recruiting ground (*ʿalayka bi-Khurāsān*); the hadith reports name Khurāsān specifically as the source of the banners (*min qibali Khurāsān*, *min Khurāsān*). No other region is involved at the same level of specificity in either layer.

The bayʿa principle (*akhdh al-bayʿa ʿalā al-jund min al-Hāshimiyya khāṣṣatan*) corresponds to the hadith commands to pledge allegiance (*bāyiʿūhu wa-law ḥabwan ʿalā al-thalj*). The pledging-even-under-hardship motif is itself diagnostic: the daʿwa required physical commitment from recruits who traveled from their home regions to the Khurāsānī base, and the crawling-over-snow imagery is a poetic intensification of that real organizational requirement.

The Hāshimiyya frame (*akhdh al-bayʿa ʿalā al-jund min al-Hāshimiyya khāṣṣatan*) corresponds to the hadith reports' emphasis on Banū Hāshim and Ahl al-Bayt. The daʿwa was operationally framed as a Hāshimid mobilization; the hadith reports' Hāshimid framing is the prophetic-form rendering of the same identification.

The cumulative judgment is precise. The Abbasid daʿwa did not publicly identify the Mahdī because ambiguity was politically useful. The slogan *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad* allowed the Abbasids to absorb Alid, Kaysānī, and broader Hāshimid hope while insiders directed loyalty toward the Abbasid imam. In the hadith reports, the Mahdī is often deliberately vague — a man from Ahl al-Bayt (Group B), *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* among the eastern banners (Groups C–D), or simply unmentioned (Groups E and the counter-reports). In Abbasid reception, the practical beneficiary is the Abbasid house: first concretely al-Saffāḥ at the moment of the revolution, then al-Manṣūr as consolidator, and then potentially al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr as the figure who explicitly took the regnal title. Abū Muslim is not the Mahdī in any matn family; he is the military preparer whose elimination by al-Manṣūr created its own counter-memory.

The cluster's political genius lay in this multilevel ambiguity. A pro-Alid sympathizer could read Group B and hear it as pointing to an Alid figure; an Abbasid loyalist could read the same matn and hear it as pointing to al-Saffāḥ. A reader in the 170s/780s could hear Group C and identify *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī* as al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr; a reader in the 130s/750s could hear the same matn and identify the figure with whichever Hāshimid candidate had received the banners' allegiance. The hadith vocabulary tracked the daʿwa's operational ambiguity, and the operational ambiguity was the daʿwa's strategic asset. The Mahdī of this cluster is structurally unstable by design.

## XI. Transmitter Notes

The cluster's transmission profile spans multiple regional environments — Kūfan (Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād, ʿAlī b. Ṣāliḥ, Sharīk, ʿAlī b. Zayd), Basran-Yamāmī (Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ, Abū Qilāba), Syrian (Abū Asmāʾ al-Raḥabī, Thawbān as a Syrian Companion in the standard biographical tradition), Yemeni-Egyptian (al-Zuhrī via Yūnus b. Yazīd, Rushdīn b. Saʿd), and the Ḥimṣī compilation environment of Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād. The breadth makes a single-sectarian-transmitter reading inadequate; what the cluster shows is the convergence of multiple regional transmitter networks around the same set of motifs in the period of the Abbasid revolution and its immediate aftermath.

**Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād** is the Group B Common Link. His weakness in the rijāl tradition is well attested and his Shīʿī sympathies are documented. His role is best read as a Kūfan transmitter whose pro-Ahl al-Bayt content was congenial to the eastern-banner matn; no claim of partisan fabrication is required.

**Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ** is the Group C/D Common Link. He is a Basran transmitter of acceptable standing whose role anchors both the expanded Thawbān form (through al-Thawrī and ʿAbd al-Razzāq) and the mawqūf short form (through ʿAbd al-Wahhāb b. ʿAṭāʾ). Bayhaqī's critical caution about the parallel weak route is the diagnostic preservation of the cluster's developmental complexity.

**Abū Qilāba** appears in multiple matn families (the Thawbān expanded form, the short command form, and the affirmative *huwa al-mahdī ḥaqqan* identification of ʿUmar II in the preceding chapter). His position across these clusters is conduit-level; he is best read as a major Basran node through whom Mahdī-related material proliferated in the early second century without himself being a partisan transmitter.

**Abū Asmāʾ al-Raḥabī** is the Syrian intermediary in the Thawbān chain. His role anchors the cluster's transmission in the Syrian environment, which is significant because Thawbān himself was a Syrian Companion.

**ʿAlī b. Zayd b. Judʿān** is the weak-route Common Link for the Aḥmad short Thawbān form. His weakness is documented across the rijāl tradition; his transmission cannot bear the weight of the report's authentication, but the matn it preserves is the diagnostic short form that may correspond to the daʿwa's actual recruiting slogan.

**Sharīk** is the immediate transmitter in the Aḥmad short Thawbān route. His late-life memory problems are well documented; the chain is methodologically weak.

**Rushdīn b. Saʿd** is the Group E Common Link. His weakness is documented; Bayhaqī's explicit note that the Kaʿb attribution is "perhaps more likely" is the cluster's clearest acknowledgment of cross-tradition apocalyptic material.

**Kaʿb al-Aḥbār** is the cluster's recurring apocalyptic source. The Kaʿb attribution appears in Group F (Nuʿaym no. 570 on the Banū al-ʿAbbās banners) and is suggested by Bayhaqī as the more likely origin of Group E (Khurāsān to Īliyāʾ). Kaʿb's role as the principal early carrier of Israelite-style apocalyptic geography in the Islamic tradition is well established; his presence in the cluster documents the Abbasid daʿwa's absorption of Israelite-style conquest apocalyptic into its propaganda apparatus.

**Muḥammad b. ʿAlī al-ʿAbbāsī** is not a hadith transmitter in the classical sense. He is the historical-control figure who organizes the daʿwa machinery. His role in the cluster is foundational rather than transmissional: he is the figure whose operational decisions (the Khurāsānī missionary, the anonymous slogan, the seventy-and-twelve organization) the hadith reports translate into prophecy.

**Abū Muslim al-Khurāsānī** is similarly historical rather than transmissional. His role is the military execution of the revolution. His elimination by al-Manṣūr in 137/755 generated its own substantial counter-tradition (the *Muslimiyya* / *Khurramiyya* / Sunbādh movements treated in subsequent chapters), but for the present cluster he is the military preparer whose banners the hadith reports command following.

## XIII. Provisional Conclusion

The black-banner cluster is the first major layer in the Mahdī corpus in which expectation becomes organized revolutionary propaganda that actually succeeds. The Abbasid daʿwa did not merely inherit Hāshimite expectation from the Kaysānī and broader Alid environments; it operationalized it. It took the ambiguous hope for a righteous figure from Āl Muḥammad, relocated it to Khurāsān, organized it through secret missionaries and twelve naqībs selected from seventy initial responders, dressed it in black, and then either generated or absorbed hadith and fitan reports that cast the movement as Prophetic command. The Mahdī figure in this layer is intentionally unstable: publicly *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*, narratively a man from Ahl al-Bayt or *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*, politically the Abbasid imam at the moment of fulfillment, militarily prepared by Abū Muslim, and historically realized first in the Abbasid seizure of power under al-Saffāḥ, with later retrospective readings available to attach the title to al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr once he took the regnal name. The instability was not a defect. It was the mechanism by which the Abbasids captured multiple streams of Hāshimite hope under a single mobilizing apparatus.

The cluster's pro-banner and anti-banner halves preserve the contestation of this capture in real time. Where the pro-banner reports sacralize the eastern army and command joining the banners even crawling over snow, the counter-reports — preserved in the same fitan compilations by the same compilers — call the same banners *fitna*, *misguidance*, and *unbelief*, identify the Abbasid cadres as "*the people of the state who call to truth but are not its people*," and explicitly attack Banū al-ʿAbbās for clothing the community in black garments that God will replace with garments of fire. The contestation is not late polemic against a settled doctrine. It is the cluster's defining feature in the period of its earliest visibility. The black-banner corpus, considered as a whole, is therefore best read not as a neutral apocalypse but as contested memory of the Abbasid revolution: pro-Abbasid forms sacralize the Khurāsānī movement; anti-Abbasid forms denounce the same banners as fitna and false claims to truth; both halves preserve specificity (Khurāsān, Banū al-ʿAbbās, the Persians, *aṣḥāb al-dawla*, *thiyāb al-sawād*) that ties the material unmistakably to the historical revolution.

This is the moment when Mahdī expectation became imperial propaganda. Before the Abbasid revolution, Mahdī material had collected around figures who had been defeated; after the Abbasid revolution, Mahdī material collected around a regime that ruled. The mechanism of capture is what this cluster documents. The Mahdī of the Abbasid black-banner layer is whoever the daʿwa's deliberate non-identification could be made to specify at the moment of fulfillment — first concretely the Abbasid imam, later available for retrospective application to al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr, narratively always "a man from my household" or *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*. The historical movement and the prophetic-form reports tracked each other because the same political work was being done in both registers. The hadiths were the daʿwa's recruiting language elevated to Prophetic command; the daʿwa was the hadiths' content made into an army that won.

## Appendix C — Pro-Banner vs. Anti-Banner Comparison

| Element | Pro-banner reading | Anti-banner reading |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Black banners | God's instrument; sign of guidance | Beginning fitna, middle misguidance, end unbelief |
| Joining the banners | Command: "go to them even crawling over snow" | Command: "stay on the ground; do not move your hands or feet" |
| Eastern army | Asks for justice; fights and wins; hands rule to Mahdī | "Calls to truth but are not its people" |
| Banū al-ʿAbbās | The legitimate dynasty; banner \= guidance | "What have I to do with Banū al-ʿAbbās? May God clothe them in garments of fire" |
| Black clothing | Visible mark of the saved community | Garments to be replaced with garments of fire |
| Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī | Present in the eastern army; allegiance commanded | Absent; the truth God gives is deferred to an unnamed future recipient |
| Mahdī identification | Eventually the Abbasid imam | Withheld; "God will give the truth to whom He wills" |

## Appendix D — Central Thesis Restated

The Abbasid black-banner cluster is the prophetic-form afterlife of the Abbasid daʿwa. The historical sources document a real missionary network that called secretly in Khurāsān to an unnamed *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*, selected seventy responders and twelve naqībs, and eventually mobilized under black banners and black clothing. The hadith and fitan reports translate that revolutionary structure into sacred command: black banners will come from the East or Khurāsān; people must go to them even crawling over snow; they will hand rule to a man from the Prophet's household or contain *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*. The reports are best read not as isolated predictions but as competing acts of revolutionary memory. Pro-Abbasid forms sacralize the Khurāsānī movement; anti-Abbasid forms denounce the same banners as fitna, misguidance, and false claims to truth. The Mahdī figure of this cluster is structurally unstable by design — publicly *al-riḍā min Āl Muḥammad*, narratively a man from Ahl al-Bayt or *Khalīfat Allāh al-Mahdī*, politically the Abbasid imam at the moment of fulfillment, retrospectively available for application to al-Mahdī b. al-Manṣūr once he took the regnal title. The instability was the mechanism by which the Abbasids captured multiple streams of Hāshimite hope. This is the moment when Mahdī expectation became imperial propaganda — and the moment at which the contestation between pro-banner and anti-banner traditions becomes the cluster's most analytically diagnostic feature.
