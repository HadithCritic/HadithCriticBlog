The hadith that forbids slapping the cheeks, tearing the garments, and "calling with the call of the Age of Ignorance" appears in Bukhārī, Muslim, the Sunan works, Aḥmad, and half a dozen other major compilations. It looks like an ancient prohibition with prophetic roots, transmitted through revered Companions: ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd on one side, ʿĀʾisha on the other. Neither attribution is genuine. Both are back-projections above a single node: a Kūfan jurist who outlived the Battle of Karbalāʾ by two to three years, watched the mourning for Ḥusayn organize in his own city, and gave the prohibition its three-part Prophetic form.

That man is **Masrūq b. al-Ajdaʿ (d. 63–64 AH)**. This article argues not merely that he is the common link — the point at which the tradition crystallises in every chain — but that the tradition is a *deliberate polemical act*: a hadith written against a specific, living practice he witnessed in Kūfa, then attributed upward to senior Companions and the Prophet to give it authority it could not have possessed on its own.

## The Prohibition and What It Actually Targets

The canonical wording, attributed to Ibn Masʿūd, runs:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="لَيْسَ مِنَّا مَنْ لَطَمَ الْخُدُودَ، وَشَقَّ الْجُيُوبَ، وَدَعَا بِدَعْوَى الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ."
  translation='"He is not one of us who slaps the cheeks, tears the collars, and calls with the call of the Age of Ignorance."'
  source="Bukhārī 1294, Muslim 103a, Nasāʾī 1862, Aḥmad 3658, and others — all via Masrūq"
/>

Three prohibited acts. The first two are physical: *laṭm al-khudūd* (slapping the face) and *shaqq al-juyūb* (tearing the collar open). The third is verbal: *daʿwā al-jāhiliyya*, the "call" or "invocation" of pre-Islamic practice. The three acts look like a generic funeral-conduct rule. They are not. They map with exact precision onto the practices documented at and after Karbalāʾ. That mapping is the argument, and it is not circumstantial.

## Kūfa, 61–64 AH: The Practices Masrūq Witnessed

Masrūq b. al-Ajdaʿ died in 63–64 AH. The Battle of Karbalāʾ occurred on 10 Muḥarram 61 AH. The window between those two dates — roughly two to three years — is when the mourning for Ḥusayn first organized in Kūfa, the very city in which Masrūq lived and taught. He was not a distant observer. He was a senior jurist and judge at the epicentre of the pro-ʿAlid movement that was coalescing around him.

The primary chronicle and *maqātil* literature — al-Ṭabarī, al-Balādhurī, Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī — documents what Masrūq would have seen. Its testimony maps the three clauses of the hadith to three independently attested behaviors.

**Clause 1: *laṭama al-khudūd*** — Slapping the cheeks. Al-Ṭabarī records an eyewitness account of the captive women of Ḥusayn's family being led past his body:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="نَظَرْتُ إِلَى تِلْكَ النِّسْوَةِ لَمَّا مَرَرْنَ بِحُسَيْنٍ وَأَهْلِهِ وَوَلَدِهِ صِحْنَ وَلَطَمْنَ وُجُوهَهُنَّ."
  translation='"I watched those women as they passed by Ḥusayn and his family and children: they screamed and slapped their faces."'
  source="al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, vol. 5, year 61 AH"
/>

The verb is *laṭamna* — the same root as *laṭama al-khudūd* in the hadith. Al-Balādhurī confirms the scene independently.

**Clause 2: *shaqqa al-juyūb*** — Tearing the collar. Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī, in *Maqātil al-Ṭālibiyyīn*, describes Zaynab bint ʿAlī on the eve of ʿĀshūrāʾ:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="فَشَقَّتْ ثَوْبَهَا، وَلَطَمَتْ وَجْهَهَا، وَخَرَجَتْ حَاسِرَةً تُنَادِي: وَاثَكْلَاه! وَا حُزَنَاه!"
  translation='"She tore her garment and slapped her face and came out unveiled, crying: O bereavement! O grief!"'
  source="Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī, Maqātil al-Ṭālibiyyīn, life of al-Ḥusayn"
/>

Both physical acts of the prohibition — *shaqq* of the garment and *laṭm* of the face — in a single sentence about the sister of Ḥusayn.

**Clause 3: *daʿwā al-jāhiliyya*** — The invocation. This is the load-bearing clause. Al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī both record what Zaynab cried out over her brother's body:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="يَا مُحَمَّدَاه، يَا مُحَمَّدَاه! صَلَّى عَلَيْكَ مَلَائِكَةُ السَّمَاءِ، هَذَا الْحُسَيْنُ بِالعَرَاءِ، مُرَمَّلٌ بِالدِّمَاءِ."
  translation='"O Muḥammad, O Muḥammad! May the angels of heaven bless you. Here is Ḥusayn in the open, soaked in blood."'
  source="al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh, vol. 5; confirmed by al-Balādhurī, Ansāb al-Ashrāf"
/>

*Yā Muḥammadāh. Yā Ḥusaynāh.* Calling upon a sacred figure by name in grief. This is exactly the act the third clause targets — not a generic warcry but an invocation of the dead by name, the form pre-Islamic mourners used to call upon tribal heroes. A Kūfan jurist in the Ibn Masʿūd tradition, trained in the austere theology of the Kūfan school, would have heard this as pre-Islamic invocation wearing Islamic dress.

The match is not approximate. It is structural:

<ContextNote title="Clause-by-Clause Mapping">

| Hadith clause | Attested post-Karbalāʾ practice | Primary source |
|---|---|---|
| لَطَمَ الْخُدُودَ (slapping cheeks) | *ṣiḥna wa-laṭamna wujūhahunna* — they screamed and slapped their faces | Ṭabarī T-3; Balādhurī |
| شَقَّ الْجُيُوبَ (tearing collars) | *fa-shaqqat thawbahā* — she tore her garment | Maqātil M-1; Ṭabarī T-2 |
| دَعَا بِدَعْوَى الْجَاهِلِيَّة (the invocation) | *yā Muḥammadāh, yā Ḥusaynāh* — calling the dead by name | Ṭabarī T-4; Balādhurī |

</ContextNote>

This is not coincidence. A three-part prohibition answering term-for-term to three independently documented acts of a specific historical moment does not arise by accident. Masrūq was present in Kūfa when these acts occurred, had the juristic standing to issue a binding ruling, and had every doctrinal motive to do so.

## The Tawwābūn: Mourning Becomes a Movement

The mourning did not remain a private eruption of grief. It organized, in Kūfa, during the exact years of Masrūq's final activity. The Penitents (*al-Tawwābūn*), led by the Companion Sulaymān b. Ṣurad al-Khuzāʿī, formed from Kūfans who had failed to defend Ḥusayn and now sought expiation. Al-Ṭabarī records their founding impulse:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="مَا لَنَا تَوْبَةٌ مِمَّا فَعَلْنَا إِلَّا أَنْ نَقْتُلَ أَنْفُسَنَا فِي الطَّلَبِ بِدَمِهِ."
  translation='"We have no atonement for what we did except to spend our lives seeking vengeance for his blood."'
  source="al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh, events of 61–65 AH (the Tawwābūn section)"
/>

Their gatherings — mourning, lamentation, graveside pilgrimage to Karbalāʾ — were forming from 61 AH onward. By 62 AH they numbered around a hundred; by the time of their armed march in 65 AH they had swelled to thousands. A senior Kūfan jurist watching an organized penitential mourning movement crystallize in his own city, explicitly centered on the veneration of a slain figure whose invocation paralleled pre-Islamic hero-cult, had both the motive and the platform to respond.

<ClaimBox title="The Polemical Thesis">

Masrūq did not preserve an older prohibition. He *gave* it its three-part Prophetic form in Kūfa during the years 61–64 AH, in direct response to the mourning practices for Ḥusayn that he witnessed: the physical *laṭm*, the physical *shaqq*, and above all the verbal invocation of a slain figure by name. He then attributed the prohibition to ʿAbd Allāh b. Masʿūd and — through a different student — to ʿĀʾisha, to give it the Prophetic authority a local juristic ruling could never command.

</ClaimBox>

## The Full Isnad: Every Line Passes Through One Man

There is no independent path to Ibn Masʿūd or to ʿĀʾisha that does not go through him. Below Masrūq, the tradition fans out through four students and two recoverable partial common links. Above Masrūq, it splits to two different senior Companions. The fan below and the fork above both point to the same node.

## Why These Two Companions? The Attribution Strategy

The choice of Ibn Masʿūd and ʿĀʾisha was not arbitrary. It was a two-track authority strategy, and it is the most direct evidence that the attribution above Masrūq was deliberate rather than a memory error.

**Ibn Masʿūd** was Masrūq's own teacher — the founder of the Kūfan legal school, resident in Kūfa, the supreme authority for the community Masrūq served. Attributing a ruling to Ibn Masʿūd meant attributing it to the source of Kūfan jurisprudence itself. For a Kūfan audience engaging in practices Masrūq wanted to stop, there was no more authoritative voice. Ibn Masʿūd was the school. And Masrūq had the personal connection to make the attribution plausible: he was Ibn Masʿūd's most prominent student. The claim that he had heard this directly from his teacher would have raised no eyebrows in Kūfa.

**ʿĀʾisha** served a different function. The ʿĀʾisha attribution — preserved only in ʿAbd al-Razzāq's *Muṣannaf* (no. 6889), carried through a third student of Masrūq (Abū al-Ḍuḥā) rather than through Ibrāhīm or Ibn Murra — extended the prohibition's reach beyond the specifically Kūfan school. ʿĀʾisha was the most authoritative female Companion, a second and independent locus of Prophetic transmission, and Masrūq did genuinely transmit from her on other traditions. Her name gave the prohibition pan-Islamic standing that Ibn Masʿūd alone, as the founder of one regional school, could not have provided.

The result is the classic "spread of isnād" above the common link: one fixed wording shown going up through two different senior Companions via two different sub-chains. This is not what honest relay looks like. It is what deliberate back-projection looks like.

<ClaimBox title="The Fork Is the Fingerprint">

A tradition with a single, stable wording whose chain "spreads" above one transmitter to two or more different Companions — with no independent route reaching either Companion except through that transmitter — is the textbook profile of a tradition that entered circulation at that transmitter. The text became a Prophetic hadith at Masrūq. The Companion names were attached afterward, by him or by those immediately below him, to give it reach.

</ClaimBox>

## The Third Clause: The Theological Spearpoint

The first two clauses regulate visible mourning behavior. Objectionable, in Masrūq's view, but regulable as cultural holdovers. The third clause is where the polemic does its real work, and a variant reading exposes its target with unusual precision.

The canonical wording reads *daʿwā al-jāhiliyya* — the "call" or "claim" of the Age of Ignorance, a generic tribal warcry. But Ibn Abī Shayba's *Muṣannaf* (no. 11677), through the Aʿmash route, reads something different:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="لَيْسَ مِنَّا مَنْ لَطَمَ الْخُدُودَ وَشَقَّ الْجُيُوبَ وَدَعَا بِدُعَاءِ أَهْلِ الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ."
  translation='"He is not one of us who slaps the cheeks, tears the collars, and calls with the invocation (duʿāʾ) of the people of the Age of Ignorance."'
  source="Ibn Abī Shayba, al-Muṣannaf, no. 11677 (al-Shathrī ed.)"
  label="Key Variant"
/>

*Daʿwā* and *duʿāʾ* are not the same word. *Daʿwā* is a slogan or tribal cry. *Duʿāʾ* is supplication — the act of calling upon, of invoking. Where *daʿwā* targets general noise, *duʿāʾ* targets a specific act: *calling upon the dead by name*. That is precisely the act documented at Karbalāʾ: *yā Muḥammadāh, yā Ḥusaynāh*. For Masrūq, trained in the Ibn Masʿūd school's austere rejection of any approach to the dead that echoed pre-Islamic hero-invocation, this vocal act was not grief. It was the pre-Islamic *daʿwā* reappearing under an Islamic name.

The *duʿāʾ* variant reveals what the third clause was always about: not the volume of mourning, but its theological direction. The prohibition is not funeral etiquette. It is a targeted strike at the practice of invoking Ḥusayn's name with the expectation of his intercession or response — the first move toward what would eventually become Twelver Imamological mourning theology.

<ContextNote title="What Masrūq Was Actually Prohibiting">

The three clauses form a single, unified intervention:

**Clauses 1–2** (*laṭm* and *shaqq*): Address the physical mourning acts women were performing at and after Karbalāʾ — acts with roots in pre-Islamic practice that the Islamic tradition had long tried to suppress. These are the visible, regulable surface.

**Clause 3** (*duʿāʾ al-jāhiliyya* in its *duʿāʾ*-reading): Attacks the theological core — the invocation of a slain figure by name, treating him as having post-mortem power to receive supplications. This is where Masrūq, as an heir to the austere Kūfan school of Ibn Masʿūd, saw the practices crossing from cultural mourning into something he understood as a structural parallel to pre-Islamic hero-cult.

The third clause is the spearpoint. The first two are the shaft.

</ContextNote>

## Ḥusayn's Own Injunction — and What It Tells Us

Al-Ṭabarī preserves a report in which Ḥusayn himself, on the eve of his death, forbids his sister the very acts the hadith forbids:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="يَا أُخَيَّة، إِنِّي أُقْسِمُ عَلَيْكِ فَأَبِرِّي قَسَمِي، لَا تَشُقِّي عَلَيَّ جَيْبًا، وَلَا تَخْمِشِي عَلَيَّ وَجْهًا، وَلَا تَدْعِي عَلَيَّ بِالْوَيْلِ وَالثُّبُورِ إِذَا أَنَا هَلَكْتُ."
  translation='"My sister, I adjure you, keep my oath: do not tear a collar for me, do not scratch a face for me, and do not call out woe and ruin over me when I am gone."'
  source="al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh, vol. 5 (the eve of ʿĀshūrāʾ)"
/>

Tearing a collar. Scratching a face. Calling out in grief. The triad of the prohibition formula, in the same order of concern, placed on the lips of Ḥusayn himself. Whether this report is a genuine memory or itself a product of the same anti-lament discourse, it demonstrates one thing: the triad was circulating as a fixed, recognizable unit in the earliest Karbalāʾ material. The prohibition and the practice belong to one moment. That moment is 61–64 AH. Masrūq was alive in it.

## Why Not a Genuine Prophetic Saying?

Two objections to the forgery reading must be addressed.

**"The prohibition of mourning-lament is older than Masrūq."** True. Traditions against *niyāḥa* (wailing lamentation) circulate under other chains with different common links — the Abū Mūsā *ḥalaqa / salaqa / kharaqa* tradition, the Abū Mālik al-Ashʿarī "four things" list, and others. These are distinct hadiths with distinct isnad profiles. They do not support the specific three-part formula *laṭm + shaqq + daʿwā/duʿāʾ al-jāhiliyya*. The older anti-lament sentiment existed; Masrūq gave it its particular three-part Prophetic form in response to particular events.

**"Masrūq knew both Ibn Masʿūd and ʿĀʾisha; he might have heard it from them."** The fork above a single wording is the counter-evidence. Had Masrūq independently heard the identical three-part prohibition from two different senior Companions, we would expect the chains to diverge in wording or emphasis as they do when a tradition genuinely has two sources. Instead, the wording is one wording, and the Companion names are two names. The economical explanation is that the wording is Masrūq's fixed point, and the Companion names were attached above it to give it bilateral Prophetic authority — Ibn Masʿūd for the Kūfan audience, ʿĀʾisha for the broader Islamic world.

## Conclusion

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">

The prohibition *laysa minnā man laṭama al-khudūd wa-shaqqa al-juyūb wa-daʿā bi-daʿwā al-jāhiliyya* is a polemical text. As a transmitted Prophetic report, it begins with Masrūq b. al-Ajdaʿ — a senior Kūfan jurist who was alive and active in Kūfa during the two to three years after Karbalāʾ, when the mourning for Ḥusayn first organized in the city where he lived and taught. The three acts the formula prohibits — slapping cheeks, tearing collars, invoking a sacred figure by name in grief — are the three acts the chronicle and *maqātil* literature independently attests the women of Karbalāʾ and the Tawwābūn performing in precisely those years.

Masrūq gave the prohibition its three-part Prophetic form in response to these practices. He then attributed it upward: to his own teacher Ibn Masʿūd, to give it the authority of the Kūfan school's founding figure; and to ʿĀʾisha, through a second student-line, to extend its reach beyond the Kūfan school into the broader Islamic tradition. The fork above him — one wording, two senior Companions — is not the signature of dual hearing. It is the structural fingerprint of back-projection above a common link.

The tradition's later career as a pan-Islamic anti-Shīʿī proof-text is a secondary application. Its origin is a local, dated, juristic polemic: a text written in Kūfa, for Kūfa, in the years when Ḥusaynid mourning was moving from raw grief into organized religious practice — and attributed to the Prophet to stop it.

</VerdictBox>
