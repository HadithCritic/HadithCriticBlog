Suppose every ḥadīth collection disappeared tomorrow. Every account of the caliph ʿUthmān gathering the Qurʾān, having copies made, and sending them to the major cities was lost. Could a historian still discover that an early written Qurʾān had been standardized and distributed?

**Yes.** Not because a later author said it happened, but because the surviving Qurʾānic manuscripts still carry the physical traces of that event.

Rejecting ḥadīth as a source of religious legislation does not require us to pretend that the past can only be known through revelation. We can investigate historical claims using manuscripts, inscriptions, dates, and the surviving objects themselves. The historical question is, *What evidence would have to exist if it really happened?*

The Qurʾān's own instruction to verify reports (49:6) provides a fitting starting point. We need not accept the story of ʿUthmān simply because somebody transmitted it. We can test its central claims against evidence independent of the narrators.

<QuranVerse verse="49:6" />

The evidence is surprisingly specific. Early copies reproduce the **same unusual spellings in the same verses**; they preserve **small regional differences that form recognizable families**; and some of the oldest surviving manuscripts fit the history of an early written standard. Researchers including **Marijn van Putten** and **Hythem Sidky** have demonstrated these patterns through systematic manuscript comparison, not merely by repeating the collection narratives.[^1][^2]

The case does not rest on one famous manuscript or one curious spelling. It rests on **many separate observations fitting the same history**.

<ClaimBox title="The Argument in One Sentence">
The standard Qurʾānic manuscripts preserve inherited, verse-specific writing choices and identifiable regional branches. Taken together with their historical dating, that evidence strongly supports an early written standard and the distribution of regional master copies associated with ʿUthmān's reign.
</ClaimBox>

## The First Clue: A Word That Refuses to Be Spelled One Way

Take the Arabic word *niʿmah* (نعمة), meaning a favor, grace, or blessing. In certain expressions, early Qurʾānic manuscripts write the end of this word in two different ways. For ease of comparison, we can represent them as **نعمت** (a *t*-shaped ending) and **نعمه** (an *h*-shaped ending). The old script often lacks modern dots, so these are explanatory forms rather than claims about every manuscript's exact letter shapes.

The crucial point is simple: **the way the word is pronounced in the relevant expressions does not tell a scribe which ending to draw.** Either graphic choice can convey the same word.[^1] (pp. 273–275)

A useful analogy is an English book where the same author sometimes writes *color* and sometimes *colour*. If several copies preserve the unusual switch at the **same exact sentences**—rather than merely using the same word—then something beyond the spoken text has survived. The scribes are preserving decisions inherited from earlier writing.

That is precisely the kind of evidence Van Putten investigated in his 2019 paper, *“The Grace of God” as Evidence for a Written ʿUthmanic Archetype*.[^1]

### Three ancient codices, two verses, the same switch

Start with just two locations: **Q 5:11** and **Q 5:20**. Three different ancient Qurʾānic codices preserve this contrast.

The manuscript transcriptions are more revealing than a modern printed Qurʾān. The actual diplomatic words in those six positions include **ںعمٮ / ںعمه** in the Parisino-Petropolitanus, **نعمت / نعمه** in the Arabe 328e fragment, and **ٮعمت / نعمه** in Mingana 1572b. Their editors use different character conventions to represent undotted ancient forms. The final graphic distinction, however, is the same.[^3]

These really are three different physical books. The **Codex Parisino-Petropolitanus** survives in pieces in several collections, and those pieces must count as *one* codex. **Arabe 328e** is associated with the book represented by British Library Or. 2165, even though this particular leaf is preserved in Paris. The manuscript **Mingana 1572b** is not the much-publicized Birmingham 1572a parchment.[^1] (pp. 276–277)

No one has to take my word for the readings. The folio links above let readers inspect the editorial transcriptions directly. These six entries were checked from **scholarly diplomatic transcriptions**; we are not claiming six independently deciphered photographs.

Now comes the question: if these were separate scribes simply writing down an orally recited word, why would they repeatedly choose the *same non-obvious spelling at the same verse*? Two examples invite the question. The full set of examples makes it more compelling.

## Not Two Words: Twenty-Three Locations

That creates 322 possible manuscript-location entries, but many leaves are missing or do not cover a particular verse. Read down that chart carefully. The spelling is **not uniform**. It keeps changing with the verse:

- **Q 5:7 — h; Q 5:11 — t; Q 5:20 — h.** The same chapter switches twice.
- **Q 14:6 — h; Q 14:28 — t; Q 14:34 — t.** Another chapter preserves a different sequence.
- **Q 16:18 — h; Q 16:71 — h; Q 16:72 — t; Q 16:83 — t.** A particularly revealing pair occurs in adjacent verses, 16:71–72.
- **Q 29:67 — h; Q 31:31 — t; Q 33:9 — h; Q 35:3 — t.** The alternating pattern continues across multiple chapters.
- **Q 43:13 — h; Q 52:29 — t; Q 68:2 — h; Q 93:11 — h.** The last passages are just as relevant as the first.[^1] (p. 278)

That is the point: the scribes did not simply agree to spell *niʿmah* with one favorite letter. **They inherited where to change the letter.**

The consequence is straightforward. **Speech transmits the word. Writing transmits this particular pattern of letters.** A single spelling convention would not explain the repeated switching at fixed locations; a verse-by-verse writing model does.

### Does the pattern have exceptions?

Yes. It would be suspicious to conceal them.

At **Q 16:114**, Van Putten's published comparison has an h-shaped ending in Saray Medina, while eight other eligible entries have a t-shaped ending. At **Q 37:57**, the table is split: four manuscripts show t and two show h. The modern Cairo edition's spelling does not automatically settle which ancient form is earlier. These disagreements are part of the evidence, not a problem to delete.[^1] (pp. 278–281)

The completed tally is therefore **383 agreeing manuscript-pairs out of 399 eligible comparisons at the same location**, or approximately **95.99%**. But do not mistake that number for 399 independent experiments: the same codices appear repeatedly, some are related, and the word was specifically selected because its spelling is informative.[^5]

A historian's inference is stronger when it can explain both the shared pattern **and** the occasional departure from it. Copying from earlier writings readily allows a scribe to preserve an old peculiarity in one passage while changing it in another.

## What, Exactly, Is a Written Archetype?

Historians call the earlier written model behind a group of surviving copies an **archetype**. This need not be an autograph signed by the first editor. It is the **earliest common written form that the surviving copies allow us to reconstruct**.

If manuscripts A, B, and C all contain the same peculiar spelling sequence, it is reasonable to infer a common earlier written model. They might have been copied through several now-lost intermediate books. We do not need to find the original sheet to detect the relationship.

<ContextNote title="Do not confuse an ancestor with a surviving manuscript">
No known extant Qurʾān manuscript has been securely demonstrated to be the very master copy held by ʿUthmān. The claim is not that we have his autograph. It is that descendants preserve identifiable features of older written exemplars from which their texts came.
</ContextNote>

Van Putten's paper is not merely proposing this as a possibility. It makes the case that **shared, irregular orthography is evidence for written transmission from a common ancestor**. A scribal rule common to the era could explain broad consistency; it is less capable of explaining **why the same irregular alternatives appear in precisely the same verses** across different codices.[^1] (pp. 274–275, 280–284)

## A Second Spelling Test: How They Wrote *Ibrāhīm*

The *niʿmah* example is not the only place where the early written tradition preserves location-specific choices. Van Putten also studied spellings of **Ibrāhīm** that differ in the presence or absence of a medial letter, especially the shorter and longer written forms conventionally represented as **ابرهـم** and **ابراهيم**.[^6]

This matters because a strong theory should not depend exclusively on one word. Our subsequent research extracted **180 *Ibrāhīm* spelling observations** in the selected source-linked atlas, with individual occurrences distinguished even when the name appears more than once in a verse. We then tested whether other manuscripts at the same precise word location could predict a withheld manuscript's spelling. Of **111 testable readings**, the positional-majority method predicted **104 correctly**; seven differed.[^5]

This result is not as uniform as the strict *niʿmah* comparison—and we should not present it as such. Among the disagreements are positions in **Q 14:35, Q 16:120, and Q 29:16**. The test uses a selected diplomatic-edition dataset, not 180 photographs newly interpreted from ink. Nevertheless, it provides another concrete way to investigate inherited written spelling habits. In some positions the manuscripts agree, in others they preserve local or scribal variation. Those are exactly the differences that a history of copying needs to explain.

The first question is now answered: the standard Qurʾānic tradition carries evidence of a **shared written past**. But the manuscripts reveal something more interesting still. Not all copies preserve *precisely the same* written details. Some differences sort them into **families**.

## The Second Clue: The Books Fall into Regional Families

Imagine copying one master document into several official copies and sending those copies to different cities. A tiny copying difference slips into one regional exemplar: a **و** (and), a **ف** (so), an extra **ا**, or a missing **ه**. Local scribes then copy that exemplar. Hundreds of miles away, another city's scribes preserve a slightly different letter.

Neither community needs to change the broad message of the passage. Yet generations later those minute differences can tell us **which earlier book each copy followed**.

That is what historians mean by a **regional manuscript family**.

### Here are ten actual examples of the regional pattern

The early books concerned with Qurʾānic spelling (*rasm*) describe a striking distribution: for many positions, **Syria and Medina share one written form, while Basra and Kufa share another**. The following examples come from the historical regional comparison compiled by Sidky, not from a claim that every one of these four master manuscripts still survives.[^2] (Table 1, pp. 140–142)

| Verse | Syrian and Medinan form | Basran and Kufan form | What changes? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2:132** | **وأوصى** | **ووصى** | An extra alif |
| **3:133** | **سارعوا** | **وسارعوا** | Opening wāw |
| **9:107** | **الذين اتخذوا** | **والذين اتخذوا** | Opening wāw |
| **18:36** | **خيرا منهما** | **خيرا منها** | Extra mīm in the pronoun |
| **26:217** | **فتوكل** | **وتوكل** | Fāʾ versus wāw |
| **40:26** | **وأن** | **أو أن** | Extra written element |
| **42:30** | **بما كسبت** | **فبما كسبت** | Opening fāʾ |
| **43:68** | **يا عبادي** | **يا عباد** | Final yāʾ |
| **43:71** | **تشتهيه** | **تشتهي** | Final hāʾ |
| **57:24** | **فإن الله الغني** | **فإن الله هو الغني** | Additional *huwa* |

The point is not that every difference is equally ancient or that every regional report is flawless. It is that **many independent locations show the same grouping of regional versions**. If scribes in four distant cities simply introduced unrelated changes on their own, why would their deviations consistently organize into the same related pairs?[^2] (pp. 142–145)

These are ten examples of a repeated family division. They are not the entire comparison. In the same source there are **Syrian-only** differences, **Kufan-only** differences, and additional variations that refine the relationships. For instance:

| Verse | Distinctive regional form | Forms elsewhere | Family clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4:66** | Syria: **قليلا** | Medina, Basra, Kufa: **قليل** | A distinctive Syrian final alif |
| **7:43** | Syria: **ما كنا لنهتدي** | Others: **وما كنا لنهتدي** | A Syrian absence of wāw |
| **7:75** | Syria: **وقال الملأ** | Others: **قال الملأ** | A Syrian addition of wāw |
| **36:35** | Kufa: **وما عملت** | Others: **وما عملته** | A distinctive Kufan ending |

These additional examples matter. The Syrian version does not simply share *everything* with Medina; it also has its own characteristic readings. The Kufan version likewise preserves traits that distinguish it from Basra. That is what allows historians to go beyond saying “there were differences” and work out a pattern of family relationships.[^2] (pp. 140–145)

### Could the medieval scholars simply have invented those regional differences?

If the only evidence were medieval descriptions, that would be a serious question. Indeed, **Michael Cook** reconstructed the basic regional relationships from the historical spelling reports in 2004, and he was careful about what such literary data could establish.[^7]

The crucial development came when researchers began checking **the reported differences against surviving manuscript leaves**. A manuscript found in Egypt might preserve the old Syrian pattern. Another book might preserve a mixed or changed pattern. Its physical text can be compared with the description independently of the prestige of the person transmitting that description.

**Hythem Sidky's 2020 study made that comparison on a much broader scale.** His material covered **30 manuscript identifiers** and more than sixty recorded regional features. His manuscript analysis reproduced the central relationships inferred from the written variant reports. From the combined evidence he reconstructed **four ancestral regional codices** and argued that the Syrian exemplar belonged specifically to the tradition of **Ḥimṣ**, not simply Damascus.[^2] (pp. 133–135, 182–184)

That is a substantial conclusion. It means surviving Qurʾānic manuscripts **independently corroborate the existence of a historically structured regional copying tradition**. They are not merely modern witnesses to a story told in a later ḥadīth collection.

## Let Us Check the Regional Evidence Against Actual Folios

The following observations come from the diplomatic manuscript transcriptions imported into our own research database. They show what it means to *check* a reported variant rather than just quote it.

### Q 9:107: Is there a wāw before “those who...”?

Sidky's regional list distinguishes **الذين اتخذوا** from **والذين اتخذوا**. Our transcriptions record both possibilities in different codices:

| Manuscript | Recorded form | Manuscript location |
|---|---|---|
| **Arabe 330g / reconstructed codex 330G** | **والذين اتخذوا**, wāw present | [67v, line 3](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/31/page/67v) |
| **Arabe 330f (F001)** | **والذين اتخذوا**, wāw present | [43r, line 5](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/500/page/43r) |
| **Codex Amrensis I** | **الذين اتخذوا**, no initial wāw | [15v, line 7](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/332/page/15v) |
| **Saray Medina** | **الذين اتخذوا**, no initial wāw | [38r, line 10](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/56/page/38r) |

That is a direct, repeatable comparison of **four manuscript transcriptions at the same verse**, not a vague statement that different manuscripts existed. Each word has a specific manuscript, folio, and line. These observations support the presence of more than one inherited written form; they do **not** by themselves prove the city in which any manuscript was copied.[^3][^5]

### Q 18:36 and Q 5:53: Differences that require only a letter

At **Q 18:36**, the historical regional forms differ between **منهما** and **منها**. The Damascus manuscript known as **DAM 01-25.1** has a recorded form **منها** at [13v, line 7](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/116/page/13v). Tübingen Ma VI 165 has an edition-derived form representing **منهما** at **7r, line 20**. The Tübingen folio was viewed during the preceding research stage, though that inspection did not independently establish the original ink state of the diagnostic letter.[^3]

At **Q 5:53**, one of the reported variants includes an opening wāw: **يقول / ويقول**. The diplomatic transcription of **Qāf 47** records the shorter form at [6v, line 7](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/15/page/6v); that of **E20** records the prefixed form at [7r, line 23](https://corpuscoranicum.org/en/manuscripts/545/page/7r). Both are precisely located observations.[^3]

A single extra letter is not a dramatic textual alteration. That is exactly why such details are useful. A scribe has little reason to invent the *same* minor regional spelling habit at a dozen different positions unless there is an inherited model, a copying convention, or a related line of transmission to explain it.

### Q 8:67: A particularly revealing Syrian clue

Sidky draws attention to **Q 8:67**, where a form conventionally written **للنبي**, with the article, appears in the early manuscripts associated with the old Syrian tradition rather than the otherwise widespread **لنبي**. The relevant manuscripts include **British Library Or. 2165**, the **Codex Parisino-Petropolitanus**, and **Cambridge University Library Add. 1125**. Sidky places photographic examples of the forms together in his **Figure 7**.[^2] (pp. 171–173)

This is important for more than a single regional spelling. The manuscripts were preserved in **different places**, including Fusṭāṭ and Damascus, yet share the same unusual textual marker. Sidky connects that marker with the **Ḥimṣī** tradition and argues that the Syrian distribution history is more specific than a generic story about sending a book to Damascus.

It is one of the clearest occasions where physical manuscripts illuminate a historical geographical claim instead of simply accepting it.

## What the Family Pattern Actually Looks Like

The four regional textual types are related, but not indistinguishable. **Syria is closest to Medina, Medina to Basra, and Basra to Kufa**, in the undirected ordering inferred from the classic regional variants. The relationship does not by itself say which copy was written first.[^2] (pp. 143–145)[^7]

This family relationship becomes much less mysterious if an initial standard was reproduced in several official exemplars, which local scribes then copied. A small letter change could become a marker for an entire descendant group. If local copying continued over generations, those markers would persist beside new scribal corrections and local spelling habits.

That is why Sidky's finding of four ancestral regional types is historically significant. It is not just a story of ancient books happening to resemble one another. **The differences themselves exhibit a pattern of shared descent**.[^2]

## We Also Tested the Pattern Computationally

Our accompanying computational work did not stop at reading the conclusions of the published papers. It converted relevant manuscript tables into machine-readable comparisons and tested how the variants fit possible family trees. Here the evidence must be separated carefully.

**First**, a small dataset that we collected directly from the publicly available **Corpus Coranicum diplomatic transcriptions** was too sparse to reconstruct a unique regional tree. In its first form all **105 of 105** possible six-manuscript trees tied. An expanded six-manuscript selection admitted **35 of 105** trees, but that narrower result depended on a disputed reading at **Q 42:30**. Removing the disputed position brought the result back to **105 of 105**.[^5]

**Second**, we reproduced a substantially larger portion of **Sidky's published manuscript collation**: **63 variant positions**, **30 manuscript identifiers**, and **1,024 reported entries**. After excluding marked corrections and restricting the coding to suitable two-form comparisons, one comparative matrix contained **462 conservatively coded observations at 32 characters**. That is a *replication and analysis of published data*, not 462 newly deciphered manuscript words.[^8]

For one carefully selected eight-manuscript comparison, a computer evaluated all **10,395 possible unrooted binary family trees** and found **one with the lowest number of required spelling changes**. When the variant assignments were randomized, the shuffled datasets fitted a tree substantially worse. In that sense, the published manuscript relationships contain a strong, non-random family pattern under the model tested.[^8]

| Question tested | Result | What it means |
|---|---:|---|
| Possible eight-manuscript trees examined | **10,395** | The program did not select a drawing by eye. |
| Best-fitting trees in the selected published comparison | **1** | One arrangement minimizes written changes under that scoring model. |
| Randomized eight-manuscript comparisons | **500** | None matched the observed score; evidence of non-random structure under this test. |
| Other manuscript profiles tested for placement | **22** | Only **3** had one best attachment; **19** remained ambiguous. |

A computer cannot turn an incomplete manuscript into a complete one. The strong broad division is not the same as certainty about every smaller branch, and the 30 manuscript profiles are not 30 freshly verified seventh-century photographs. The useful result is narrower and still powerful: **the regional differences behave like inherited information, not a random scatter of spelling decisions**.[^8]

### A disputed reading we did not erase

At **Q 42:30**, Sidky's published Appendix B lists the **Codex Parisino-Petropolitanus** on the side lacking an opening **ف**, whereas the current Corpus Coranicum edition of **Arabe 328b, folio 59r, line 13** codes a form with **ف**. Alrahawan's separate 2021 manuscript study also reports the no-fāʾ form.[^2] (Appendix B, p. 204)[^9]

Which reflects the original ink? Until the manuscript image and any corrections are assessed, it would be irresponsible to choose the answer simply because it makes a family tree look cleaner. The same caution applies to disputed source representations at **Q 3:184**, **Q 4:66**, and **Q 7:43**.[^8]

But those unresolved details do **not** make the larger evidence for a written ancestor vanish. Van Putten's position-specific spelling case stands on many other observations, and Sidky's wider collation has a much broader set of regional markers than any one disputed character.

## How Early Does This Written History Go?

The early Qurʾānic manuscripts are not medieval copies invented centuries after the supposed standardization. Some belong, on script and book-history grounds, to the **seventh century** or the seventh-to-eighth-century transition.

The **Codex Parisino-Petropolitanus** is assigned by François Déroche to approximately the **third quarter of the seventh century**. Tübingen Ma VI 165 has a much-discussed radiocarbon range around **649–675 CE**, though that date pertains to the **parchment**, not directly to the ink. Manuscript estimates must distinguish the animal skin's age, the style of writing, and the date at which a particular textual layer was copied.[^1] (pp. 276–277)[^4][^10]

This matters for the historical reconstruction. The written tradition with its peculiar spellings and regional forms must have existed **before the surviving copies that reproduce them**. Manuscript descent therefore pushes the common models further back than the dates of the manuscripts available to us today.

In his 2024 chapter *Textual Criticism of the Quran*, Van Putten adopts the conclusion that **four master copies were produced at the standardization of the Qurʾānic text and distributed to four major regions**. Sidky reaches a similarly definite historical conclusion in the closing discussion of his 2020 study. These conclusions arise from the convergence of manuscript evidence with the historical setting—not from a requirement that every late report be accepted as truthful.[^2] (pp. 182–184)[^11] (pp. 156–157)

## What About the Qurʾān Manuscript That Names ʿUthmān?

There is even a surviving manuscript that **claims** to have been written by ʿUthmān himself. This is not a ḥadīth report. It is a statement written onto a physical codex.

The manuscript is **Istanbul, Turkish and Islamic Arts Museum (TIEM), inventory no. 457**. Its final folio contains an attribution conventionally transcribed:

**The attribution is not accepted as authentic.** The manuscript's script and decoration place it after ʿUthmān's lifetime; the inscription cannot turn the codex into an autograph from 30 AH. Nor can we simply assume the attribution was added when the main text was first copied. Its evidentiary value depends on when and why that claim was written.[^12]

This is a useful historical distinction. **A manuscript may be genuine and its claim about its own author false.** The object tells us that someone asserted ʿUthmānic authority; it does not independently prove the claim written on it. TIEM 457 is therefore interesting evidence for the *history of attribution and authority*, not the central proof of early standardization.

The much stronger proof is the collective written structure preserved by independently surviving manuscript families.

## Does the Ṣanʿāʾ Palimpsest Contradict All This?

An old manuscript found at Ṣanʿāʾ contains an earlier Qurʾānic text that was erased and written over. Its **lower text** has substantive differences from the standard tradition at some locations; its **upper text** belongs to the standardized family. Scholars call such reused parchment a **palimpsest**.[^13]

This is not something to hide. It demonstrates that the earliest history of Qurʾānic writing was **not simply every known manuscript already being letter-for-letter identical**. A common written ancestor for the *dominant standardized text* does not require denying earlier or alternative written forms.

In fact, it makes the argument more precise. We are identifying **the ancestor of a particular family of surviving manuscripts**, not claiming that no alternative written text ever existed. Any reconstruction that erased the Ṣanʿāʾ lower text from consideration would be methodologically weaker.

## The Difference Between a Historical Fact and a Religious Authority

A Submitter does not need ḥadīth to legislate religion in order to recognize historical findings that can be checked through physical evidence. These are different questions:

| Question | Type of claim | How it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| Must a reported Prophetic statement become religious law? | **Religious authority** | The Qurʾān is the criterion of religious legislation. |
| Did early scribes reproduce an earlier written Qurʾān? | **Historical transmission** | Compare shared manuscript spellings at the same verses. |
| Were several distinct master texts copied and distributed? | **Historical reconstruction** | Compare regional letter patterns, manuscript families, and dates. |
| Was ʿUthmān the commissioner? | **Historical attribution** | Combine the early chronology, regional exemplar evidence, and critically assessed historical testimony. |

That last step is an **inference**. But historical inference is not the same as baseless speculation. Much of ancient history is established through several independent lines of evidence that fit one explanation better than its alternatives.

It would be an odd standard to reject a strongly corroborated historical event simply because later writers also reported it. A traditional narrative does not automatically become false because it appears in a ḥadīth collection; nor does it become true merely because it appears there. **The evidence determines the judgment.**

<VerdictBox title="What the Manuscripts Establish">
The Qurʾānic manuscript tradition preserves a highly specific written inheritance. Fourteen manuscript witnesses in Van Putten's published comparison repeat the unusual placement of the two *niʿmah* spellings across twenty-three Qurʾānic locations. A different body of regional letter differences, corroborated by surviving manuscripts, identifies four ancestral regional textual families. Sidky and Van Putten regard that evidence as strongly corroborating the early standardization and distribution of Qurʾānic exemplars associated with ʿUthmān.

We do not have to find the caliph's signature, accept every collection report, or treat ḥadīth as religious authority to reach that historical conclusion. We can examine the books, compare their surviving letters, and reconstruct the written tradition they came from.
</VerdictBox>
