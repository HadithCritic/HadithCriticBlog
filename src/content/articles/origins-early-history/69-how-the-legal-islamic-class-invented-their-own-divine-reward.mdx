## The Report That Rewards Judicial Error

The report is famous because it sounds modest. A judge strives, reaches the right ruling, and receives two rewards. The same judge strives, reaches the wrong ruling, and receives one reward. In later legal theory, the statement became a convenient proof that qualified ijtihād remains religiously valuable even when the result is mistaken.

<HadithBlock
  label="Core report"
  arabic="إِذَا حَكَمَ الْحَاكِمُ فَاجْتَهَدَ ثُمَّ أَصَابَ فَلَهُ أَجْرَانِ، وَإِذَا حَكَمَ فَاجْتَهَدَ ثُمَّ أَخْطَأَ فَلَهُ أَجْرٌ"
  translation="When a judge gives judgment and strives, then reaches the correct ruling, he receives two rewards. When he gives judgment, strives, and errs, he receives one reward."
  source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7352; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1716a; with parallel versions in Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, Aḥmad, Ibn Ḥibbān, al-Tirmidhī, and others."
/>

The moral problem begins with the office named in the report. The hadith speaks about the ḥākim, the person who issues judgment. A judge can transfer property, validate liability, dissolve a dispute, punish an accused person, or give public force to a claim. When his ruling fails, the cost falls on litigants and communities. The report, however, frames the error through the judge's reward. It gives the official a spiritual account of his own mistake while leaving the harmed party outside the wording.

<ClaimBox title="Core claim">
This report gives religious value to professional legal error. Sincere judges may be forgiven by God; that point is too broad to settle the historical problem. The sharper issue is the route by which a hadith rewarding mistaken judgment enters the record through jurists, judges, and legal transmitters whose authority depended on ijtihād being treated as pious even when it failed.
</ClaimBox>

The report became useful once jurists had to defend disagreement. If one jurist forbids what another permits, the legal class needs a way to keep both scholars inside the circle of religious legitimacy. If a judge rules incorrectly after exerting effort, the legal class needs a way to protect the office from appearing morally reckless. The judge reward hadith supplies that protection in compressed prophetic language: success earns double reward, error still earns reward, and the method survives the contradiction.

## The Variant Map

The variants cluster around two main lines. The first line reaches the Prophet through ʿAmr b. al-ʿĀṣ and becomes visible through Yazīd b. ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Hād. The second line reaches the Prophet through Abū Hurayrah and becomes visible in the extant collections through the Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm line, then through Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd, Sufyān al-Thawrī, Maʿmar, and ʿAbd al-Razzāq. The wording is stable enough to show one doctrine, while the route history shows how narrow the recoverable transmission is.[^1]

This map should already make the reader slower. The hadith has wide later collection, but wide collection after a bottleneck does not create early public circulation. The question concerns the point at which the doctrine first becomes historically recoverable, and the evidence keeps returning to Medinan legal transmitters.

## The Yazīd Bundle

The strongest canonical form runs through Yazīd b. ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Hād. The report travels through ʿAmr b. al-ʿĀṣ, then Abū Qays, Busr b. Saʿīd, Muḥammad b. Ibrāhīm al-Taymī, and Yazīd. From Yazīd it branches through Ḥaywah b. Shurayḥ, ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Darāwardī, Bakr b. Muḍar, and ʿAbd Allāh b. Jaʿfar into the later collections.

Yazīd is not an easy target for a cheap rijāl attack. Ibn Ḥajar lists him as a Medinan transmitter and records the normal reliability language: Aḥmad saw no problem in him, Ibn Maʿīn and al-Nasāʾī called him trustworthy, Ibn Saʿd described him as reliable and abundant in hadith, and Yaʿqūb b. Sufyān called him a trustworthy Medinan with good hadith.[^2] A historical argument has to be more careful than saying that a weak narrator invented the report.

The issue lies in the shape and function of the bundle. Al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-awsaṭ* preserves the useful critical notice that the ʿAmr b. al-ʿĀṣ form is not narrated from ʿAmr except through this isnād and that Yazīd b. al-Hād is alone with it.[^3] That sentence does not condemn Yazīd, but it names the bottleneck. Once the report is used as a major proof for the moral safety of legal error, this bottleneck matters more than the later multiplication of collectors.

## The Confirmation Note Inside Yazīd's Report

The most important feature in the canonical bundle is the internal confirmation note. Yazīd says that he narrated the report to Abū Bakr b. Muḥammad b. ʿAmr b. Ḥazm, who replied that Abū Salamah had narrated the same report to him from Abū Hurayrah.

<HadithBlock label="Internal confirmation note">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic">
    فَحَدَّثْتُ بِهَذَا الْحَدِيثِ أَبَا بَكْرِ بْنَ مُحَمَّدِ بْنِ عَمْرِو بْنِ حَزْمٍ، فَقَالَ: هَكَذَا حَدَّثَنِي أَبُو سَلَمَةَ، عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "I narrated this hadith to Abū Bakr b. Muḥammad b. ʿAmr b. Ḥazm, and he said: Abū Salamah narrated it to me in this way from Abū Hurayrah."
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

A defender reads this as confirmation. A historian reads it as a claim of confirmation embedded inside the same narrative unit. It may preserve a real exchange between Yazīd and Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm, and it may also function as an authentication device. In both cases, the note places the legal authority of Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm inside the report itself. The report therefore arrives with its own Medinan legal endorsement already attached.

The identity of Abū Bakr makes the note more relevant, not less. Ibn Ḥajar describes him as the Medinan judge. Mālik is reported to have said that no one in Medina possessed knowledge of judgment like Abū Bakr b. Muḥammad b. ʿAmr b. Ḥazm. The same notice says that ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz appointed him, ordered him to write knowledge from ʿAmrah bt. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān and al-Qāsim b. Muḥammad, and that Abū Bakr held authority in Medina, judgment, and the pilgrimage season.[^4] The confirmation note is therefore not a random memory from an ordinary transmitter. It is the report's legal world speaking in its own voice.

<ContextNote title="Why the note matters">
The Abū Hurayrah confirmation inside Yazīd's report should be treated as evidence, but its evidentiary role is limited. It shows that Yazīd's version already knew how to appeal to Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm and the Abū Salamah line. It does not by itself prove an independent pre-Yazīd public circulation of the report.
</ContextNote>

## The Abū Hurayrah Line and the ʿAbd al-Razzāq Bottleneck

The Abū Hurayrah route later appears as its own isnād: Abū Hurayrah to Abū Salamah, Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm, Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Anṣārī, Sufyān al-Thawrī, Maʿmar, ʿAbd al-Razzāq, and then later collectors. On paper, this looks like a second Companion route. In practice, it corresponds to the very confirmation line already named inside Yazīd's report.

/>

The early critics noticed the narrowness of this route. Al-Tirmidhī says that the Abū Hurayrah report is ḥasan gharīb from this route, and that he knows it from Sufyān al-Thawrī through Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd only by way of ʿAbd al-Razzāq from Maʿmar from Sufyān.[^5] In *al-ʿIlal al-kabīr*, al-Tirmidhī asks Muḥammad b. Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī about the report. The answer is even sharper: he did not know anyone who narrated it from Maʿmar except ʿAbd al-Razzāq, and ʿAbd al-Razzāq made mistakes in some of what he narrated.[^6]

Al-Bazzār gives the same structural warning from another angle. After citing the Abū Hurayrah route, he says that he does not know a better isnād for it from Abū Hurayrah, and he does not know anyone who shared with ʿAbd al-Razzāq in transmitting it with this isnād.[^7] A later compiler can multiply the report through the students of ʿAbd al-Razzāq, but the older critical notices keep pointing to a single recoverable route at the Maʿmar to ʿAbd al-Razzāq layer.

This does not make ʿAbd al-Razzāq a fabricator. The point is narrower. The Abū Hurayrah line cannot be used as if it were a broad independent companion tradition, because the surviving form is already tied to the confirmation line named by Yazīd and is then carried through a late and identifiable bottleneck.

## How the Two Routes Relate

The two routes are best read as connected legal evidence, not as cleanly independent public memory. The ʿAmr route contains an internal appeal to the Abū Hurayrah line through Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm. The later Abū Hurayrah route is the same Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm line in independent isnād form, but its preservation runs through Yaḥyā, Sufyān, Maʿmar, and ʿAbd al-Razzāq.

The legal usefulness of the report is also concentrated in the same milieu. Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm was a judge and legal administrator. Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Anṣārī was a Medinan judge and jurist. Ibn Ḥajar calls him the Medinan qāḍī, and the rijāl notices preserve high praise for his fiqh: Ayyūb reportedly said he left no one in Medina more knowledgeable in law than Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd, and al-Thawrī considered him highly esteemed among the people of Medina.[^8]

This does not turn every judge into a suspect narrator. It gives the report a different evidentiary profile from a neutral devotional report. A statement about judicial error moves through judges and jurists, while a statement granting spiritual value to legal reasoning moves through people whose profession depended on legal reasoning having religious value. The overlap belongs to the route map itself.

## The Mālik Test

The silence of Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ* remains one of the strongest contextual problems. Mālik died in 179 AH, and his book preserves Medinan law, reports, Companion practice, Successor opinion, and the working assumptions of early Medinan legal culture. A hadith about the reward of judicial ijtihād, transmitted through Medinan legal authorities, belongs exactly to the world the *Muwaṭṭaʾ* is supposed to represent.

The absence of the report from the *Muwaṭṭaʾ* cannot be treated as a mathematical disproof. Mālik omitted many reports. Still, this omission has weight because the report is so well suited to Mālik's legal environment. It concerns judicial reasoning. It travels through Medinan legal names. It later becomes central to arguments about ijtihād. If the report had been a settled early Medinan proof, Mālik's silence becomes harder to explain.

The silence also interacts with the route evidence. The missing item is a Medinan legal report whose later canonical form narrows through Yazīd and whose Abū Hurayrah support is tied to Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm and then to an ʿAbd al-Razzāq bottleneck. The report becomes visible in the period when jurists were increasingly formalizing legal method and defending disagreement.

## Why Later Legal Theory Needed the Report

By the late second and early third Islamic centuries, jurists were arguing about more than individual rulings. They were defending method: analogy, solitary reports, disagreement, juristic effort, and the authority of judges when explicit texts did not decide a case. A doctrine that gives reward to the mistaken judge is valuable in exactly that world.

The report allowed jurists to say that qualified effort has religious value even when it fails. It made disagreement less threatening, because the mistaken party could still be treated as rewarded. It also protected the prestige of the legal office. A judge who ruled wrongly after serious effort could remain a pious agent rather than a man who had bound people to error.

The later use confirms the function. Al-Bayhaqī's *al-Sunan al-ṣaghīr* quotes the report and then moves directly into al-Shāfiʿī's legal qualification: if a judge later sees that his ijtihād contradicts the Book, the Sunna, consensus, or an analogy in that meaning, he must return from it.[^9] The report is therefore not sitting in the tradition as a general consolation about mistakes. It is part of a technical discussion about judicial method, legal correction, and the status of ijtihād.

## The Qur'anic Pressure

The Qur'an places heavy moral pressure on judgment. It commands people to return trusts to their owners and to judge with justice. It warns believers not to follow what they do not know. It condemns false attribution to God. These verses do not build a professional doctrine of rewarded legal error; they make proof, justice, and responsibility central.

A defender can answer that the hadith assumes qualified effort rather than careless guessing. This is the strongest charitable reading, and the wording does include ijtihād rather than arbitrary opinion. Even so, the report's moral center is the judge's reward. It says nothing about repair for the person harmed by the wrong ruling. It gives no warning about institutional incentives. It gives no mechanism for preventing a class of jurists from turning their own fallible reasoning into religious authority.

The Qur'anic pressure therefore does not disprove the hadith by itself. It gives the critic the right question: why does a report about legal error locate the blessing in the official's effort rather than in justice for those affected by the ruling?

## The Rijāl Evidence Strengthens the Historical Reading

The rijāl evidence does not give us a villain. It gives us a social map. Yazīd b. al-Hād is a reliable Medinan transmitter and the recoverable bottleneck for the ʿAmr route. Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm is a judge, administrator, and recorder of legal knowledge under ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz. Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd is a Medinan qāḍī and jurist of high standing. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz al-Darāwardī is a Medinan transmitter whose reports are accepted, while the critics also warn that he could err when narrating from other people's books.[^10] ʿAbd al-Razzāq is a major authority, but the Abū Hurayrah line depends on him at the critical preservation point.

This profile is more useful than a simple accusation of fabrication. The report emerges where legal expertise, judicial office, and hadith transmission overlap. A professional doctrine seeking prophetic authorization would be expected to pass through precisely this kind of social location. The transmitters need not be dishonest for the report to reflect the interests of their world. A doctrine can arise from institutional pressure, circulate among sincere jurists, and become sacred through repetition.

<ContextNote title="A better formulation">
The argument should not rest on calling every transmitter corrupt. The stronger argument is that the report's earliest recoverable circulation belongs to Medinan legal authorities, that its internal confirmation already appeals to a Medinan judge, and that its later use solves a problem created by juristic authority itself.
</ContextNote>

## What the Evidence Supports

The judge reward hadith is not best explained as a public prophetic maxim that spread widely from the beginning. Its canonical ʿAmr route narrows through Yazīd b. al-Hād. Its Abū Hurayrah support first appears inside Yazīd's own confirmation story, then survives as a separate route through the same Abū Bakr b. Ḥazm line and through the narrow ʿAbd al-Razzāq transmission from Maʿmar. The old critics themselves preserve warnings about the narrowness of these routes, especially in al-Tirmidhī, al-Bazzār, and the *ʿIlal* material.

The legal setting explains why the report mattered. A growing juristic class had to defend disagreement, human reasoning, and judicial error without surrendering religious authority. The hadith gives them a compact answer: qualified legal effort earns reward whether the ruling succeeds or fails. The people harmed by a wrong judgment remain outside the wording, while the officeholder receives a divine accounting of his effort.

The report deserves suspicion because its wording benefits judges, its confirmation story invokes a judge, and its later route runs through major jurists. Its earliest Medinan legal test, Mālik's *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, does not preserve it, while its later legal use fits the period in which ijtihād needed prophetic support. The report's historical shape points to a legal culture that gave its own uncertainty a sacred reward structure.

<VerdictBox title="Assessment">
The hadith may preserve an old legal sentiment. The extant evidence, however, is too narrow to justify treating it as a broadly transmitted prophetic foundation for ijtihād. Its route map, institutional setting, and later function fit a Medinan juristic doctrine that became prophetic proof once the legal class needed a theology of rewarded error.
</VerdictBox>
