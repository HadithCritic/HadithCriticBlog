<QuoteBlock author="Opponent's Challenge">
  “Show me one Rāfiḍī Shīʿī in the chains of al-Bukhārī.”
</QuoteBlock>

<ClaimBox title="Core Argument">
  The answer is not that every accused narrator was a later Twelver Shīʿī in the developed sectarian sense. The answer is that al-Bukhārī’s corpus and the later Sunni rijāl record contain transmitters who were explicitly described with labels such as Shīʿī, inclined toward Shīʿism, excessive in tashayyuʿ, or even Rāfiḍī. Those labels did not function as automatic exclusion markers when a critic still regarded the narrator as usable, truthful, reliable, or safe under certain conditions.
</ClaimBox>

## What the dispute is really about

The internet version of the argument treats “Sunni” and “Shīʿī” as if they were clean modern membership categories. The early rijāl record does not work that way. A narrator could be Kūfan, pro-ʿAlid, accused of tashayyuʿ, praised for precision, rejected by one critic, accepted by another, and used selectively by a compiler. The labels are real, but they are not always identical in force.

The question, then, is not whether al-Bukhārī had a hidden sectarian filter that removed every controversial transmitter. The question is how a compiler and later critics balanced sectarian accusation against perceived reliability, corroboration, pairing, and the content of the report. The examples below show that the balance was inconsistent, negotiated, and often more pragmatic than later apologetic slogans suggest.

<ContextNote title="Method note">
  A narrator being labeled Shīʿī does not by itself prove that every report he transmitted is fabricated. It proves something narrower and more useful: sectarian accusation was not an absolute barrier inside the Sunni transmission economy. Once that is admitted, the myth of a perfectly sectarian-clean canon becomes much harder to maintain.
</ContextNote>

## 1. ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Aʿyan al-Kūfī

<section class="hc-fig" aria-labelledby="abd-al-malik-title">
  <div>
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">Dossier 01 · Kūfan transmitter</p>
    <h3 id="abd-al-malik-title">ʿAbd al-Malik ibn Aʿyan al-Kūfī <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">عبد الملك بن أعين الكوفي</span></h3>
    <p>
      This case is useful because the labels do not come from one isolated hostile remark. The profile combines trustworthiness language, explicit Shīʿī identification, harsher Rāfiḍī accusations, and the note that al-Bukhārī used him with another narrator rather than as an isolated pillar.
    </p>
    <a href="https://shamela.ws/narrator/4014">Shamela narrator profile 4014</a>
  </div>

  <div class="hc-fig hc-fig--cols">
    <article class="hc-fig__item">
      <span class="hc-fig__kicker">al-Bukhārī / al-Kalābādhī</span>
      <p>Al-Bukhārī is reported to have said that he was Shīʿī. Al-Kalābādhī adds that Muḥammad, meaning al-Bukhārī, only narrated from him when paired with another narrator.</p>
    </article>
    <article class="hc-fig__item">
      <span class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn Ḥajar / al-Dhahabī</span>
      <p>Ibn Ḥajar describes him as truthful and Shīʿī, while al-Dhahabī says he was Shīʿī, truthful, and narrated by the two Shaykhs when paired.</p>
    </article>
    <article class="hc-fig__item">
      <span class="hc-fig__kicker">Abū Ḥātim / Ibn Ḥibbān</span>
      <p>Abū Ḥātim places him among the early Shīʿa while still writing his hadith down. Ibn Ḥibbān says he inclined toward Shīʿism.</p>
    </article>
    <article class="hc-fig__item">
      <span class="hc-fig__kicker">Stronger criticism</span>
      <p>Al-ʿAqīlī and Sufyān b. ʿUyaynah transmit harsher Rāfiḍī language, while Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn and Ibn al-Jārūd are cited with severe reliability criticism.</p>
    </article>
  </div>

  <details class="hc-fig">

      <p><b>Aḥmad b. Ṣāliḥ al-Miṣrī:</b> trustworthy Kūfan; elsewhere, “there is no harm in him.” <i>Ikmāl Tahdhīb al-Kamāl</i> 8/302.</p>

      <p><b>al-ʿAqīlī:</b> Rāfiḍī; al-Thawrī narrated from him and then abandoned him. <i>Ikmāl Tahdhīb al-Kamāl</i> 8/302.</p>

      <p><b>al-Kalābādhī:</b> al-Bukhārī only narrated from him when paired with another narrator, and he was Shīʿī. <i>Ikmāl Tahdhīb al-Kamāl</i> 8/302.</p>
      <p><b>Sufyān b. ʿUyaynah:</b> Shīʿī Rāfiḍī and a man of sectarian opinions; also reported simply as “Rāfiḍī.” <i>Ikmāl Tahdhīb al-Kamāl</i> 8/302; <i>Tahdhīb al-Kamāl</i> 18/282.</p>

## 2. al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUtaybah

<section class="hc-fig" aria-labelledby="hakam-title">
  <div>
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">Dossier 02 · Kūfan jurist</p>
    <h3 id="hakam-title">al-Ḥakam ibn ʿUtaybah <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">الحكم بن عتيبة</span></h3>
    <p>
      Al-Ḥakam is not presented in the sources as a marginal figure. He is a jurist of Kūfa, connected to the school of Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, and treated with respect. That makes the sectarian notices more important, because they occur inside a profile of legal and scholarly authority rather than only inside a dismissal.
    </p>
    <a href="https://shamela.ws/narrator/1580">Shamela narrator profile 1580</a>
  </div>

      <span class="hc-fig__kicker">Abū Isḥāq al-Fazārī</span>

## 3. ʿAdī ibn Thābit al-Anṣārī

<section class="hc-fig" aria-labelledby="adi-title">
  <div>
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">Dossier 03 · strongest public-label case</p>

    <p>
      ʿAdī is the clearest example in this set because the language goes beyond private inclination. He is described as reliable by several critics, while other notices identify him as a Shīʿī preacher, imam of a Shīʿī mosque, extreme Shīʿī, or Rāfiḍī. If the challenge was to find a transmitter in this orbit, this is one of the most direct answers.

## 5. ʿAbbād ibn Yaʿqūb

<section class="hc-fig" aria-labelledby="abbad-title">
  <div>
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">Dossier 05 · severe sectarian profile</p>
    <h3 id="abbad-title">ʿAbbād ibn Yaʿqūb al-Rawājinī <span lang="ar" dir="rtl">عباد بن يعقوب الرواجني</span></h3>
    <p>
      ʿAbbād is the extreme end of the spectrum. Some critics still speak about his transmission with trust language, but his sectarian profile is treated as suspicious, excessive, and polemically severe. This is the sort of case that exposes the gap between “usable in isnād” and “clean ideological pedigree.”
    </p>

## What this proves about al-Bukhārī’s methodology

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--c2" aria-label="What the evidence shows">
  <article class="hc-fig__item">
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">What the evidence shows</p>
    <h3>Sectarian accusation did not erase a transmitter automatically.</h3>
    <p>
      Several profiles combine sectarian criticism with reliability language. That means al-Bukhārī and the broader Sunni critical tradition could accept, pair, limit, or selectively use transmitters whose theological profile was controversial. The canon was not built through the simple exclusion of every transmitter accused of Shīʿism.
    </p>
  </article>
  <article class="hc-fig__item">
    <p class="hc-fig__kicker">What the evidence does not require</p>
    <h3>The argument does not need modern sectarian anachronism.</h3>
    <p>
      The point is not to force every early Kūfan accused of tashayyuʿ into a later sectarian box. The point is that the classical record itself preserves enough accusations to defeat the claim that the Ṣaḥīḥ is untouched by transmitters bearing Shīʿī or Rāfiḍī labels.
    </p>
  </article>
</div>

The larger implication concerns the way modern readers use rijāl. Much of what people know about a narrator comes from later biographical and critical compilations: al-Mizzī, Ibn Ḥajar, al-Dhahabī, Ibn ʿAdī, al-ʿUqaylī, and others. These works organize, preserve, and sometimes intensify earlier evaluations. They also reveal that the neat image of an ideologically purified canon is a retrospective myth, not the messy profile we actually find when the biographies are read closely.

<ContextNote title="Why this matters for hadith apologetics">
  The common defense is that al-Bukhārī’s conditions were so strict that controversial transmitters could not meaningfully enter his collection. The examples above show a different reality. The stricter claim has to become narrower: al-Bukhārī may have judged certain controversial transmitters usable in particular contexts, sometimes with pairing or corroboration. That narrower claim is historically possible, but it is much weaker than the slogan that no Shīʿī or Rāfiḍī-associated transmitter appears in his chains.
</ContextNote>

  The evidence does not merely answer the challenge with one name. It shows that the challenge was built on a false picture of how the Sunni transmission system worked. The rijāl record preserves transmitters who were accused of Shīʿism, tashayyuʿ, or rafḍ while still being treated as usable by some critics and compilers.
