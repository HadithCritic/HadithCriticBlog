## The One Word That Makes the Miracle Work

The green Arabia hadith has become one of the most repeated modern miracle claims in Muslim apologetics. You have probably heard it in a lecture, a dawah video, a TikTok clip, or a list of “scientific prophecies.” The argument is simple. The Prophet allegedly said that Arabia would return to meadows and rivers. Scientists now say Arabia was once green in the distant past. Therefore, Muhammad must have known something no seventh-century Arab could have known. Therefore, Islam is true.

That is the entire argument.

But the argument depends on one word.

Not the whole report. Not the whole Arabic sentence. Not the whole transmission history. One word in English: **reverts**.

If the report says Arabia will **become** meadows and rivers, the claim is weak. Land becoming fertile is not miraculous. Human beings have irrigated dry regions for thousands of years. States have dug canals, moved water, reclaimed land, planted estates, and turned barren territory into agricultural zones. Nothing about that requires supernatural knowledge.

But if the report says Arabia will **revert** to meadows and rivers, the claim suddenly changes. Now the apologist can say the Prophet was not merely predicting future agriculture. He was allegedly predicting a return to a previous climatic state. That is where the paleoclimate argument enters. That is where the miracle is manufactured.

So before anything else, keep the issue clear. The modern miracle claim does not merely require the hadith to exist. It requires the hadith to mean “return to a former green state.” If that meaning collapses, the miracle collapses with it.

<ClaimBox>
The apologetic claim is not built on the existence of the hadith alone. It is built on a specific translation choice, a specific modern interpretation, and a specific assumption that the Arabic must refer to ancient green Arabia.
</ClaimBox>

The problem is that all three are unstable.

The transmission is unstable. The historical setting is suspicious. The classical interpretation does not support the modern miracle. And the English translation used by the most popular hadith website was changed at exactly the point where the dawah argument needed it changed.

That is what makes this case important. It is not only a weak prophecy claim. It is a case study in how miracle claims are built. A text is isolated from its transmission history. A late political context is erased. A flexible Arabic word is pushed into the one English meaning that helps the argument. Then the whole thing is presented to ordinary Muslims as if it has always been obvious.

It was not obvious. It was made obvious after the fact.

## The Hadith as It Is Used Today

The report is usually cited from Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. In the current Sunnah.com rendering of Muslim 157b, the key line reads:

<div class="b73-quote-card">
  <div class="b73-quote-content">
    “The Last Hour will not come before wealth becomes abundant and overflowing, so much so that a man takes Zakat out of his property and cannot find anyone to accept it from him, and till the land of Arabia <strong>reverts</strong> to meadows and rivers.”
  </div>
  <div class="b73-quote-source">Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 157b, current Sunnah.com translation</div>
</div>

That wording is perfect for the miracle claim. The English reader sees “reverts,” then immediately thinks of a previous state. The report appears to say that Arabia was once meadows and rivers, then became desert, then will go back to its earlier condition. Modern paleoclimate research is then brought in as confirmation.

But this is not how evidence works.

You do not begin with a desired miracle and then force the wording to carry it. You ask what the report actually says, where it comes from, who transmits it, whether the wording is stable, whether earlier readers understood it the same way, and whether the historical setting gives us a better explanation for why such a report would appear.

Once those questions are asked, the miracle begins to fall apart.

The Arabic word at the center of the dispute is **taʿūd**. It can mean “return.” It can also be used in the sense of “become.” This is not a hidden anti-Islamic trick. It is a known feature of the word. Classical Arabic lexicons allow both senses. The question is not whether “reverts” is grammatically possible in the abstract. The question is whether this specific hadith, in this specific context, should be turned into a paleoclimate prophecy.

And there is no good reason to do that.

The report does not mention ancient Arabia. It does not mention a former climate. It does not say, “as it used to be.” It does not say, “as it was before.” It gives a future eschatological statement in a cluster of other end-times motifs, especially overflowing wealth. The modern reading imports the ancient-green-Arabia idea into the text after scientific research made that idea useful.

That is not prophecy. That is retrofitting.

## The First Problem: The Report Comes Through One Man

Before we even get to Sunnah.com, the transmission itself is already a problem.

The green Arabia motif converges on one transmitter: **Suhayl ibn Abī Ṣāliḥ Dhakwān al-Sammān**, who died around the middle of the second Islamic century. The basic chain runs:

**Suhayl ← his father Abū Ṣāliḥ ← Abū Hurayrah ← the Prophet**

There is no strong independent route that reaches Abū Ṣāliḥ or Abū Hurayrah without first passing through Suhayl. That matters. In isnād-cum-matn analysis, when multiple later chains all converge on a single earlier transmitter, that transmitter becomes the common link. He is the earliest recoverable point at which the report can be historically located. Sometimes that means he composed it. Sometimes it means he was the decisive proliferator. Either way, the report cannot be securely pushed behind him without independent corroboration.

This is why the common-link point matters so much. The miracle claim wants the report to go back to the Prophet in the seventh century. The transmission profile points instead to Suhayl in the eighth century.

The later compilations preserve several versions of the report. They are not all identical. They do not look like a fixed prophetic sentence preserved with perfect precision. They look like a motif moving through a small set of transmitters and being attached to related eschatological material.

The main branches are these.

### Branch One: Yaʿqūb ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān

Through Yaʿqūb ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, the report appears in Muslim 157c, Aḥmad 9395, and Ibn Ḥibbān 6700. These versions already show meaningful variation. Ibn Ḥibbān preserves a shorter form with the greening clause and the spread of killing or chaos. Muslim and Aḥmad preserve longer forms that include overflowing wealth.

That matters because it suggests the greening statement did not necessarily begin as part of one stable prophetic paragraph. It circulated in connection with other end-times motifs and was expanded or embedded differently in later transmission.

### Branch Two: Ismāʿīl ibn Zakariyyā

Through Ismāʿīl ibn Zakariyyā, Aḥmad 8833 gives another form. This version opens with the greening clause and then adds a security prophecy about safe travel between Iraq and Mecca. That addition is absent from the first branch.

This is not a small detail. Iraq and Mecca were not neutral places in the early Islamic political imagination. The pilgrimage routes, the security of travel, and the control of movement between Iraq and the Ḥijāz were all politically meaningful. A version that localizes the report around safe travel from Iraq to Mecca looks like a report being made useful for a particular audience.

### Branch Three: Sufyān

The most interesting version is preserved by al-Ḥākim. It contains the bare greening statement, but with a hesitation over whether the statement was actually raised to the Prophet:

<div class="b73-quote-card">
  <div class="b73-quote-content">
    <HadithBlock>لا أعلم إلا قد رفعه</HadithBlock>
    “I do not know except that he raised it.”
  </div>
</div>

That is not confidence. That is uncertainty. A transmitter is admitting that the prophetic attribution is not fully secure in his mind. For a miracle claim, this is devastating. The whole argument requires the statement to be prophetic. Yet within the transmission history itself, we find doubt over whether the statement was even marfūʿ.

So the first problem is not the translation. The first problem is the report.

It comes through one key transmitter. Its branches vary. One branch adds a politically meaningful travel motif. Another preserves hesitation over prophetic attribution. That is not the profile of a secure, widely known prophecy from the Prophet. It is the profile of a late report whose earliest recoverable layer sits in the second Islamic century.

## Suhayl ibn Abī Ṣāliḥ Is Not a Solid Foundation

Suhayl was not an unknown man. He was a Medinan transmitter and the son of Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān. But the fact that a man appears in the books of hadith does not mean every report through him becomes historically secure. This is exactly where traditional hadith discourse often misleads ordinary Muslims. It gives them a grade, then asks them to stop asking historical questions.

But the grade is not evidence that the Prophet said it. It is only a later scholarly judgment about the acceptability of a chain.

Classical rijāl critics themselves did not treat Suhayl as flawless. Reports about him include remarks about weakness, memory issues, and limited probative value. Ibn Maʿīn is reported to have said that he was not like others. Al-Nasāʾī said his hadith was not proof. Abū Ḥātim said his hadith may be written down, but not used as proof. Al-Dhahabī notes that he may have been afflicted by forgetfulness in some of his hadiths.

This does not prove that Suhayl invented every report he transmitted. That would be lazy. The point is more precise. If a report depends entirely on Suhayl, lacks independent corroboration, varies in later branches, and carries obvious political usefulness in his setting, then Suhayl becomes the historical problem. He is not a bridge that safely carries the report back to the Prophet. He is the point where the report becomes visible.

That is a major difference.

A traditional apologist will say: Suhayl is in the chain, Muslim included it, therefore the report is authentic.

A historical critic asks: why does the report only become visible through Suhayl, why does it vary after him, why is its content useful in his period, and why do we not have independent routes around him?

Those are not the same questions. And they do not produce the same answer.

## The Umayyad Setting Explains the Report Better Than a Miracle Does

The strongest explanation for the report is not paleoclimate. It is politics.

The report becomes visible in the period when Umayyad elites were deeply invested in land reclamation, irrigation, agriculture, and the transformation of uncultivated land into productive estates. This was not merely private farming. It was state policy, elite wealth, taxation, legitimacy, and control.

The reign of Hishām ibn ʿAbd al-Malik, from 724 to 743 CE, is especially important. His period is associated with major investment in agriculture, water channels, plantations, estates, and reclamation projects. The point was not only to make land fertile. It was to turn land into revenue and to tie elite power to the visible revival of the landscape.

The Ḥijāz was not untouched by this. Local elites could lose land through confiscation, pressure, purchase, distress sale, or Umayyad encroachment. Agricultural expansion was not simply a beautiful story about dead land coming back to life. It was also a story about power. Who controlled the water? Who owned the land? Who received tax exemptions? Who benefited from reclamation? Who had to watch old property relations disappear?

A. Asa Eger’s work on Umayyad agricultural landscapes shows that elite development of abandoned or non-agricultural lands was part of a larger administrative and fiscal program. Irrigation did not happen in a vacuum. It was tied to ownership, taxation, and the interests of the ruling class.

Now read the hadith again in that setting.

Arabia will become meadows and rivers.

That is not a random end-times image. It is exactly the kind of statement that could sacralize an imperial project. If the state is reclaiming land, digging water channels, creating estates, and presenting agricultural revival as a sign of blessing, then a hadith saying Arabia will become meadows and rivers is politically useful. It tells people that what they are seeing is not merely Umayyad expansion. It is destiny. It is prophecy. It is the movement of history toward the Hour.

That is how policy becomes prophecy.

<ContextNote>
The issue is not that Suhayl’s political context proves personal intent. We do not need to pretend we can read his mind. The point is that the report first becomes historically visible in a setting where its content fits the needs of power remarkably well.
</ContextNote>

This is the kind of thing hadith criticism must take seriously. A report does not float above history. It enters the record somewhere. It serves someone. It answers a concern. It makes a claim that is useful in one context rather than another. When a report appears at exactly the time when the ruling order needed religious language for agricultural transformation, that cannot be ignored.

## Suhayl’s Social World Matters

Suhayl was a Medinan mawla connected to Ghatafān. Ghatafān belonged to the wider Qays tribal alignment, and the Qays were favored under Hishām after earlier balances in Umayyad tribal politics shifted. Khalid Yahya Blankinship discusses the importance of these northern Arab alignments in late Umayyad administration and military life.

Again, this does not mean we can write a cartoon version of history where Suhayl wakes up and says, “Today I will fabricate a hadith for the Umayyads.” That is not serious argumentation. The point is subtler and stronger. Suhayl belongs to the social world in which a report like this makes sense. He is not a neutral voice dropped from the sky. He is a transmitter living under Umayyad power, in Medina, during the period when land, water, tribal patronage, and political legitimacy were all entangled.

A report about Arabia becoming meadows and rivers would not be harmless in that world. It could help reinterpret disruptive policy as divine order. It could turn confiscation and elite estate-building into eschatological fulfillment. It could tell people that resistance to the transformation of the land was resistance to what the Prophet had already foretold.

That is why the historical context matters more than the modern miracle claim.

The modern apologist wants you to imagine seventh-century Arabia and twenty-first-century paleoclimate research. The transmission points you instead to eighth-century Medina and Umayyad land politics.

That is a completely different story.

## The Classical Commentaries Do Not Read It as a Paleoclimate Miracle

Even if someone accepts the hadith as authentic, the modern miracle interpretation still fails because earlier Muslim scholars did not read it this way.

They knew Arabic. They knew the word **taʿūd**. They had no anti-Islamic reason to avoid a miracle reading. If the report clearly meant that Arabia would return to a prehistoric green condition, they could have said that. But that is not how they explained it.

Al-Nawawī explains the report as a future social condition connected to war, depopulation, neglect, and the abandonment of cultivation. In his reading, the land becomes overgrown or changes because people abandon it and stop working it properly.

<div class="b73-quote-card">
  <div class="b73-quote-content">
    <HadithBlock>
    معناه والله أعلم أنهم يتركونها ويعرضون عنها فتبقى مهملة لا تزرع ولا تسقى من مياهها وذلك لقلة الرجال وكثرة الحروب
    </HadithBlock>
    “Its meaning, and God knows best, is that they will leave it and turn away from it, so it will remain neglected, neither cultivated nor irrigated from its waters. That will be due to the scarcity of men and the abundance of wars.”
  </div>
  <div class="b73-quote-source">Al-Nawawī, <em>Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim</em></div>
</div>

That is not paleoclimate. That is social breakdown.

Al-Qurṭubī gives another explanation. He says the Arabs will turn away from their old habits of movement, raiding, and war, and will instead occupy themselves with planting the land, cultivating it, and channeling its waters.

<div class="b73-quote-card">
  <div class="b73-quote-content">
    <HadithBlock>
    تنصرف دواعي العرب عن مقتضى عادتهم من انتجاع الغيث والارتحال عن المواطن للحروب والغارات … فيشتغلوا بغراسة الأرض وعمارتها وإجراء مياهها
    </HadithBlock>
    “The motives of the Arabs will turn away from what their custom required of seeking rain, moving from place to place, wars, and raids … so they will occupy themselves with planting the land, cultivating it, and channeling its waters.”
  </div>
  <div class="b73-quote-source">Al-Qurṭubī</div>
</div>

That is not paleoclimate either. That is human agriculture.

Even Ibn ʿUthaymīn, a modern Sunni scholar, reads the hadith in terms of contemporary agricultural development, wells, farms, and water. He does not need the report to mean ancient green Arabia. He treats it as a statement about Arabia becoming cultivated and watered.

So ask the obvious question.

If the hadith was always a clear scientific prophecy about ancient green Arabia, why did the major commentators not explain it that way? Why did they explain it through depopulation, war, cultivation, irrigation, and human activity?

The answer is simple. The paleoclimate miracle is not the classical reading. It is a modern apologetic reading placed onto the text after the scientific discovery became useful.

## Then Sunnah.com Changed the Word

Now we reach the modern part of the story.

For English readers, the most important translation of Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim has long been Abdul Hamid Siddiqui’s. His translation of this hadith, preserved on QuranX, says:

<div class="b73-quote-card">
  <div class="b73-quote-content">
    “… till the land of Arabia <strong>becomes</strong> meadows and rivers.”
  </div>
  <div class="b73-quote-source">Siddiqui Translation</div>
</div>

Not “reverts.” Not “returns.” **Becomes.**

That is a problem for the dawah claim. If Arabia merely “becomes” meadows and rivers, the report can easily refer to future agricultural development. It does not necessarily imply a return to ancient conditions. But if it “reverts,” then the reader is pushed toward the paleoclimate argument.

This is where the timing becomes suspicious.

In 2019, IERA published *Forbidden Prophecies* by Abu Zakariya. The book used the green Arabia hadith as evidence for Muhammad’s prophethood and leaned heavily on the “return” reading. It argued that the Prophet knew Arabia had once been green.

The book was announced in late 2019 and promoted in early 2020. By April 2020, the free PDF was being shared online. People reading the book could check the hadith on Sunnah.com. And when they checked it, the site still said “becomes.”

Then the wording changed.

First the book promotes the miracle. Then the PDF spreads. Then people can verify the report on Sunnah.com and see that the translation does not support the miracle as strongly as the book needs. Then, within the same window of online circulation, the site changes “becomes” to “reverts.”

Only the crucial word changes. Not the whole translation. Not the translator. Not a new explanatory note. Just the one word that makes the miracle claim work.

That is not how a transparent academic resource behaves.

If Sunnah.com believed “reverts” was the better translation, it could have said so. It could have added a note. It could have said the Siddiqui translation has been modified. It could have identified the editor. It could have explained the lexical reason for preferring “reverts” over “becomes.” Instead, the site presents the changed wording as if it were simply the translation.

That matters because Sunnah.com is not a random blog. For English-speaking Muslims, it functions as the default hadith lookup site. When it changes a word like this, ordinary readers will assume they are seeing the standard translation. They will not know that the older published translation used “becomes.” They will not know that the change helps a specific dawah argument. They will not know that the classical commentaries do not support the paleoclimate reading.

They will just see “reverts” and think the miracle is obvious.

That is the danger.

## The Word “Taʿūd” Cannot Carry the Whole Argument

A defender may respond by saying that **taʿūd** can mean “return.” That is true. But it is not enough.

A word having a possible meaning does not prove that this is the intended meaning in a specific report. Arabic words, like words in any language, take meaning from usage and context. You cannot take one possible sense from a lexicon, ignore the transmission history, ignore the commentaries, ignore the political setting, and then build a miracle out of it.

Yes, **taʿūd** can mean “return.” It can also function in the sense of becoming or turning into a condition. The question is which reading is justified here. Classical commentary points toward future social or agricultural transformation. The political context points toward Umayyad land reclamation and irrigation. The transmission points to a second-century common link. None of that points to a seventh-century paleoclimate prophecy.

This is how miracle claims cheat. They use maximum flexibility when building the claim, then pretend the final interpretation is the only possible meaning.

When the Arabic is useful, they say it is precise.

When the transmission is challenged, they hide behind later grading.

When classical scholars disagree with the modern reading, they ignore them.

When the English translation weakens the claim, they change it.

That is not evidence. That is apologetic editing.

## Hadith Wording Was Never Preserved Like the Quran

There is another problem. The argument assumes that the wording of the hadith is stable enough to hang a scientific miracle on one verb. But Muslim hadith scholars themselves never claimed that hadith wording was preserved with Quranic precision.

Hadith were often narrated by meaning. This is called **riwāya bil-maʿnā**. Many scholars allowed transmitters to report the meaning of a hadith in their own wording if they believed they understood it. That means the wording of a report can shift while the general idea remains.

This matters because scientific miracle claims require exact wording. They need the Prophet to have said the precise term that later becomes useful. But hadith transmission often does not give us that kind of precision.

A simple example shows the problem. There is a report about a man who wanted to marry but had no dowry. Across different narrations, the Prophet’s statement is transmitted with very different Arabic verbs:

  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Ownership / transfer</div>

  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Muslim 1425b</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell arabic">أَنْكَحْتُكَهَا</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">I wed you to her</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Marriage contract</div>

  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Al-Bayhaqī 13823</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell arabic">وَهَبْتُكَهَا</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">I gifted her to you</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Gift language</div>

  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Al-Bayhaqī 14359</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell arabic">خُذْهَا</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Take her</div>
  <div class="b73-wording-cell">Command</div>
</div>

These are not tiny spelling variants. They are different verbs with different legal and conceptual shades. The same story is carried with different wording.

So when someone builds a miracle claim on **taʿūd**, we have to ask: are you sure this was the exact prophetic wording? Are you sure it was not the wording of Suhayl? Are you sure it was not a paraphrase? Are you sure it was not shaped by later transmission? Are you sure the report was even marfūʿ when one route preserves hesitation over that very point?

The honest answer is no.

And if the answer is no, then you cannot build a scientific miracle on that verb.

## The Pattern Is Bigger Than This One Hadith

This case matters because it shows a broader pattern in modern Islamic miracle claims.

First, a vague or flexible text is selected.
Second, a modern discovery is placed next to it.
Third, the wording is translated in the strongest possible way.
Fourth, the classical interpretive record is ignored.
Fifth, the transmission history is treated as settled because a later collection included the report.
Sixth, ordinary Muslims are told that the miracle was sitting there the whole time.

But it was not sitting there the whole time.

It had to be constructed.

The green Arabia claim is especially revealing because we can watch the construction happen. We can see the difference between “becomes” and “reverts.” We can see the dawah book requiring the stronger reading. We can see Sunnah.com changing the word in the exact period when the claim was being promoted. We can see the classical commentaries failing to support the modern reading. We can see the isnād converging on one second-century transmitter.

This is not how strong evidence behaves. Strong evidence does not need this much assistance.

If the prophecy were clear, no one would need to quietly adjust the translation. If the report were widely transmitted, it would not depend on Suhayl. If the wording were exact, narration by meaning would not be a problem. If the interpretation were classical, the commentators would have said it. If the claim were honest, the change from “becomes” to “reverts” would have been disclosed.

But none of that happened.

<VerdictBox title="Final Verdict">
The green Arabia hadith should not be used as proof of prophethood.

At the transmission level, it does not give us a secure seventh-century prophecy. It converges on Suhayl ibn Abī Ṣāliḥ, a second-century transmitter with contested reliability and no independent corroborating route for this motif. The variants show movement and expansion, not a fixed prophetic sentence. One route even preserves uncertainty over whether the statement was raised to the Prophet at all.

At the historical level, the report fits the Umayyad world better than the Prophet’s world. It makes sense in a period of land reclamation, irrigation, elite estates, taxation, and the religious legitimation of agricultural revival. In that context, “Arabia becoming meadows and rivers” is not a miracle about ancient climate. It is a useful political slogan placed into prophetic form.

At the interpretive level, the classical commentaries do not support the modern paleoclimate reading. Al-Nawawī explains the report through war, abandonment, and neglect. Al-Qurṭubī explains it through cultivation and irrigation. Ibn ʿUthaymīn reads it through modern agriculture and water access. None of them present it as a proof that the Prophet knew Arabia was green in the distant past.

At the modern apologetic level, the case becomes even worse. Siddiqui’s translation said “becomes.” Sunnah.com later changed the key word to “reverts,” the one word the miracle claim needed. The change happened after *Forbidden Prophecies* began promoting the report as a major proof and during the period when the book was circulating online.

It proves something else: when a tradition needs a miracle badly enough, it can turn policy into prophecy, ambiguity into precision, and a changed translation into divine proof.
</VerdictBox>
