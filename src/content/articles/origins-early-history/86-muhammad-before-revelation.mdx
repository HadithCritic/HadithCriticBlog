<ClaimBox title="Abstract">The earliest surviving Islamic sources preserve a picture of Muhammad's pre-prophetic life that is substantially more complicated than the traditional account of a lifelong Abrahamic monotheist. He is described as having spent forty years upon the affairs of his people, as having borne sins from the Jahiliyya, and, in one early exegetical report, as having been involved in shirk. The subsequent development of tafsir shows an increasingly elaborate effort to distinguish his association with Quraysh's religious practices from their idolatrous beliefs and to establish that he had always been a monotheist.</ClaimBox>

The historical possibility that Muhammad participated in the religious life of pre-Islamic Mecca, before God guided him through revelation, deserves serious consideration. A further line of evidence comes from hadith and sīrah reports that call Muhammad *al-ṣābiʾ*, while early Arabic lexicographers explain the designation as one who has left one religion for another and explicitly connect its use to Quraysh's description of Muhammad and his followers. These reports do not, by themselves, prove a particular act of idol worship; they do, however, provide an important social and linguistic context for the exegetical reports that associate his earlier condition with the religion of his people and, in al-Ḍaḥḥāk's interpretation, with shirk.

## The Problem with the Traditional Narrative

The conventional Islamic account portrays Muhammad as someone who was religiously exceptional even before receiving revelation.

Although raised in a predominantly polytheistic society, he is traditionally understood to have rejected the worship of idols, avoided the religious practices of Quraysh, and followed the remnants of the religion of Abraham that had scarcely passed down to his time. His visits to the cave of Hira are consequently presented as the continuation of an already established monotheistic disposition.

What do the earliest surviving Islamic interpretations actually say about Muhammad's religious condition before revelation? Did early exegetes understand him to have been a Hanif, consciously following the religion of Abraham? Or did some of them understand his pre-prophetic existence as a period in which he belonged to the religious world of his people, a world from which God subsequently guided him?

The early exegetical tradition makes the discussion considerably more interesting by preserving several descriptions of his pre-prophetic condition, some of which sit uneasily alongside the later theological insistence that he had never departed from monotheism.

## 93:7: God Found Muhammad Astray

The starting point is 93:7.

<QuranVerse verse="93:7" highlightEnglish={["astray"]} highlightArabic={["ضَآلًّا"]} />

The verse describes an earlier condition followed by an act of divine guidance. The meaning of *dalal*, however, became the subject of substantial exegetical discussion. The traditional explanation generally associates the word with Muhammad's lack of knowledge concerning revelation, the Qur'an, and the laws subsequently communicated to him.

That interpretation is certainly present in early sources. But it is not the only one.

### Muqatil ibn Sulayman

Muqatil ibn Sulayman (d. 150 AH), one of the earliest surviving Qur'anic commentators, interprets the verse:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Muqatil ibn Sulayman, Tafsir, 4:732, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَوَجَدَكَ ضَالًّا عَنِ الدَّلَالَةِ فَهَدَى ... فَهَدَاكَ لِدِينِهِ</p>
  <p>“He found you astray from guidance, and guided ... He guided you to His religion.”</p>
</div>

The interpretation associates Muhammad's earlier condition with a lack of guidance and describes his subsequent movement *towards* God's religion.

Muqatil does not explicitly identify this condition as idolatry, nevertheless, his interpretation does not describe Muhammad as already possessing the complete religious guidance subsequently associated with his prophetic mission.

### Al-Tabari and the forty years upon the affairs of Quraysh

In his commentary on 93:7, al-Tabari first writes:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:489, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَوَجَدَكَ عَلَى غَيْرِ الَّذِي أَنْتَ عَلَيْهِ الْيَوْمَ</p>
  <p>“He found you upon something <strong>other</strong> than that which you are upon today.”</p>
</div>

He then transmits the following interpretation attributed to al-Suddi:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Suddi, in al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:489</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">كَانَ عَلَى أَمْرِ قَوْمِهِ أَرْبَعِينَ عَامًا</p>
  <p>“He was upon the affair of his people for forty years.”</p>
</div>

Muhammad is not merely described as having lived among an idolatrous community, but the wording associates his own forty-year pre-prophetic life with the affairs of that community, with the context of the verse suggesting specifically his religious affair.

The report creates an uncomfortable question for the later reconstruction of Muhammad as someone who had always maintained a separate, consciously Abrahamic religious identity. Why describe his entire pre-prophetic existence as being upon the affairs of his people? What exactly were those affairs? The context was a society in which the worship of al-Lat, al-Uzza, Hubal and other deities formed part of its religious life.

### Al-Zajjaj independently records the interpretation

Al-Zajjaj (d. 311 AH), in *Ma'ani al-Qur'an wa-I'rabuh*, also records the interpretation:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Zajjaj, Ma'ani al-Qur'an wa-I'rabuh, 5:339–340, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَقَالَ قَوْمٌ: كَانَ عَلَى أَمْرِ قَوْمِهِ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً</p>
  <p>“Some people said: He was upon the affair of his people for forty years.”</p>
</div>

Al-Zajjaj personally prefers a different interpretation. Nevertheless, he preserves the alternative. This matters because the interpretation was not confined to one isolated passage in al-Tabari. It appears in another early exegetical work, albeit without a named attribution. The early exegetical landscape therefore preserved an interpretation in which Muhammad's pre-prophetic existence was associated with the affairs of his community.

### The development of the interpretation

Al-Tha'labi (d. 427 AH) preserves al-Suddi's statement using the same expression, *amr qawmihi*. Al-Wahidi (d. 468 AH) also preserves it.

However, by the time of al-Razi (d. 606 AH), the statement is presented as:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Razi, Mafatih al-Ghayb, 31:197–199, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">كَانَ عَلَى دِينِ قَوْمِهِ أَرْبَعِينَ سَنَةً</p>
  <p>“He was upon the religion of his people for forty years.”</p>
</div>

The original report in al-Tabari uses *amr*, whereas al-Razi gives *din*. The later wording is more explicitly religious, and its circulation illustrates how the report could be interpreted and reformulated.

Al-Razi also records an interpretation attributed to al-Kalbi:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Kalbi, in al-Razi, Mafatih al-Ghayb, 31:197–199</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَجَدَكَ ضَالًّا يَعْنِي كَافِرًا فِي قَوْمٍ ضُلَّالٍ فَهَدَاكَ لِلتَّوْحِيدِ</p>
  <p>“He found you astray, meaning an unbeliever among a misguided people, and guided you to monotheism.”</p>
</div>

This is an even more direct interpretation. The verse is being understood as a transition from an earlier state of unbelief to monotheism.

Al-Tha'labi's earlier version of al-Kalbi's interpretation says:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Tha'labi, Al-Kashf wa al-Bayan, 29:488–490, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَجَدَكَ فِي قَوْمٍ ضُلَّالٍ فَهَدَاكَ لِلتَّوْحِيدِ وَالنُّبُوَّةِ</p>
  <p>“He found you among a misguided people and guided you to monotheism and prophethood.”</p>
</div>

It does not contain the word *kafiran*. The more explicit unbeliever formulation first appears in al-Wahidi's *al-Tafsir al-Basit*. Thus, the wording attributed to al-Kalbi became more explicit in the later transmission.

## 94:2: The Burden of the Jahiliyya

The next important passage is Qur'an 94:2:

<QuranVerse verse="94:2" highlightEnglish={["load"]} highlightArabic={["وِزْرَكَ"]} />

The word *wizr* became the subject of several interpretations. Some exegetes understood it as the weight of prophethood, the distress associated with revelation, or the burden of Muhammad's mission. Others understood it as sin. The second interpretation is particularly significant when examining his pre-prophetic religious life.

### Mujahid and Qatada

Al-Tabari transmits an interpretation from Mujahid:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Mujahid, in al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:492–493</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">ذَنْبَكَ</p>
  <p>“Your sin.”</p>
</div>

He also preserves two transmissions from Qatada, both containing the following statement:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Qatada, in al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:492–493</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">كَانَتْ لِلنَّبِيِّ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ ذُنُوبٌ قَدْ أَثْقَلَتْهُ فَغَفَرَهَا اللَّهُ لَهُ</p>
  <p>“The Prophet had sins which had weighed heavily upon him, and God forgave them for him.”</p>
</div>

These reports are important because they understand the burden as something arising from Muhammad's own actions. They do not identify those sins specifically as idolatry, nevertheless, the interpretation of the passage is not simply that God removed the difficulties of prophethood. It is that He forgave sins that had burdened Muhammad.

### Ibn Zayd: A sin before prophethood

Al-Tabari also transmits a report from Ibn Zayd through Yunus ibn Abd al-A'la and Ibn Wahb:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn Zayd, in al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:492–493</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَغَفَرَ لَهُ ذَنْبَهُ الَّذِي كَانَ قَبْلَ أَنْ يُنَبَّأَ</p>
  <p>“And He forgave him his sin which occurred before he was made a prophet.”</p>
</div>

This is especially important because the chronological qualification is explicit. The sin is situated before prophethood. The report does not require us to infer the timing from other material. It says so directly.

### Muqatil on Qur'an 48:2

Another particularly interesting interpretation appears in Muqatil's commentary on 48:2:

<QuranVerse verse="48:2" highlightEnglish={["your past sins"]} highlightArabic={["مَا تَقَدَّمَ مِن ذَنۢبِكَ"]} />

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Muqatil ibn Sulayman, Tafsir, 4:66, on 48:2</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">مَا تَقَدَّمَ مِنْ ذَنْبِكَ يَعْنِي مَا كَانَ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ</p>
  <p>“What preceded of your sin means what occurred in the Jahiliyya.”</p>
</div>

Muqatil subsequently identifies what followed as what occurred after prophethood. An early exegete understood the verse concerning the forgiveness of Muhammad's sins as referring to his pre-Islamic life and the period following his prophetic mission. Al-Shafi'i also has an interpretation of the preceding sin in 48:2 as occurring before revelation, preserved in al-Bayhaqi's compilation of his Qur'anic commentary.

This establishes that early commentators had no uniform requirement to interpret every reference to Muhammad's sin as metaphorical, communal, or related exclusively to the responsibilities of prophethood.

## The Most Explicit Report: Al-Dahhak and Shirk

Perhaps the most important passage in this investigation appears in al-Tabari's commentary on Qur'an 94:2. After transmitting the reports of Mujahid and Qatada, al-Tabari records:

<HadithBlock
  label={"Al-Dahhak on 94:2, in al-Tabari"}
  source={"Al-Tabari, Jami' al-Bayan, 24:492–493"}
  arabic={"حُدِّثْتُ عَنِ الْحُسَيْنِ، قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ أَبَا مُعَاذٍ يَقُولُ: ثَنَا عُبَيْدٌ، قَالَ: سَمِعْتُ الضَّحَّاكَ يَقُولُ فِي قَوْلِ اللَّهِ: وَوَضَعْنَا عَنْكَ وِزْرَكَ: يَعْنِي الشِّرْكَ الَّذِي كَانَ فِيهِ"}
  translation={"I was informed from al-Husayn, who said: I heard Abu Mu'adh say: Ubayd related to us, saying: I heard al-Dahhak say concerning God's words, 'And We removed from you your burden': He means the shirk which he was in."}
  highlightArabic={["يَعْنِي الشِّرْكَ الَّذِي كَانَ فِيهِ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["He means the shirk which he was in."]}
/>

This is one of the most explicit attributions of pre-prophetic shirk identified in the tafsir literature. The wording is remarkable. The interpretation does not say that Muhammad merely lived among polytheists, was ignorant of the revealed laws, or had participated in some harmless social customs. It identifies the burden with *al-shirk alladhi kana fihi* — the shirk in which he was. If taken literally, this is a direct attribution of a former state of polytheism to Muhammad. The later *Mawsu'at al-Tafsir al-Ma'thur* reproduces the report from al-Tabari, while Bint al-Shati' quotes it in her commentary.

The report remains important as evidence that an exegetical interpretation explicitly associating Muhammad's earlier condition with shirk existed within the early tafsir tradition.

The question is why this particular interpretation became so marginal in subsequent commentary.

## The Religion of His People: How Was the Evidence Reinterpreted?

One of the most interesting developments in the material is the appearance of expressions that explicitly associate Muhammad with the religious ways of his people. These expressions are particularly revealing because some commentators acknowledge that Muhammad participated in aspects of his community's religious life while simultaneously insisting that he never worshipped idols.

### Al-Wahidi: Following the ways of Quraysh

Al-Wahidi, commenting on 94:2 in *al-Tafsir al-Basit*, provides the following statement:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Wahidi, Al-Tafsir al-Basit, 24:132–133, on 94:2</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">لِأَنَّهُ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ كَانَ فِي كَثِيرٍ مِنْ مَذَاهِبِهِ عَلَى مَذَاهِبِ قَوْمِهِ، وَإِنْ لَمْ يَكُنْ عَبَدَ صَنَمًا وَلَا وَثَنًا</p>
  <p>“Because he was, in many of his ways, upon the ways of his people, although he did not worship an idol or an image.”</p>
</div>

Al-Wahidi acknowledges that Muhammad was upon many of the ways of his community. However, he explicitly excludes idol worship. The two claims are presented together: conformity with the people's ways, but without worshipping their idols. Why was this qualification necessary?

The wording suggests that the relationship between Muhammad and the religious practices of Quraysh required explanation. It was possible to associate him with many of their customs while establishing an exception concerning their central cultic practices.

This is an important development in the traditional narrative. Rather than denying all association with the religious life of Mecca, the interpretation distinguishes acceptable forms of participation from the practices that would constitute shirk.

### Al-Sam'ani: The religion of his people

Al-Sam'ani (d. 489 AH) provides an even more direct example in his commentary on 94:2.

He asks:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Sam'ani, Tafsir al-Qur'an, 6:249, on 94:2</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَهَلْ كَانَ عَلَى دِينِ قَوْمِهِ قَبْلَ النُّبُوَّةِ أَوْ لَا؟</p>
  <p>“Was he upon the religion of his people before prophethood or not?”</p>
</div>

He then answers:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Sam'ani, Tafsir al-Qur'an, 6:249</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قَدْ وَرَدَ فِي التَّفْسِيرِ: أَنَّهُ كَانَ عَلَى دِينِ قَوْمِهِ قَبْلَ ذَلِكَ</p>
  <p>“It has come in the tafsir that he was upon the religion of his people before that.”</p>
</div>

This is a significant statement. Al-Sam'ani does not merely discuss whether Muhammad attended public gatherings. He explicitly introduces the question of whether he was upon the religion of his people.

He then qualifies its meaning:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Sam'ani, Tafsir al-Qur'an, 6:249</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَمَعْنَى ذَلِكَ: أَنَّهُ كَانَ يَشْهَدُ مَشَاهِدَهُمْ، وَيُوَافِقُهُمْ فِي بَعْضِ أُمُورِهِمْ مِنْ غَيْرِ أَنْ يَعْبُدَ صَنَمًا أَوْ يُعَظِّمَ وَثَنًا، وَقَدْ كَانَ اللَّهُ عَصَمَهُ عَنْ ذَلِكَ</p>
  <p>“The meaning of that is that he used to attend their gatherings and agree with them in some of their affairs, without worshipping an idol or venerating an image. God had protected him from that.”</p>
</div>

Al-Sam'ani preserves the *din qawmihi* formulation but immediately restricts its meaning. The question is no longer simply whether Muhammad was associated with his people's religion. The question becomes which parts of their religion he could have shared without compromising the later doctrine of prophetic protection from idolatry.

### Ibn Atiyya: Attending gatherings and eating sacrifices

Ibn Atiyya (d. 542 AH), in *al-Muharrar al-Wajiz*, provides another particularly interesting discussion.

He records an interpretation in which Muhammad's pre-prophetic burden consisted of:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn Atiyya, Al-Muharrar al-Wajiz, 5:496–497, on 94:2</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">صُحْبَةُ قَوْمِهِ وَأَكْلُهُ مِنْ ذَبَائِحِهِمْ وَنَحْوُ هَذَا</p>
  <p>“His keeping company with his people, his eating from their slaughtered animals, and things of this kind.”</p>
</div>

He also records a statement attributed to al-Dahhak, through the book of al-Naqqash, concerning Muhammad's attendance at religious gatherings that God did not approve of. Ibn Atiyya describes Muhammad's earlier condition as one of bewilderment. He could recognise the wrongness of the idolatrous practices of Quraysh, but no clear divine instruction had yet been communicated to him.

He then concludes:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn Atiyya, Al-Muharrar al-Wajiz, 5:496–497</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَأَمَّا عِبَادَةُ الْأَصْنَامِ فَلَمْ يَلْتَبِسْ بِهَا قَطُّ</p>
  <p>“As for the worship of idols, he was never involved in it.”</p>
</div>

This is one of the clearest examples of the distinction developing in the commentary tradition. Association with the community, participation in some of its affairs, and even eating from its slaughtered animals are discussed as aspects of Muhammad's pre-prophetic existence.

Idol worship, however, is explicitly denied. The important historical observation is that these distinctions were being formulated in response to questions about what Muhammad had actually been doing before revelation.

## 109:2–3 and the Question of Whether Muhammad Worshipped God Before Revelation

An especially revealing passage appears in al-Zamakhshari's commentary on Surah al-Kafirun.

The passage concerns the words:

> <Arabic>وَلَا أَنَا عَابِدٌ مَا عَبَدْتُمْ</Arabic>
>
> “Nor am I a worshipper of what you worship.”

Al-Zamakhshari (d. 538 AH) explains:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Zamakhshari, Al-Kashshaf, 4:809, on Surah al-Kafirun</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">يَعْنِي لَمْ تُعْهَدْ مِنِّي عِبَادَةُ صَنَمٍ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ</p>
  <p>“Meaning, the worship of an idol was never known of me in the Jahiliyya.”</p>
</div>

He then discusses the grammatical distinction between the past and present forms of worship in the passage.

His explanation concludes:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Zamakhshari, Al-Kashshaf, 4:809</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">لِأَنَّهُمْ كَانُوا يَعْبُدُونَ الْأَصْنَامَ قَبْلَ الْمَبْعَثِ، وَهُوَ لَمْ يَكُنْ يَعْبُدُ اللَّهَ تَعَالَى فِي ذَلِكَ الْوَقْتِ</p>
  <p>“Because they were worshipping idols before the mission, while he was not worshipping God Most High at that time.”</p>
</div>

Al-Zamakhshari explicitly denies that Muhammad was known to have worshipped idols in the Jahiliyya, but he also states that Muhammad was not worshipping God at that time. The passage then presents a religious situation that does not fit neatly into the traditional description of a man already practising an established Abrahamic form of worship.

### The subsequent theological response

Later exegetes objected to the implications of al-Zamakhshari's statement. They invoked reports concerning Muhammad's *tahannuth* in the cave of Hira. The objection was that Muhammad had indeed engaged in worship before revelation, even if the specific form of his worship was not yet known through the revealed Islamic law.

The debate increasingly concerned how to preserve a particular conception of Muhammad's pre-prophetic religious status. Was he already worshipping God through an earlier religious law? Was he following the remaining Abrahamic traditions? Or was his religious life something different, with guidance and formal worship emerging through revelation?

## 16:123: When Was Muhammad Instructed to Follow Abraham?

Another important passage is 16:123:

<QuranVerse verse="16:123" highlightEnglish={["Then we inspired you"]} highlightArabic={["ثُمَّ أَوْحَيْنَآ إِلَيْكَ"]} />

The verse does not simply describe Muhammad as someone who had always consciously followed the religion of Abraham.

It says:

> <Arabic>ثُمَّ أَوْحَيْنَا إِلَيْكَ</Arabic>
>
> “**Then** We revealed to you.”

God subsequently instructs Muhammad to follow Abraham's religion. This is a revealed command and should be understood as such. The verse does not logically prove that Muhammad had never previously believed in one God. A command may affirm or reaffirm an existing conviction. But it also cannot be used as straightforward evidence that he was already a practising Hanif before revelation.

The traditional account frequently assumes the latter without establishing it independently.

There is also an interesting later interpretation of this verse. Isma'il Haqqi (d. 1127 AH), in *Ruh al-Bayan*, makes the following statement:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Isma'il Haqqi, Ruh al-Bayan, 5:95, on 16:123</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَكَانَ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ عَلَى دِينِ قَوْمِهِ قَبْلَ النُّبُوَّةِ</p>
  <p>“He was upon the religion of his people before prophethood.”</p>
</div>

But he immediately explains what he means:

He was upon the remaining heritage of Abraham and Ishmael found among Quraysh, including their pilgrimage, rites, commercial practices and manners.

He then adds:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Isma'il Haqqi, Ruh al-Bayan, 5:95</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَأَمَّا التَّوْحِيدُ فَإِنَّهُمْ كَانُوا قَدْ بَدَّلُوهُ وَالنَّبِيُّ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ لَمْ يَكُنْ إِلَّا عَلَيْهِ</p>
  <p>“As for monotheism, they had changed it, and the Prophet was upon nothing but it.”</p>
</div>

This is a blatant example of later exegetical harmonisation. Haqqi accepts the statement that Muhammad followed the religion of his people, but reconstructs that religion as a collection of surviving Abrahamic rituals and customs, while excluding their polytheistic creed. The formulation allows him to retain the expression *din qawmihi* without conceding that Muhammad shared their idolatrous beliefs.

It is precisely the kind of interpretive distinction that needs to be examined historically rather than simply accepted as an established description of pre-Islamic religion.

## Was the Idea Rejected Only in Later Islam?

It would be inaccurate to suggest that no early scholar ever objected to the stronger interpretations of Muhammad's dalal. Some objections appear quite early.

Al-Maturidi (d. 333 AH), for example, discusses several interpretations of 93:7, including an unnamed interpretation that understood Muhammad to have been in a state of genuine misguidance before God guided him to monotheism. He rejects this as an inappropriate attribution.

Al-Wahidi (d. 468 AH) is also a good example. After presenting the interpretation that Muhammad was an unbeliever among a misguided people, he explicitly states that the scholars of his theological school held that the Messenger had never been an unbeliever. He nevertheless preserves the alternative interpretation.

Al-Zamakhshari similarly writes that the forty-year report is acceptable if it means that Muhammad shared his people's lack of revealed knowledge, but not if it means that he followed their religion and unbelief.

He writes:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Zamakhshari, Al-Kashshaf, 4:768, on 93:7</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">فَإِنْ أَرَادَ أَنَّهُ كَانَ عَلَى دِينِهِمْ وَكُفْرِهِمْ فَمَعَاذَ اللَّهِ</p>
  <p>“But if he meant that he was upon their religion and unbelief, then God forbid!”</p>
</div>

These passages are important because they demonstrate that the debate was not entirely absent in the early centuries. Al-Tabari and al-Zajjaj preserve the broad interpretation that Muhammad was upon the affairs of his people for forty years without explicitly condemning it in those passages.

Subsequently, commentators increasingly explain the statement in terms of shared ignorance of revealed laws, outward conformity, or participation in harmless customs. The stronger interpretation of personal unbelief or shirk receives considerably more explicit resistance.

## The Hadith and the Problem of Idol Contact

Another interesting passage occurs in al-Qurtubi's commentary on 42:52. He discusses a report attributed to Jabir, transmitted from Uthman ibn Abi Shayba, which describes Muhammad attending gatherings of idolaters.

The report contains the expression:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Qurtubi, Al-Jami' li-Ahkam al-Qur'an, 16:58–60, on 42:52</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَعَهْدُهُ بِاسْتِلَامِ الْأَصْنَامِ</p>
  <p>“His last contact was touching the idols.”</p>
</div>

The implication is that Muhammad had previously touched idols. However, al-Qurtubi explicitly rejects the report. He cites Ahmad ibn Hanbal as describing it as fabricated or resembling a fabrication, and al-Daraqutni as identifying an error in its chain. Al-Qurtubi subsequently describes the narration as objectionable.

It would therefore be inappropriate to present this hadith as established proof of Muhammad's pre-prophetic idol worship. Its value is different. It illustrates that traditions concerning his association with idol-related gatherings and practices circulated within the exegetical tradition and were subsequently subjected to criticism.

## *Ṣābiʾ*: The Qurayshite Designation for Religious Defection

The discussion so far has concentrated on Qur'anic passages and exegetical reports. A separate body of evidence appears in hadith and sīrah narratives: Muhammad himself is repeatedly identified as **al-ṣābiʾ** (<Arabic>الصابئ</Arabic>), while converts who embraced his message are described as having *ṣabaʾū* (<Arabic>صبأوا / صبوا</Arabic>) or as belonging to the collective *al-ṣubāt* (<Arabic>الصباة</Arabic>).

This terminology matters because early lexicographers explain the relevant verb as leaving one religion for another. It therefore supplies a social description of Muhammad's religious rupture with Quraysh. The label was not simply a neutral name for a new theological school. In Quraysh's usage, it marked someone who had departed from the religion inherited from his people. This does not by itself establish which rites Muhammad had personally performed before revelation, but it makes it difficult to describe him as someone whom the early narrative tradition consistently understood to have stood outside the religious world of Quraysh from the beginning.

### Muhammad himself called *al-ṣābiʾ*

The reports are not confined to a single narrative.

**The woman with the waterskins — al-Bukhārī 344.** During the well-known journey in which the Prophet's companions encounter a woman carrying water, she asks whether they mean the man known as:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"الَّذِي يُقَالُ لَهُ الصَّابِئُ"}
  translation={"The man who is called al-ṣābiʾ."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 344"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:344"
/>

When she later returns to her people, she describes the two men as having taken her to:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"هَذَا الَّذِي يُقَالُ لَهُ الصَّابِئُ"}
  translation={"This man who is called al-ṣābiʾ."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 344"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:344"
/>

The label functions as a recognizable public identifier for Muhammad. Particularly important is the explanatory note transmitted immediately after the report in al-Bukhārī:

<HadithBlock
  label={"The gloss after the report"}
  arabic={"صَبَأَ: خَرَجَ مِنْ دِينِهِ إِلَى غَيْرِهِ"}
  translation={"Ṣabaʾa: he departed from his religion for another."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 344"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:344"
/>

Al-Bukhārī then separately preserves Abū al-ʿĀliya's explanation of the Sabians as a group associated with the Psalms. The juxtaposition is useful: the narrative designation is explained through the act of leaving one religion for another, while a distinct, more technical use refers to a religious community. The two senses should not be collapsed.

**Abū Dharr — al-Bukhārī 3522; Muslim 2473.** After Abū Dharr publicly declares his Islam, Quraysh respond:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"قُومُوا إِلَى هَذَا الصَّابِئِ"}
  translation={"Get up against this ṣābiʾ."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3522"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3522"
/>

In a version preserved by Muslim, Abū Dharr asks where the man is whom they call:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"أَيْنَ هَذَا الَّذِي تَدْعُونَهُ الصَّابِئَ؟"}
  translation={"Where is this man whom you call al-ṣābiʾ?"}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2473a"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim:2473a"
/>

The report then has the people point to Abū Dharr and identify him by that label. The sequence is significant: the label is applied to a person precisely in the context of his public alignment with Muhammad and Islam.

**The shepherd's question — al-Ḥākim, *al-Mustadrak* 3:9 and 5:241 (numbered 4273 in some databases).** A shepherd asks Muhammad:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"أَنْتَ الَّذِي تَزْعُمُ قُرَيْشٌ أَنَّهُ صَابِئٌ؟"}
  translation={"Are you the one whom Quraysh claim is a ṣābiʾ?"}
  source={"Al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 3:9, 5:241"}
/>

Muhammad answers:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"إِنَّهُمْ لَيَقُولُونَ ذَلِكَ"}
  translation={"That is indeed what they say."}
  source={"Al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 3:9, 5:241"}
/>

This report is especially pertinent because it explicitly attributes the designation to Quraysh. Al-Ḥākim judged the report sound, and al-Dhahabī is reported to have agreed. It remains supporting evidence rather than a report at the level of the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections, but its wording directly connects the label with Quraysh's public characterization of Muhammad.

**ʿUmar's description of Muhammad — Ibn Isḥāq, preserved by Ibn Hishām.** In the account of ʿUmar's conversion, ʿUmar says that he is setting out to kill:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"مُحَمَّدًا هَذَا الصَّابِئَ"}
  translation={"This ṣābiʾ, Muhammad."}
  source={"Ibn Hishām, al-Sīrah al-nabawiyyah"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/ibnhisham:395"
/>

He then describes Muhammad as having divided Quraysh, disparaged their judgment, criticized their religion, and insulted their gods. This is a sīrah narrative rather than a sound hadith with a fully connected canonical chain, and should be identified as such. Its value is that the label appears in a story that explicitly frames Muhammad's preaching as a challenge to Quraysh's religion and gods.

**Abū Lahab at the tribal fairs — Ibn Isḥāq's sīrah material.** In another narrative, Abū Lahab follows Muhammad as he presents his message to the tribes and warns them:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"فَإِنَّهُ صَابِئٌ كَاذِبٌ"}
  translation={"He is a lying ṣābiʾ."}
  source={"Ibn Isḥāq, sīrah material"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/history:827"
/>

The accusation again presents Muhammad as a religious defector. The epithet is deployed to discredit his preaching and deter other tribes from following him.

These reports do not all have the same transmission status. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim provide the strongest narrative attestations; the al-Ḥākim report is a useful additional witness; and the Ibn Isḥāq material belongs to the sīrah tradition and must be assessed on its own terms. Their convergence is more important than treating every version as an independent, equally sound testimony.

### Converts called *ṣābiʾ* or said to have *ṣabaʾū*

The meaning becomes clearer when the same vocabulary is applied to other people at the moment they adopt Islam.

**ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb — al-Bukhārī 3864–3865.** In one report, the crowd says:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"نُرِيدُ هَذَا ابْنَ الْخَطَّابِ الَّذِي صَبَا"}
  translation={"We want this Ibn al-Khaṭṭāb who has ṣabaʾa'd."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3864"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3864"
/>

Another version says:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"صَبَا عُمَرُ"}
  translation={"ʿUmar has ṣabaʾa'd."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3865"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3865"
/>

The report identifies the occasion as ʿUmar's embracing Islam. In Ibn Isḥāq's longer account, Jamīl b. Maʿmar publicly announces that ʿUmar has *ṣabaʾa*'d, and ʿUmar corrects him: "He lies; rather, I have embraced Islam" (<Arabic>كَذَبَ، وَلَكِنِّي قَدْ أَسْلَمْتُ</Arabic>). The contrast between the accusation and ʿUmar's own description is revealing: *ṣabaʾa* is the community's label for his departure, whereas *aslama* is his own religious self-identification.

A longer version attributed to Anas b. Mālik makes the semantic connection explicit: **<Arabic>مَا أَرَاكَ إِلَّا قَدْ صَبَوْتَ وَتَرَكْتَ دِينَكَ الَّذِي أَنْتَ عَلَيْهِ</Arabic>** ("I see that you have *ṣabaʾta* and left the religion you were upon"). It is reported through al-Qāsim b. ʿUthmān and appears in al-Ḥākim and other compilations, but al-Dhahabī judged this route *munkarah jiddan* (very objectionable). It should therefore be mentioned only as a weak lexical parallel, not used to establish what happened during ʿUmar's conversion. The sound Bukhārī versions and the early lexicographical explanations carry the main argument.

**Thumāma b. Uthāl — al-Bukhārī 4372 and Muslim 1764a.** After Thumāma embraces Islam and travels to Mecca, someone asks:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"صَبَوْتَ؟"}
  translation={"Have you ṣabaʾta?"}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4372"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:4372"
/>

He replies:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"لَا، وَلَكِنْ أَسْلَمْتُ مَعَ مُحَمَّدٍ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ"}
  translation={"No; rather, I have embraced Islam with Muḥammad, the Messenger of Allah."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1764a"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim:1764a"
/>

This is among the clearest examples because the question and answer are preserved in both al-Bukhārī and Muslim. The report explicitly contrasts the label *ṣabaʾta* with the convert's preferred description, *aslamtu*. It shows that the same term was used of someone who had entered Muhammad's religion, just as it was used of Muhammad himself.

**Banū Jadhīma — al-Bukhārī 4339 and 7189.** When Khālid b. al-Walīd calls the tribe to Islam, the report says:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"فَلَمْ يُحْسِنُوا أَنْ يَقُولُوا أَسْلَمْنَا، فَجَعَلُوا يَقُولُونَ: صَبَأْنَا صَبَأْنَا"}
  translation={"They did not know how to say 'We have embraced Islam', so they kept saying, 'We have ṣabaʾnā, we have ṣabaʾnā."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4339"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:4339"
/>

This is particularly useful because the narrative itself places *ṣabaʾnā* beside *aslamnā* as the expression the tribe was trying, unsuccessfully, to use. The phrase is not evidence that Banū Jadhīma belonged to the later Sabian religious community. It is a conversion-related expression, understood in the narrative as the community's attempt to say that it had entered Islam.

**Ḥamza b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib — al-Ḥākim 4878 and the Ibn Isḥāq sīrah account.** After Ḥamza declares that Muhammad is God's messenger, men of Quraysh say:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"مَا نَرَاكَ يَا حَمْزَةُ إِلَّا صَبَأْتَ"}
  translation={"We see you, Ḥamza, as having ṣabaʾta."}
  source={"Al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 4878"}
/>

The narrative subsequently places in the devil's mouth the accusation:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"اتَّبَعْتَ هَذَا الصَّابِئَ وَتَرَكْتَ دَيْنَ آبَائِكَ"}
  translation={"You have followed this ṣābiʾ and abandoned the religion of your fathers."}
  source={"Al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 4878"}
/>

The account explicitly describes Ḥamza as having been upon the religion of his people before this event. It is valuable as a narrative illustration of the charge of ancestral-religion abandonment, but the Ibn Isḥāq transmission includes an unnamed intermediary and should not be placed on the same evidentiary level as the Ṣaḥīḥ reports.

Other reports extend the usage to Ṣudayy b. ʿAjlān, Rifāʿa b. Mālik, Ṭalḥa b. ʿUbayd Allāh, ʿUqba b. Abī Muʿayṭ, Wāthila b. al-Asqaʿ, and Abū Sufyān. Their individual chains and versions vary. They are best treated as a wider pattern in the narrative corpus, not as a collection of equally strong independent proofs.

### The collective name *al-ṣubāt*

The terminology also appears as a collective label for Muhammad's followers. In al-Bukhārī 3950, Abū Jahl challenges Saʿd b. Muʿādh:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"أَلَا أَرَاكَ تَطُوفُ بِمَكَّةَ آمِنًا وَقَدْ أَوَيْتُمُ الصُّبَاةَ"}
  translation={"Do I not see you circumambulating Mecca safely, when you have sheltered the ṣubāt?"}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3950"}
  url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3950"
/>

The plural *al-ṣubāt* identifies the people who have left their former religious affiliation and joined the Prophet's movement. Ibn Durayd and later lexicographers likewise record that Quraysh used this collective designation for the Prophet's companions.

This is important for the historical argument: the term is not an isolated nickname or an accusation reserved for Muhammad. It belongs to a broader vocabulary used to describe Muhammad and the community that gathered around him as people who had broken with their inherited religious affiliation.

### A narrative that explains the label directly

A report attributed to Mudrik b. al-Ḥārith al-Ghāmidī, preserved in *al-Āḥād wa-l-mathānī* (4:364–365) and repeated in later compilations, gives an unusually explicit explanation. At Minā, a boy asks his father about a crowd gathered around a man. The father replies:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"هَذَا الصَّابِئُ الَّذِي تَرَكَ دِينَ قَوْمِهِ"}
  translation={"This is the ṣābiʾ who abandoned the religion of his people."}
  source={"Ibn Abī ʿĀṣim, al-Āḥād wa-l-mathānī 4:364–365"}
  highlightArabic={["تَرَكَ دِينَ قَوْمِهِ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["abandoned the religion of his people"]}
/>

This wording is striking because the gloss is built into the narrative itself. It directly associates *al-ṣābiʾ* with leaving the religion of one's people. The report is not established here at the level of the Ṣaḥīḥ collections; it should be presented as an illustrative sīrah-era report, with its grading left open unless its full chain and variants are separately assessed.

### Early Arabic lexicographers explain the usage

The hadith narratives are reinforced by a substantial lexicographical tradition. The recurring definition is:

> <Arabic>خَرَجَ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى دِينٍ</Arabic>
>
> "He departed from one religion into another."

The early authorities are particularly valuable because they do not merely give an abstract dictionary definition; several explicitly explain how Quraysh used the term for Muhammad and the early Muslims.

**Abū ʿUbayd al-Qāsim b. Sallām (d. 224 AH), *Gharīb al-ḥadīth* 1:305:**

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Abū ʿUbayd, Gharīb al-ḥadīth, 1:305</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">الصَّابِئُ عِنْدَ الْعَرَبِ الَّذِي قَدْ خَرَجَ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى دِينٍ</p>
  <p>“Among the Arabs, the <em>ṣābiʾ</em> is someone who has departed from one religion to another.”</p>
</div>

He then explains:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Abū ʿUbayd, Gharīb al-ḥadīth, 1:305</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَلِهَذَا كَانَ الْمُشْرِكُونَ يَقُولُونَ لِلرَّجُلِ إِذَا أَسْلَمَ فِي زَمَنِ النَّبِيِّ عَلَيْهِ السَّلَامُ: قَدْ صَبَأَ فُلَانٌ</p>
  <p>“For this reason, the polytheists used to say of a man who embraced Islam during the Prophet's time: ‘So-and-so has <em>ṣabaʾa</em>'d.’”</p>
</div>

This is one of the most important lexical statements for the present argument. Abū ʿUbayd directly connects the general meaning of religious departure with the historical practice of calling converts to Islam *ṣabaʾū*.

**Ibn Durayd (d. 321 AH), *Jamharat al-lughah* 2:1024:** Ibn Durayd records that Quraysh called the Prophet's companions *al-ṣubāt* in the early period of Islam, and cites the announcement that ʿUmar had *ṣabaʾa*'d. He also preserves ʿUmar's answer that he had embraced Islam rather than accepted the label.

**Ibn al-Anbārī (d. 328 AH), *al-Zāhir* 2:215:**

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn al-Anbārī, al-Zāhir, 2:215</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وَكَانَتْ قُرَيْشٌ تُسَمِّي رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صَابِئًا، وَيُسَمُّونَ أَصْحَابَهُ كَذَلِكَ، لِخُرُوجِهِمْ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى دِينٍ</p>
  <p>“Quraysh used to call the Messenger of Allah a <em>ṣābiʾ</em>, and they called his companions the same, because they had departed from one religion to another.”</p>
</div>

This is unusually direct: Muhammad, his companions, Quraysh's terminology, and the meaning of religious departure are all explicitly connected.

**Ibn al-Athīr (d. 606 AH), *al-Nihāya* 3:3:** Ibn al-Athīr states that the Arabs called the Prophet *al-ṣābiʾ* because he had left the religion of Quraysh for the religion of Islam, and that they called those who entered Islam *maṣbūʾ* and Muslims collectively *al-ṣubāt*. Ibn Manẓūr repeats this explanation in *Lisān al-ʿArab* 1:107–108. These are later lexicographical witnesses, but they preserve an explicit account of the historical usage.

Other lexicographers—including al-Khaṭṭābī, al-Jawharī, Ibn Fāris, al-Harawī, al-Thaʿālibī, Ibn Sīda, Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ, al-Fayyūmī, al-Fīrūzābādī, and al-Zabīdī—also give the general sense of leaving one religion for another. The oldest lexicon in the dossier, *Kitāb al-ʿAyn*, also preserves a narrower association with adopting the religion of the Sabians. The lexicons therefore show semantic development and more than one use; the hadith context and the explicit explanations of Abū ʿUbayd, Ibn Durayd, and Ibn al-Anbārī are what establish the conversion-related sense relevant here.

### What the *ṣābiʾ* evidence establishes—and what it does not

The lexical and narrative evidence supports several conclusions:

1. Muhammad is repeatedly represented as being called *al-ṣābiʾ* by people who identify him with a break from Quraysh's religious order.
2. The same word-family is applied to converts such as ʿUmar, Abū Dharr, Thumāma, and Ḥamza, and to the Muslim community collectively.
3. Early lexicographers explicitly explain the designation as departure from one religion to another; some specifically state that Quraysh used it for Muhammad and those who entered his religion.
4. The terminology therefore provides evidence for how Muhammad's religious transition was perceived and described in the early narrative tradition.

The argument for a pre-prophetic polytheist past is cumulative. The exegetical report attributed to al-Ḍaḥḥāk is the explicit attribution of shirk; the *ṣābiʾ* narratives and lexicons corroborate the underlying transition away from Quraysh's religious world. Together, they suggest Muhammad had previously participated in Quraysh's polytheistic religion.

The important observation is that these reports do not all make the same claim. However, they collectively challenge the assumption that Muhammad's pre-prophetic religious identity is historically straightforward.

The earliest sources preserve descriptions of him living within the religious world of his people, following their affairs for forty years, attending their gatherings, and bearing sins from the Jahiliyya. One attribution explicitly identifies his burden as shirk.

The subsequent commentators repeatedly introduce distinctions that are absent from the simplest reading of these earlier descriptions. The question is whether these distinctions represent historically accurate descriptions of Muhammad's earlier religious practice or whether they are theological constructions designed to preserve a particular conception of prophetic purity.

## From the Religion of Quraysh to Divine Guidance

The Qur'an does not present divine guidance as something that would be unnecessary for Muhammad before his prophetic mission. On the contrary, Qur'an 93:7 explicitly describes God finding him astray and guiding him. Qur'an 16:123 describes God subsequently revealing to him the instruction to follow Abraham.

These passages can be read as describing a transformation in Muhammad's religious condition. On this reconstruction, Muhammad was born and raised within the religious environment of his community. His pre-prophetic life was shaped by that environment, and his relationship with its religious practices was not necessarily the same as the later Islamic understanding of monotheism.

God subsequently guided him through revelation, taught him the religion he was to proclaim, and established the distinction between his new religious community and the surrounding polytheistic society.

There is nothing inherently contradictory about such a narrative. In fact, it gives the language of guidance in 93:7 a particularly concrete historical significance. Rather than reducing guidance to the acquisition of knowledge about the Qur'an and its legislation, it allows for a more comprehensive transformation in religious identity.

## Conclusion: Reconsidering Muhammad's Religious Identity Before Prophethood

The traditional description of Muhammad as a lifelong Abrahamic monotheist deserves more critical examination than it generally receives.

The Qur'an describes him as having been astray before God guided him. Early exegetes preserve interpretations associating his forty pre-prophetic years with the affairs of his people. Other reports identify sins that preceded his prophetic mission and place the burden of 94:2 within the Jahiliyya.

Most significantly, al-Tabari preserves the report attributed to al-Dahhak that explicitly identifies Muhammad's former burden with shirk. The hadith and sīrah tradition adds a complementary line of evidence: Muhammad is repeatedly called *al-ṣābiʾ*, while converts and the early Muslim community receive the same designation. Abū ʿUbayd, Ibn Durayd, and Ibn al-Anbārī explicitly explain this usage as departure from one religion to another, and Ibn al-Anbārī specifically says that Quraysh called Muhammad and his companions by this name. This evidence does not independently identify an idol rite, but it reinforces the picture of a break with the inherited religion of Quraysh.

Other commentators acknowledge his association with the customs, gatherings and religious affairs of Quraysh while simultaneously denying that he worshipped idols.

Al-Zamakhshari's discussion of Qur'an 109:3 has him denying that Muhammad worshipped idols, but also states that Muhammad was not worshipping God at that time. Later commentators found this formulation difficult to accept and appealed to reports of his pre-prophetic worship at Hira.

Qur'an 16:123, meanwhile, explicitly presents the instruction to follow Abraham as something communicated to Muhammad through revelation. It cannot independently establish that he had always been a practising Hanif before his mission.

The historical picture is therefore more complicated than the familiar traditional account suggests. My argument is that Muhammad's pre-prophetic religious identity should be reconstructed within the actual religious environment of pre-Islamic Mecca, rather than through assumptions derived from later doctrines of prophetic purity.

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">The available evidence makes a pre-prophetic association with Quraysh's religious practices difficult to dismiss. It also leaves open the possibility that his earlier religious life involved participation in aspects of their polytheistic tradition. What is much clearer is that the idea of an uninterrupted, consciously Abrahamic religious life is not the only picture preserved in the early sources. The transition from his earlier religious environment to the religion proclaimed through revelation deserves to be taken seriously.</VerdictBox>
