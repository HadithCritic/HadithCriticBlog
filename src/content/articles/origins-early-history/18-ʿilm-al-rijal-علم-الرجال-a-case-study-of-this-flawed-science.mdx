<ClaimBox title="Thesis">

The problem with *ʿilm al-rijāl* is not that every biographical judgment is useless. The problem is that the discipline is often presented as if it produces objective certainty, while its own dictionaries preserve contradictory, politically charged, and theologically motivated testimony. ʿIkrimah, the freedman of Ibn ʿAbbās, is a useful case study because the same tradition that made him a pillar of tafsīr and hadith also preserved accusations that he lied against Ibn ʿAbbās, promoted Kharijite views, and transmitted material that conveniently serves those views.

</ClaimBox>

Traditional hadith authentication depends on two major claims. First, the chain must be connected. Second, the transmitters must be upright and precise. The second claim is where the difficulty appears. “Uprightness” and “precision” are not measurable qualities. They are reconstructed from reports, rival memories, sectarian loyalties, student networks, and later scholarly preference. A biographical entry does not usually give the reader a clean verdict. It gives the reader an archive of competing witnesses.

The popular apologetic version of the science hides that archive. It gives the final label—*thiqah*, *ṣadūq*, *ḍaʿīf*, *matrūk*—and treats the label as though it were the result of neutral measurement. The classical sources themselves are messier. They record praise and attack, acceptance and suspicion, canonical use and local rejection. A later scholar then decides which testimony matters and which testimony can be explained away.

<ContextNote title="What This Article Is and Is Not Arguing">

This is not an argument that every narrator in the hadith corpus was a liar. It is also not an argument that every criticism of ʿIkrimah is equally sound. The narrower point is stronger: when a narrator’s file contains serious early accusations, sectarian allegations, and direct legal usefulness for his alleged ideology, the later decision to certify him as reliable is not an empirical result. It is a judgment call.

</ContextNote>

## The Case Study: ʿIkrimah, Freedman of Ibn ʿAbbās

ʿIkrimah was one of the most influential transmitters from Ibn ʿAbbās. The rijāl sources describe him as a major authority in Qurʾānic interpretation, legal reports, maghāzī, and the traditions of Ibn ʿAbbās. This is exactly why his case matters. He is not a marginal weak narrator whose rejection would cost little. He is a narrator whose acceptance became structurally useful for the Sunni canon.

The classical record about him is not one-sided. It contains strong praise.

<HadithBlock
  label="Praise Preserved in the Rijāl Archive"
  source="al-Mizzī, Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, entry on ʿIkrimah mawla Ibn ʿAbbās"

  translation={`Muḥammad b. Fuḍayl transmitted from ʿUthmān b. Ḥakīm: I was sitting with Abū Umāmah b. Sahl b. />

The same entry also preserves the familiar statements that made ʿIkrimah valuable to later scholars. Jābir b. Zayd is reported to have called him “the ocean” and “the most knowledgeable of people.” al-Shaʿbī is reported to have said that no one remained more knowledgeable about the Book of God than ʿIkrimah. Qatādah classified him among the leading specialists in tafsīr and sīrah. These statements explain why later tradition fought to keep him.

## The Other Side of the File

The same biographical tradition preserves severe accusations. The issue is not a single hostile line from an obscure enemy. The criticisms concern three recurring themes: lying against Ibn ʿAbbās, Kharijite affiliation, and instability in transmission.

<HadithBlock
  label="Accusation from the Ibn ʿAbbās Household"
  source="al-Mizzī, Tahdhīb al-Kamāl; also cited by al-Dhahabī in Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ"
  arabic={`وَقَالَ جَرِيرُ بْنُ عَبْدِ الْحَمِيدِ، عَنْ يَزِيدَ بْنِ أَبِي زِيَادٍ: دَخَلْتُ عَلَى عَلِيِّ بْنِ عَبْدِ اللَّهِ بْنِ عَبَّاسٍ، وَعِكْرِمَةُ مُقَيَّدٌ عَلَى بَابِ الْحَشِّ، قَالَ: قُلْتُ: مَا لِهَذَا كَذَا؟ قَالَ: إِنَّهُ يَكْذِبُ عَلَى أَبِي.`}
  translation={`Jarīr b. ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd transmitted from Yazīd b. Abī Ziyād: I entered upon ʿAlī b. ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAbbās while ʿIkrimah was bound at the door of the privy. I said: “Why is this man like this?” He replied: “He lies against my father.”`}
/>

This report matters because it does not come from a later polemicist saying, “I do not like ʿIkrimah.” It places the accusation inside the household whose authority ʿIkrimah claimed to transmit. Even if a defender disputes the chain of this accusation, the fact that such an accusation circulated in the rijāl archive is itself significant. The later science did not discover a clean narrator and then verify him. It inherited a contested narrator and then chose how to manage the contest.

The criticisms continue.

<HadithBlock
  label="Early Critics Calling Him Unreliable"
  source="al-Mizzī, Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, entry on ʿIkrimah"
  arabic={`وَقَالَ مُسْلِمُ بْنُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ، عَنِ الصَّلْتِ بْنِ دِينَارٍ أَبِي شُعَيْبٍ الْمَجْنُونِ: سَأَلْتُ مُحَمَّدَ بْنَ سِيرِينَ عَنْ عِكْرِمَةَ، فَقَالَ: مَا يَسُوءُنِي أَنَّهُ يَكُونُ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْجَنَّةِ، وَلَكِنَّهُ كَذَّابٌ. وَقَالَ وَهَيْبُ بْنُ خَالِدٍ: سَمِعْتُ يَحْيَى بْنَ سَعِيدٍ الْأَنْصَارِيَّ وَأَيُّوبَ ذَكَرَا عِكْرِمَةَ، فَقَالَ يَحْيَى: كَانَ كَذَّابًا، وَقَالَ أَيُّوبُ: لَمْ يَكُنْ بِكَذَّابٍ.`}
  translation={`Muslim b. Ibrāhīm transmitted from al-Ṣalt b. Dīnār Abū Shuʿayb: I asked Muḥammad b. Sīrīn about ʿIkrimah. He said: “It would not displease me if he were among the people of Paradise, but he is a liar.” Wuhayb b. Khālid said: I heard Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Anṣārī and Ayyūb mention ʿIkrimah. Yaḥyā said: “He was a liar,” while Ayyūb said: “He was not a liar.”`}
/>

That last sentence is the whole problem in miniature. Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd says one thing. Ayyūb says the opposite. Later hadith authentication cannot simply quote Ayyūb and pretend the problem disappeared. It has selected one early judgment over another.

## The Sectarian Problem: Kharijite Accusations

The Kharijite accusation is not a decorative biographical detail. It becomes historically relevant because the legal report examined below establishes a rule of execution for religious defection and theological deviance. A narrator accused of Kharijite leanings transmitting a maxim that authorizes killing apostates is not a neutral coincidence. It is motive-relevant evidence.

<HadithBlock
  label="Reports of Kharijite / Ṣufrī Affiliation"
  source="al-Mizzī, Tahdhīb al-Kamāl; al-Dhahabī, Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ"
  arabic={`وَقَالَ عَلِيُّ بْنُ الْمَدِينِيِّ: كَانَ عِكْرِمَةُ يَرَى رَأْيَ نَجْدَةَ الْحَرُورِيِّ. وَقَالَ أَبُو بَكْرِ بْنُ أَبِي خَيْثَمَةَ: سَمِعْتُ يَحْيَى بْنَ مَعِينٍ يَقُولُ: إِنَّمَا لَمْ يَذْكُرْ مَالِكُ بْنُ أَنَسٍ عِكْرِمَةَ، لِأَنَّ عِكْرِمَةَ كَانَ يَنْتَحِلُ رَأْيَ الصُّفْرِيَّةِ. وَقَالَ عُمَرُ بْنُ قَيْسٍ الْمَكِّيُّ، عَنْ عَطَاءٍ: كَانَ عِكْرِمَةُ أَبَاضِيًّا.`}
  translation={`ʿAlī b. al-Madīnī said: “ʿIkrimah held the opinion of Najdah the Ḥarūrī.” Abū Bakr b. Abī Khaythamah said: I heard Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn say: “Mālik b. Anas only refrained from mentioning ʿIkrimah because ʿIkrimah professed the Ṣufrī view.” ʿUmar b. Qays al-Makkī transmitted from ʿAṭāʾ: “ʿIkrimah was Ibāḍī.”`}
/>

The sources also preserve more extreme material: ʿIkrimah allegedly stood at the door of a mosque and declared everyone inside a disbeliever; in another report from Ifrīqiyah, he allegedly wished he were at the pilgrimage season with a spear, striking those present right and left. A defender can challenge the strength of individual reports. The accumulated pattern remains: the tradition itself associated ʿIkrimah with takfīr-oriented sectarian currents.

<ContextNote title="Why This Matters for Legal Hadith">

A sectarian accusation is not automatically proof of fabrication. But it becomes probative when the report under review advances precisely the kind of legal-theological logic associated with that sectarian posture. The issue is not simply, “Was ʿIkrimah a Kharijite?” The issue is that the rijāl archive itself preserves accusations of Kharijite affiliation, while the hadith transmitted through him authorizes killing people for religious defection.

</ContextNote>

## Mālik’s Treatment of ʿIkrimah

Mālik b. Anas is especially important because his legal and hadith culture stands closer to the Medinan material than later canonization. The biographical sources report that Mālik did not view ʿIkrimah as reliable and ordered that his reports not be taken. al-Shāfiʿī is quoted as saying that Mālik had a poor opinion of ʿIkrimah and said, “I do not think anyone should accept his hadith.”

<HadithBlock
  label="Mālik's Rejection"
  source="al-Mizzī, Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, transmitting reports from Ibn Abī Khaythamah, ʿAlī b. al-Madīnī, and al-Shāfiʿī"
  arabic={`وَقَالَ إِبْرَاهِيمُ بْنُ الْمُنْذِرِ الْحِزَامِيُّ، عَنْ مَعْنِ بْنِ عِيسَى وَمُطَرِّفِ بْنِ عَبْدِ اللَّهِ الْمَدَنِيِّ وَمُحَمَّدِ بْنِ الضَّحَّاكِ الْحِزَامِيِّ، قَالُوا: كَانَ مَالِكٌ لَا يَرَى عِكْرِمَةَ ثِقَةً، وَيَأْمُرُ أَنْ لَا يُؤْخَذَ عَنْهُ. وَقَالَ الرَّبِيعُ بْنُ سُلَيْمَانَ، عَنِ الشَّافِعِيِّ: وَهُوَ - يَعْنِي مَالِكَ بْنَ أَنَسٍ - سَيِّئُ الرَّأْيِ فِي عِكْرِمَةَ، قَالَ: لَا أَرَى لِأَحَدٍ أَنْ يَقْبَلَ حَدِيثَهُ.`}
  translation={`Ibrāhīm b. al-Mundhir al-Ḥizāmī transmitted from Maʿn b. ʿĪsā, Muṭarrif b. ʿAbd Allāh al-Madanī, and Muḥammad b. al-Ḍaḥḥāk al-Ḥizāmī: “Mālik did not regard ʿIkrimah as trustworthy, and he would order that reports not be taken from him.” al-Rabīʿ b. Sulaymān transmitted from al-Shāfiʿī: “Mālik b. Anas had a poor opinion of ʿIkrimah. He said: ‘I do not think anyone should accept his hadith.’”`}
/>

A later Sunni can answer that Mālik was stricter here than other critics, or that Mālik’s concern was ʿIkrimah’s doctrine rather than his truthfulness. That answer still proves the point. The verdict depends on how the scholar weighs competing considerations: doctrine, memory, canon-use, local acceptance, and inherited legal need.

## The Canonical Counterweight

The same rijāl archive also records strong defenses of ʿIkrimah. Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal is reported to have said that his hadith may be used as proof. Ibn Maʿīn is quoted declaring him trustworthy alongside Saʿīd b. Jubayr. Some later defenders went further, treating criticism of ʿIkrimah as a suspicious sign.

This is the methodological center of the article. A traditionalist can defend ʿIkrimah. A critic can indict him. Both sides can quote the classical archive. The later label “reliable” does not erase the subjectivity of the path by which that label was chosen.

## The Legal Flashpoint: “Whoever Changes His Religion, Kill Him”

The stakes become serious when ʿIkrimah transmits one of the most consequential reports in Islamic legal history.

<HadithBlock
  label="The Apostasy Proof-Text"
  source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6922; also repeated in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3017"
  arabic={`حَدَّثَنَا أَبُو النُّعْمَانِ مُحَمَّدُ بْنُ الْفَضْلِ، حَدَّثَنَا حَمَّادُ بْنُ زَيْدٍ، عَنْ أَيُّوبَ، عَنْ عِكْرِمَةَ، قَالَ: أُتِيَ عَلِيٌّ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ بِزَنَادِقَةٍ فَأَحْرَقَهُمْ، فَبَلَغَ ذَلِكَ ابْنَ عَبَّاسٍ، فَقَالَ: لَوْ كُنْتُ أَنَا لَمْ أُحَرِّقْهُمْ لِنَهْيِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ، وَلَقَتَلْتُهُمْ لِقَوْلِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ صَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ: مَنْ بَدَّلَ دِينَهُ فَاقْتُلُوهُ.`}
  translation={`Abū al-Nuʿmān Muḥammad b. al-Faḍl narrated to us: Ḥammād b. Zayd narrated to us from Ayyūb, from ʿIkrimah, who said: Some zanādiqah were brought to ʿAlī, and he burned them. When that reached Ibn ʿAbbās, he said: “Had it been me, I would not have burned them, because of the Messenger of God’s prohibition; I would have killed them because of the Messenger of God’s statement: ‘Whoever changes his religion, kill him.’”`}
/>

The exact Bukhārī proof-text is not an abstract legal maxim floating independently in the earliest recoverable material. It appears inside a story. ʿAlī burns a group of theological deviants. Ibn ʿAbbās corrects the method but approves the killing. The report therefore does two things at once: it normalizes the execution of religious deviants and places ʿAlī in need of correction by Ibn ʿAbbās.

For a narrator accused of Kharijite leanings, this narrative shape is historically suspicious. Kharijite memory had reasons to diminish ʿAlī’s authority. Kharijite legal-theological instincts also had reasons to turn religious deviation into a death-worthy category. The report does not merely say, “Apostasy is punished.” It gives the rule through a scene in which ʿAlī mishandles the punishment and Ibn ʿAbbās supplies the prophetic correction.

<ContextNote title="The More Precise Claim">

The strongest argument is not that no later book contains any non-ʿIkrimah wording about apostasy. The stronger argument is that the decisive Bukhārī proof-text, the compact formula, and the early Muṣannaf material are centered on ʿIkrimah → Ibn ʿAbbās, while many claimed parallels either use broader wording such as “leaving the community,” appear as mursal reports, or look like later legal reinforcement of an already circulating formula.

</ContextNote>

## Earlier and Parallel Evidence Does Not Remove the Problem

The broader legal tradition contains other reports about killing people who abandon the community, fight God and His Messenger, or return to unbelief after Islam. These reports are often cited to protect the ʿIkrimah report from criticism. They should be separated rather than blurred together.

The early Muṣannaf of ʿAbd al-Razzāq is especially helpful because it shows both the story form and the compact legal formula in close proximity through the same Ayyūb → ʿIkrimah channel.

The wording variation is important. The same report oscillates between “changed,” “returned,” and a narrative about people who “disbelieved after their Islam.” That instability does not look like a universally known public pronouncement preserved by multiple independent Companion memories. It looks like a legal maxim stabilized inside a story and then extracted into a shorter slogan.

## Why the Report Serves a Kharijite-Imaginable Agenda

A critic does not need to prove that ʿIkrimah personally fabricated the report in a modern courtroom sense. Historical criticism works with probability, motive, transmission shape, and ideological fit. On those terms, this report has a problem.

This is where rijāl reaches its limit. A later scholar may say: “Ayyūb trusted him, Aḥmad used him, Ibn Maʿīn declared him trustworthy, and al-Bukhārī included him.” A historical critic may answer: “Mālik rejected him, the Ibn ʿAbbās household accused him, Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd called him a liar, and the report serves an ideology with which he was associated.” Both sides are working from the same inherited record.

The difference is not science versus ignorance. It is canon-preserving judgment versus historical suspicion.

## The Qurʾānic Baseline

The Qurʾān repeatedly treats belief as a matter that cannot be compelled by coercive religious authority.

<QuranVerse verse="10:99" />

<QuranVerse verse="18:29" />

<QuranVerse verse="88:21-22" />

A hadith report that creates a death penalty for changing religion therefore carries an unusually heavy burden of proof. It cannot be protected merely by saying that the chain is “sound” after a later scholar has chosen one side of a disputed narrator file. If a report overrides the Qurʾān’s repeated pattern of non-compulsion, reminder, and individual accountability, the evidence for that report must be stronger than a contested transmitter whose own archive preserves accusations of sectarian extremism and lying.

<ClaimBox title="The Methodological Failure">

The failure is not that rijāl contains criticism. The failure is that later hadith culture can preserve the criticism, acknowledge the controversy, and still treat the resulting hadith as if the controversy has been neutralized. Once a contested narrator receives canonical protection, the hadith becomes “authentic,” the law becomes “Islamic,” and the Qurʾānic baseline is forced to bend around a disputed report.

</ClaimBox>

## The Modern Afterlife

The legal afterlife of this hadith is not theoretical. Classical jurists did not agree on every procedural detail—repentance periods, male and female apostates, public rebellion, imprisonment, and state authority were debated—but the Bukhārī formula became one of the most quoted proof-texts for the death penalty. Modern apostasy laws are not produced by this report alone, but the report helped give later legal systems a prophetic slogan for religious coercion.

Pew Research Center’s 2019 legal survey, published in 2022, found that 22 of 198 countries and territories had laws or policies against apostasy; 13 of those were in the Middle East and North Africa. That does not mean every modern statute descends directly from ʿIkrimah’s report. It means the legal problem created by these traditions still has public consequences.

## What This Case Study Shows

ʿIkrimah’s case exposes the weakness of presenting *ʿilm al-rijāl* as a clean scientific filter. The filter did not remove a narrator accused of lying against his master, associated with Kharijite currents, rejected by Mālik, and attached to a report that supports exactly the kind of punitive sectarian logic with which he was associated. Instead, the filter absorbed the controversy and then allowed later scholars to canonize one side of it.

<VerdictBox title="Final Assessment">

The rijāl dictionaries do not give us certainty. They give us contested memory. When the contested memory concerns a narrator whose report authorizes killing people for changing religion, the burden of proof must be much higher than “al-Bukhārī accepted him.” A Qurʾān-first method has no reason to subordinate explicit non-compulsion to a legally violent report transmitted through such a disputed figure.

</VerdictBox>
