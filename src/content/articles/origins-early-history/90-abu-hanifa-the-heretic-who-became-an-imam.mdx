In Sunni Islam today, four names sit together as the founders of its major legal schools: **Abū Ḥanīfa, Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal**. They are presented as representatives of the same orthodox inheritance. Yet one of the books transmitted in Aḥmad's own circle contains a remarkable question about the first of those four imams:

<HadithBlock label="Would a man be rewarded for hating Abū Ḥanīfa?" source="ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad, al-Sunna, no. 228, 1:180 (Shamela 4012)" translation="I was informed from Isḥāq b. Manṣūr al-Kawsaj, who said: I asked Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal: 'Is a man rewarded for hating Abū Ḥanīfa and his followers?' He said: 'Yes, by God.'" arabic="وَأُخْبِرْتُ عَنْ إِسْحَاقَ بْنِ مَنْصُورٍ الْكَوْسَجِ، قَالَ: قُلْتُ لِأَحْمَدَ بْنِ حَنْبَلٍ يُؤْجَرُ الرَّجُلُ عَلَى بُغْضِ أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ وَأَصْحَابِهِ؟ قَالَ: إِي وَاللَّهِ" />

This is not a modern anti-Ḥanafī tract. It appears at the beginning of a lengthy section of *al-Sunna*, compiled by Aḥmad's son ʿAbd Allāh (d. 290 AH), under the heading *“What I preserved from my father and other teachers concerning Abū Ḥanīfa.”* The wording begins **“I was informed”** (<Arabic>وأخبرت</Arabic>), rather than claiming that ʿAbd Allāh personally heard the exchange. That distinction matters for the report's historical reliability. But its **placement in the collection** is beyond dispute: the compiler chose to open his dossier on Abū Ḥanīfa with this kind of material.[^1]

It is a strange opening for the biography of a man who would later be celebrated as a Sunni imam. And this is only the beginning. The same section preserves accusations of unbelief, curses, celebrations of his death, comparisons to disease, claims that his legal reasoning damaged Islam, and allegations that his own teacher and students repudiated him.

How did the alleged enemy of Islam become one of Sunni Islam's defining authorities?

<ClaimBox title="The central argument" facts={[{ value: '10', label: 'Source books searched' }, { value: '61', label: 'Titled legal disputes' }, { value: '183', label: 'Entries in al-Sunna section' }]}>

The early and medieval sources preserve **not one unanimous judgment**, but an argument over who was entitled to define Islam. Abū Ḥanīfa became a test case in conflicts over transmitted reports, legal reasoning, theology, political authority, and scholarly identity. Later Sunni orthodoxy did not simply inherit an uncontested four-imam tradition; it emerged after influential circles had attempted to exclude one of the men it would eventually canonize.

</ClaimBox>

This article builds on the earlier Quran Talk studies of Abū Ḥanīfa and on Ahmad Khan's research into medieval Sunni orthodoxy. It adds a direct examination of ten Arabic source texts, while distinguishing **what a compiler recorded** from **what can be established that an alleged speaker actually said**.[^2]

## 1. The Chronology Everyone Forgets

Abū Ḥanīfa died in **150 AH / 767 CE**. The books in which many of the most inflammatory reports survive were composed decades or centuries afterward. A statement attributed to al-Thawrī, for example, might be placed in the second century, but the surviving copy of that statement may be found in a third-, fourth-, or fifth-century compilation. Those are **two different dates**.

This chronology changes the question. We are not reading transcripts secretly recorded during Abū Ḥanīfa's lifetime. We are reading **later literary presentations of an earlier dispute**, often supplied with isnāds meant to connect the compiler to the supposed speaker.

Ahmad Khan identifies an early stage of chiefly legal and hadith criticism in **800–850 CE**, a more systematic discourse of heresy in **850–950**, and subsequent overlapping developments that made broader acceptance possible in the tenth and eleventh centuries. This is a history of changing representations, not a claim that all opponents suddenly changed their opinions on the same day.[^3]

## 2. From a Legal Opponent to a Religious Enemy

Consider the *sequence* that ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad placed at the beginning of his chapter. After the question about earning reward for hatred (no. 228), he transmits a question about choosing between poorly informed hadith transmitters and jurists associated with independent legal opinion. The response attributed to Aḥmad is uncompromising:

<HadithBlock label="Even a weak hadith?" source="al-Sunna, no. 229, 1:180–181 (4012)" translation="He should ask the people of hadith, not the people of opinion. A weak hadith is better than the opinion of Abū Ḥanīfa." arabic="يَسْأَلُ أَصْحَابَ الْحَدِيثِ وَلَا يَسْأَلُ أَصْحَابَ الرَّأْي، الضَّعِيفُ الْحَدِيثِ خَيْرٌ مِنْ رَأْيِ أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ" />

The next report compares Abū Ḥanīfa's opinions to something worthless, an insult repeated elsewhere in an explicit “dung” formulation. The section then immediately begins naming theological accusations, including statements attributed to Abū Yūsuf, Abū Ḥanīfa's famous disciple.[^5]

The cumulative effect is not a balanced inquiry about one mistaken legal verdict. The editorial construction moves from **hatred**, to **methodological rejection**, to **personal ridicule**, to **heresy**. Even if several anecdotes were inaccurate, the compiler's arrangement is itself evidence: he wanted readers to understand Abū Ḥanīfa as a religious problem, not simply as a rival jurist.

The distinction matters. Opposition to a legal opinion is part of normal juristic disagreement. Presenting another Muslim as an infectious threat to the religion is an effort to **exclude** that person from legitimate religious authority.

## 3. The Quotations Were Worse Than Disagreement

The sources offer far more than one colorful insult. They preserve different forms of hostility attributed to scholars from Kūfa, Baṣra, Medina, and Syria.

### His own teacher allegedly called him an unbeliever

Ḥammād b. Abī Sulaymān was Abū Ḥanīfa's teacher. Yet a report transmitted through al-Thawrī has Ḥammād object to the idea that the Qurʾān is created and instruct someone to confront Abū Ḥanīfa:

<HadithBlock label="Ḥammād b. Abī Sulaymān — reported denunciation" source="al-Sunna, no. 241, 1:184–185 (4012)" isnad="Isḥāq b. Abī Yaʿqūb → Aḥmad b. ʿAbd Allāh b. Yūnus → Sulaym al-Muqriʾ → Sufyān al-Thawrī → Ḥammād" translation="Are you not astonished at Abū Ḥanīfa saying, 'The Qurʾān is created'? Tell him: 'You unbeliever, you heretic!'" arabic="أَلَا تَعْجَبُ مِنْ أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ يَقُولُ: الْقُرْآنُ مَخْلُوقٌ، قُلْ لَهُ يَا كَافِرُ يَا زِنْدِيقُ" />

This does **not** independently prove that Abū Ḥanīfa professed the createdness of the Qurʾān. It proves that a later collection transmitted an accusation in precisely those terms, with a claimed chain back to his teacher. The distinction is especially significant because controversies about God's speech and the created Qurʾān became defining markers of religious loyalty in the ninth century.[^6]

### Ayyūb allegedly treated him as contagious

<HadithBlock label="Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī — the scabies episode" source="al-Sunna, no. 253, 1:188–189 (4012)" isnad="Saʿīd b. ʿĀmir → Sallām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → reported episode involving Ayyūb" translation="When Ayyūb saw Abū Ḥanīfa approaching, he said to his companions: 'Get up, so he does not infect us with his scabies! Get up, so he does not infect us with his scabies!'" arabic="فَلَمَّا رَآهُ أَيُّوبُ قَالَ لِأَصْحَابِهِ: قُومُوا لَا يُعْدِنَا بِجَرَبِهِ، قُومُوا لَا يُعْدِنَا بِجَرَبِهِ" />

The original situates the alleged encounter in the Sacred Mosque. The imagery is deliberate: association with Abū Ḥanīfa is described not as intellectual error but as exposure to disease. A nearby report attributed to Ibn ʿAwn calls him the most ill-omened person born among Muslims.[^7]

### Mālik allegedly called him an incurable disease

<HadithBlock label="Mālik b. Anas — 'an incurable disease'" source="al-Sunna, no. 295, 1:199–200 (4012); compare Ibn ʿAdī, al-Kāmil, 8:237 (1372)" translation="Abū Ḥanīfa is among the incurable diseases ... Abū Ḥanīfa undoes the traditions." arabic="أَبُو حَنِيفَةَ مِنَ الدَّاءِ الْعُضَالِ ... أَبُو حَنِيفَةَ يَنْقُضُ السُّنَنَ" />

In another attributed exchange, Mālik asks whether people in the listener's town even mention Abū Ḥanīfa. On receiving an affirmative answer, he replies that such a town should not be inhabited. Other sources transmit related anti-Ḥanafī imagery. But the same words appearing in *al-Sunna*, Ibn ʿAdī, and *Tārīkh Baghdād* do not by themselves represent three independent observations: all chains must be compared.[^8]

### Sharīk preferred wine sellers to his followers

<HadithBlock label="Sharīk b. ʿAbd Allāh — the wine-seller comparison" source="al-Sunna, no. 305, 1:203 (4012)" translation="Having a wine seller selling wine in every quarter of Kūfa would be better than having someone there who follows the opinion of Abū Ḥanīfa." arabic="لَأَنْ يَكُونَ فِي كُلِّ رَبْعٍ مِنْ أَرْبَاعِ الْكُوفَةِ خَمَّارٌ يَبِيعُ الْخَمْرَ خَيْرٌ مِنْ أَنْ يَكُونَ فِيهِ مَنْ يَقُولُ بِقَوْلِ أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ" />

This last report turns an abstract disagreement into a social judgment: even the physical presence of Abū Ḥanīfa's followers becomes worse than a publicly condemned trade. These were not innocuous remarks about legal analogy.[^9]

This is a catalogue of **attributions preserved by a hostile compilation**, not six separate historical facts certified by their presence on the page. Still, the breadth of allegations shows what readers were being invited to believe.[^10]

## 4. The Death of Abū Ḥanīfa Becomes a Cause for Celebration

Perhaps the most arresting report concerns the news of Abū Ḥanīfa's death. *Tārīkh Baghdād* records a version attributed to Sufyān al-Thawrī:

<HadithBlock label="The death announcement attributed to al-Thawrī" source="al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī, Tārīkh Baghdād, 15:548–549, biography 7249 (5602)" isnad="Al-Khaṭīb's routes converge on Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād → Ibrāhīm b. Muḥammad al-Fazārī → alleged reaction of Sufyān al-Thawrī." translation="Praise be to God, who has relieved the Muslims of him. He used to undo the bonds of Islam, bond by bond. No one more ill-omened for the people of Islam was ever born into Islam." arabic="الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ الَّذِي أَرَاحَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ مِنْهُ، لَقَدْ كَانَ يَنْقُضُ عُرَى الْإِسْلَامِ عُرْوَةً عُرْوَةً، مَا وُلِدَ فِي الْإِسْلَامِ مَوْلُودٌ أَشْأَمَ عَلَى أَهْلِ الْإِسْلَامِ مِنْهُ" />

Read those words carefully. They do not merely accuse Abū Ḥanīfa of making mistakes. They portray his **death as a benefit to the Muslim community**. Even the imagery—undoing Islam one bond after another—is meant to make him appear as a continuous danger to the religion.[^11]

Yet the corpus immediately gives us a reason not to turn this dramatic scene into unqualified biography. Variants of the same formula occur elsewhere with **al-Awzāʿī**, **Ibn ʿAwn**, **Sufyān b. ʿUyayna**, and other alleged speakers. Some versions contain the death announcement; others use the “most ill-omened person born in Islam” phrase independently. Ibn ʿAdī preserves a shorter version through material involving al-Bukhārī and Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād. In *al-Sunna* no. 324, the death reaction is attributed instead to al-Awzāʿī.[^12]

**This is precisely where counting quotations ceases to be serious historical work.** Five appearances of a formula do not necessarily mean five independent witnesses to a real event. A phrase can be reused; one later student can distribute it under different authorities; different encounters can also generate similar rhetoric. The task is to reconstruct each isnād and compare its wording, not simply add the citations together.

In the supplied al-ʿUqaylī entry, one early report involving Ḍirār b. Ṣurad is explicitly followed by **<Arabic>ضرار ليس بثقة</Arabic>**—“Ḍirār is not reliable.” That is a warning within the very book that preserves the accusation.[^13]

<ContextNote title="What this does—and does not—prove" collapsible>

The distribution above supports a demonstrable conclusion: **later transmitters circulated a recognizable repertoire of anti-Abū Ḥanīfa formulas**. It does not yet identify a fabricator or prove which alleged speaker, if any, originated each saying. In particular, the appearance of Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād or Ibrāhīm al-Fazārī in an important strand is a subject for isnād comparison, not evidence by itself that either invented the statement. The source corpus is extensive, but an exhaustive isnād-cum-matn reconstruction remains a separate task.

</ContextNote>

## 5. What Was the Actual Argument? Reports Against Legal Reasoning

The most useful answer is preserved by the antagonists themselves. In a report attributed to al-Awzāʿī, he explains why they objected to Abū Ḥanīfa:

<HadithBlock label="Al-Awzāʿī explains the complaint" source="al-Sunna, no. 326, 1:208 (4012)" translation="We do not fault Abū Ḥanīfa for having an opinion; all of us have opinions. What we fault him for is that a hadith from the Messenger of God is mentioned to him, and he gives a legal opinion contrary to it." arabic="إِنَّا لَا نَنْقِمُ عَلَى أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ الرَّأْيَ كُلُّنَا نَرَى، إِنَّمَا نَنْقِمُ عَلَيْهِ أَنَّهُ يُذْكَرُ لَهُ الْحَدِيثُ عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ صَلَّى اللهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ فَيُفْتِي بِخِلَافِهِ" />

This is much more precise than the cliché that Abū Ḥanīfa simply “liked reason” whereas his opponents only “followed revelation.” Al-Awzāʿī's transmitted words concede that **everyone exercises judgment**. The dispute was about the authority of particular reported traditions over the formation of legal rules.[^14]

In another report, al-Awzāʿī is quoted saying that Abū Ḥanīfa “lost the foundations and turned to analogy” (<Arabic>ضيع الأصول وأقبل على القياس</Arabic>). But this report also has the distancing phrase <Arabic>بلغني</Arabic>—“it reached me”—in its route. The two statements should not be treated as equally direct evidence of al-Awzāʿī's own words merely because both appear in the same chapter.[^15]

### Ibn Abī Shayba did not just criticize him in passing

Ibn Abī Shayba (d. 235 AH) devoted an entire section of his *Muṣannaf* to **“The Refutation of Abū Ḥanīfa”** (<Arabic>كتاب الرد على أبي حنيفة</Arabic>). It begins with a programmatic claim:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Abī Shayba — chapter heading" source="al-Muṣannaf, Kitāb al-Radd ʿalā Abī Ḥanīfa, book 35 (Shamela 6996)">

<Arabic>هَذَا مَا خَالَفَ بِهِ أَبُو حَنِيفَةَ الْأَثَرَ الَّذِي جَاءَ عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ صَلَّى اللهُ عَلَيْهِ وَسَلَّمَ</Arabic>

“This is where Abū Ḥanīfa contradicted the transmitted report that came from the Messenger of God.”

</QuoteBlock>

In the **particular uploaded edition** used here, the refutation contains **61 titled legal issues**, populated by reports numbered **36049–36534**—486 consecutively numbered items. An earlier description counted 485; that difference should be resolved against the relevant editions before presenting either number as universally definitive. More importantly, **486 hadith entries are not 486 independently demonstrated mistakes**.[^16]

The very first issue concerns a Jewish man and woman alleged to have committed adultery. Ibn Abī Shayba lists multiple reports that the Prophet ordered stoning and then contrasts them with an opinion attributed to Abū Ḥanīfa:

<HadithBlock label="The opening legal dispute: the Jewish couple" source="Ibn Abī Shayba, al-Muṣannaf, nos. 36049–36053, p. 277 in supplied text (6996)" translation="It was mentioned that Abū Ḥanīfa said: 'There is no stoning upon them.'" arabic="وَذُكِرَ أَنَّ أَبَا حَنِيفَةَ قَالَ: لَيْسَ عَلَيْهِمَا رَجْمٌ" />

The phrase **<Arabic>وذكر أن</Arabic>**—“and it was mentioned that”—is important. Ibn Abī Shayba places a legal opinion beside multiple hadith reports, but this is **his presentation of a conflict**. It does not, without external controls, tell us exactly how Abū Ḥanīfa formulated, qualified, or defended that opinion, nor whether all of the opposing reports represent equally early evidence.[^17]

And the disagreements are not restricted to criminal law. His refutation continues through ritual practice, charity, transactions, and other legal topics. The historian can say with confidence that **a substantial early legal polemic against Abū Ḥanīfa existed**. The historian cannot turn every cited report into an unquestioned Prophetic fact merely because Ibn Abī Shayba positioned it against him.

## 6. The Accusations Became Theological—and Political

By the third century, the anti-Abū Ḥanīfa repertoire did not stop at law. *Al-Sunna* repeats claims that he had to repent of unbelief, that he supported religiously suspect ideas about faith, and that he participated in controversies concerning the createdness of the Qurʾān.

<HadithBlock label="The repeated-repentance allegation" source="al-Sunna, no. 268, 1:193 (4012); compare al-ʿUqaylī, entry 1876 (1564)" isnad="ʿUbayd Allāh b. Muʿādh → his father Muʿādh → Sufyān al-Thawrī" translation="Abū Ḥanīfa was made to repent of unbelief twice." arabic="اسْتُتِيبَ أَبُو حَنِيفَةَ مِنَ الْكُفْرِ مَرَّتَيْنِ" />

Other versions describe multiple episodes of repentance, sometimes using the word *zandaqa* (heresy), and one dramatically depicts Abū Ḥanīfa seized by the beard and marched through scholarly gatherings. These are accusations of **public humiliation and compromised belief**, not neutral evaluations of a hadith narrator.[^18]

In the same compilation, a report attributed to Abū Yūsuf asserts that Abū Ḥanīfa “died a Jahmī,” while another attributes to him the extraordinary assertion that Abū Ḥanīfa was the first to say the Qurʾān was created. Those are **traditions about a student's denunciation of his teacher**. Because contrary depictions of their relationship also survive, they should be tested rather than automatically accepted as proof of the teacher's actual creed.[^19]

The political material is another revealing dimension. Al-ʿUqaylī preserves an account of Abū Ḥanīfa allegedly encouraging two men to go out and join **Ibrāhīm's uprising**. Other denunciations label him someone who “held the sword” (<Arabic>يرى السيف</Arabic>), an idiom associated in these discussions with supporting armed action. Ahmad Khan shows that the relationship between accusations of heresy and perceptions of rebellion deserves its own analysis.[^20]

The mechanism is understandable. A rival jurist can be presented as wrong about evidence, dangerous in theology, and unsafe in politics. Once these allegations are collected together, the subject no longer appears as a person with disputable arguments. He appears as someone **outside the safe boundaries of the community**. That is the work this literature was capable of doing.

## 7. When Criticism Became a Claim of Consensus

Later biographical critics did not merely preserve hostile anecdotes. Some writers framed hostility to Abū Ḥanīfa as the settled verdict of learned Islam.

Ibn Ḥibbān's *al-Majrūḥīn* (entry 1127 in the uploaded edition) describes Abū Ḥanīfa as a man of disputation and outward piety, but claims that he was not professionally competent in hadith. Ibn Ḥibbān alleges that out of 130 musnad reports, Abū Ḥanīfa erred in 120, either by changing chains or wording unintentionally. He then says that the religious authorities across the regions criticized him, with only occasional exceptions.[^21]

<HadithBlock label="Ibn Ḥibbān's sweeping judgment" source="Ibn Ḥibbān, al-Majrūḥīn, entry 1127, 3:63–64 (4973)" translation="He transmitted 130 musnad reports ... and erred in 120 of them ... The imams of the Muslims and people of religious scrupulousness, in all the cities and regions, criticized him and spoke against him, except for one person here and another there." arabic="حَدَّثَ بِمِائَةٍ وَثَلَاثِينَ حَدِيثًا مَسَانِيدَ ... أَخْطَأَ مِنْهَا فِي مِائَةٍ وَعِشْرِينَ حَدِيثًا ... عَلَى أَنَّ أَئِمَّةَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ وَأَهْلَ الْوَرَعِ فِي الدِّينِ فِي جَمِيعِ الْأَمْصَارِ وَسَائِرِ الْأَقْطَارِ جَرَحُوهُ وَأَطْلَقُوا عَلَيْهِ الْقَدْحَ إِلَّا الْوَاحِدَ بَعْدَ الْوَاحِدِ" />

Notice how two different assertions have been brought together. One is a purported assessment of **particular hadith transmissions**. The other is a claim about **the consensus of entire regions**. Neither claim becomes a modern statistical fact just because the author expressed it numerically or emphatically. To test the 120-of-130 calculation, we would need his complete sample and judgments. To test the regional claim, we would need to count and evaluate **both negative and favorable evidence**.

The problem with taking the consensus claim literally is that the same broader literature preserves unmistakably positive testimonies.

## 8. The Same Sources Preserve the Opposite Picture

*Tārīkh Baghdād*, despite its enormous collection of hostile quotations, also transmits a very different scene. A man tells the famous hadith critic **Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn** that Abū Ḥanīfa is a liar. Yaḥyā refuses the accusation:

<HadithBlock label="Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn rejects the charge of lying" source="al-Khaṭīb, Tārīkh Baghdād, 15:580, biography 7249 (5602)" isnad="al-Khaṭīb → al-Ṭabarī → al-Bayḍāwī → al-Raqqī → ʿAbbās al-Dūrī → Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn" translation="Abū Ḥanīfa was too noble to lie. He was truthful, although his hadith has the difficulties found in the hadith of other shaykhs." arabic="كَانَ أَبُو حَنِيفَةَ أَنْبَلَ مِنْ أَنْ يَكْذِبَ، كَانَ صَدُوقًا إِلَّا أَنَّ فِي حَدِيثِهِ مَا فِي حَدِيثِ الشُّيُوخِ" />

This response is revealing because it **distinguishes an imperfect transmitter from a dishonest one**. A critic can object to some of a jurist's hadith without branding him a liar. The same biography also records Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Qaṭṭān as saying that he had heard no opinion better than Abū Ḥanīfa's and that he had adopted many of his positions.[^22]

Then there is an attribution to **al-Shāfiʿī**, the founder of another school later placed beside Abū Ḥanīfa's own:

<HadithBlock label="Al-Shāfiʿī — the counter-tradition" source="al-Khaṭīb, Tārīkh Baghdād, biography 7249, favorable section, 15:473–475 (5602)" translation="People are dependent on Abū Ḥanīfa in jurisprudence." arabic="النَّاسُ عِيَالٌ عَلَى أَبِي حَنِيفَةَ فِي الْفِقْهِ" />

Both a favorable and an unfavorable attribution can be historically fragile. Neither should be given an exemption from source criticism because it suits someone's preferred image. But their coexistence defeats the simple story that every respected early Muslim scholar uniformly detested Abū Ḥanīfa.[^23]

The biographical conflict even reaches his **ancestry**. *Tārīkh Baghdād* collects accounts that place his family's origins in different lands, describe his father as once enslaved, and elsewhere preserve testimony from a descendant insisting upon free Persian ancestry. These are contradictory claims, not a settled genealogical verdict. Khan's examination of the material shows why foreignness and descent could be deployed in disputes over religious status.[^24]

One particularly telling detail is that the same *Tārīkh Baghdād* entry preserves the rejection of the allegation that Abū Ḥanīfa lied alongside reports attributing that allegation to Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal. A medieval compiler did not have to choose between them. A modern reader **does** have to confront the contradiction, rather than pretending the negative quotation stands alone.

## 9. How a Heretic Became a Sunni Imam

The change did not require the physical erasure of the earlier hostile reports. Many survived and are available for us to read today. What changed was the **framework in which Abū Ḥanīfa was remembered**.

Khan's study traces the later construction of a more accommodating historical identity through two especially important genres: **manāqib**, narratives of a scholar's virtues, and **masānīd**, hadith collections arranged around his purported transmissions. Both helped furnish the figure of Abū Ḥanīfa with attributes that a mature Sunni tradition could recognize: piety, legal brilliance, respect for hadith, sound religious identity, and continuity with earlier generations. Khan documents this process rather than assuming that acceptance followed automatically from the passage of time.[^25]

This is one reason the evidence must be read in **both directions**. A hostile early source can craft a heretic. A later celebratory source can craft a saintly imam. Neither genre is an unfiltered recording of the second century. And yet the tension between them is exactly what makes the historical transformation visible.

For comparison, three biographies listed in the larger digital catalog deserve direct study: al-Ṣaymarī's *Akhbār Abī Ḥanīfa wa-Aṣḥābihi* (Shamela ID 5323); Ibn Abī al-ʿAwwām's *Faḍāʾil Abī Ḥanīfa wa-Akhbāruhu* (23995); and Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr's *al-Intiqāʾ* (5366). Their specific contents were **not** part of the ten full-text books examined here, so this article does not claim to have established each work's individual transmission history. They are the next needed control corpus.[^26]

<HadithCriticBlog id="origins-early-history/30-imam-abu-hanifa-false-attributions" description="A related HadithCritic investigation into later works attributed to Abū Ḥanīfa, including disputed theological books and collections of his hadith." />

There is an irony here. The more forcefully early polemicists presented Abū Ḥanīfa as incompatible with their understanding of Islam, the more work subsequent scholars had to do to create a Sunni past in which he belonged. That process did not merely rescue one man's reputation. It helped **define what Sunni orthodoxy would eventually include**.

## 10. The Surviving Books Do Not Permit a Simple Verdict

Some defenders of Abū Ḥanīfa might wish to dismiss every hostile tradition as fabrication. Some of his critics might wish to accept every accusation as proven history. **Neither move follows from the sources.**

The evidence supports a stronger conclusion than either partisan shortcut. Major medieval collectors deliberately assembled and circulated hostile material about a scholar whom Sunni Muslims would later venerate. The hostility was expressed through particular legal disputes, but it grew into accusations about his belief, legitimacy, morality, influence, and political danger. Other transmitters preserved praise that cannot simply be discarded without explanation.

<QuoteBlock author="The issue" source="Synthesis of the ten-book source study">

The question is not merely whether Abū Ḥanīfa was criticized. He was—at length, by name, and sometimes with extraordinary cruelty in the surviving literature. The deeper question is **who gained the authority to decide that a scholar once depicted as a destroyer of Islam belonged among Islam's great imams**.

</QuoteBlock>

The Qurʾān asks its readers to investigate reports rather than surrender their judgment to the status of a transmitter. This historical episode offers an unusually concrete reason why that discipline matters. A chain of prestigious names does not erase contradictions. The label *imam* cannot retroactively authenticate every flattering biography; the label *heretic* cannot authenticate every condemnation.[^27]

<VerdictBox title="The historical verdict" facts={[{ value: '150 AH', label: 'Abū Ḥanīfa died' }, { value: '290 AH', label: 'ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad died' }, { value: '463 AH', label: 'al-Khaṭīb died' }]}>

The familiar picture of four mutually compatible Sunni imams is a **later historical achievement**, not an adequate description of every early scholar's attitude toward Abū Ḥanīfa. The primary sources reveal a conflict over transmitted authority and the boundaries of religious legitimacy. The survival of contradictory reports shows why we must distinguish **a medieval writer's account of the past** from **what independent historical evidence establishes about that past**.

</VerdictBox>
