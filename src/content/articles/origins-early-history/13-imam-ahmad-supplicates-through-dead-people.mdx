<ClaimBox title="Thesis">

The problem is not merely that later Sunnis praised the Prophet, honored relics, or loved saints. The problem is that Sunni legal and devotional literature preserved practices in which created beings become religious channels: unseen servants are addressed for help, prophetic relics are touched for blessing, and the Prophet is used as a means in supplication after his death. Imam Aḥmad is important because he stands near the beginning of the Sunni legal canon, yet material attached to him already shows the very intermediary logic that the Qurʾān condemns.

</ClaimBox>

This article does not need to prove every later grave-practice from Imam Aḥmad. That would be too broad, and it would give defenders an easy way out. The stronger case is narrower. Aḥmad’s own transmitted practice and later Hanbalī reports show three categories that Sunni tradition struggled to contain: calling upon unseen servants, seeking blessing through physical contact with prophetic traces, and making tawassul through the Prophet. Later scholars then expanded the same logic into open defenses of istighātha through the Prophet and righteous figures.

The Qurʾānic issue is simple. The Quraysh did not deny God as Creator. Their error was that they placed created beings between themselves and God, claiming that these beings brought them closer to Him or interceded for them. Once that pattern is identified, changing the object from idols to prophets, angels, graves, relics, or saints does not solve the problem. It preserves the structure of the problem while giving it Islamic language.

## The Qurʾānic Baseline: Intermediaries Were the Problem

The Qurʾān records that the pagan opponents of Muhammad already acknowledged God as the Creator of the heavens and the earth:

<QuranVerse verse="31:25" />

Their error was devotional and mediatory. They placed other beings into the religious space between themselves and God, then explained that these beings were only a means of nearness:

<QuranVerse verse="39:3" />

The same logic appears in the language of intercession:

<QuranVerse verse="10:18" />

<ContextNote title="The Standard Used Here">

This article uses the Qurʾānic standard rather than the later sectarian vocabulary of the madhhabs. A practice is not purified merely because the person says, “God is the real actor.” The Quraysh also placed their intermediaries beneath God. The Qurʾān rejects the act of invoking and religiously relying upon created intermediaries, even when those intermediaries are framed as channels to God.

</ContextNote>

## 1. Imam Aḥmad Calling Upon the “Servants of God”

The clearest report is preserved by al-Bayhaqī in <em>al-Jāmiʿ li-Shuʿab al-Īmān</em>. The chain runs through ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad, who says he heard his father describe what he did when he lost his way during pilgrimage.

<HadithBlock
  label="Aḥmad's Own Practice"
  source="al-Bayhaqī, al-Jāmiʿ li-Shuʿab al-Īmān, no. 7298 in one common numbering; also cited from Masāʾil al-Imām Aḥmad, riwāyat ʿAbd Allāh"
  arabic={`أخبرنا أبو عبد الله الحافظ، أخبرنا أحمد بن سلمان الفقيه ببغداد، حدثنا عبد الله بن أحمد بن حنبل، قال: سمعت أبي يقول: حججت خمس حجج، منها اثنتان راكبًا وثلاثة ماشيًا، أو ثلاثة راكبًا واثنتان ماشيًا، فضللت الطريق في حجة وكنت ماشيًا فجعلت أقول: يا عباد الله، دلوني على الطريق. قال: فلم أزل أقول ذلك حتى وقفت على الطريق، أو كما قال أبي.`}
  translation={`Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Ḥākim informed us; Aḥmad b. Salmān the jurist in Baghdad informed us; ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal narrated to us, saying: I heard my father say: “I performed five pilgrimages: two riding and three walking, or three riding and two walking. I lost the road during one pilgrimage while I was walking, so I began saying: ‘O servants of God, guide me to the road.’ I continued saying that until I came upon the road,” or as my father said.`}
/>

<IsnadDiagram
  width={900}

This report does not describe Aḥmad asking God directly. It describes him addressing “servants of God” in the vocative: <span dir="rtl" lang="ar">يا عباد الله</span>. The request is also direct: <span dir="rtl" lang="ar">دلوني على الطريق</span>, “guide me to the road.” The wording is softer than “save me,” but the religious structure is the same: a person in distress calls upon unseen created beings for practical help.

The background to Aḥmad’s action is the well-known report attributed to the Prophet about calling upon the “servants of God” when one is lost or in need of assistance in a deserted place.

<HadithBlock
  label="The Formula Behind the Practice"
  source="al-Ṭabarānī, al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr, 17:117-118, report of ʿUtba b. Ghazwān"
  arabic={`إذا أضل أحدكم شيئًا أو أراد أحدكم عونًا وهو بأرض ليس بها أنيس، فليقل: يا عباد الله أغيثوني، يا عباد الله أغيثوني، فإن لله عبادًا لا نراهم.`}
  translation={`If one of you loses something, or one of you needs assistance while he is in a land where there is no companion, let him say: “O servants of God, help me! O servants of God, help me!” For God has servants whom we do not see.`}
/>

This report is disputed. Some strengthen it through supporting routes; others, including al-Albānī and Salafi writers, weaken it and argue that it cannot justify calling upon dead saints. That defensive move still concedes the point that matters here. On the restrictive reading, the “servants of God” are not idols and not dead saints, but angels or jinn—created unseen beings. The practice still teaches a person to call upon a created being who is not visibly present.

<ContextNote title="Why the Angel/Jinn Defense Does Not Solve the Qurʾānic Problem">

Defenders often say that the report refers to angels or unseen attendants, not the dead. That may protect the report from one kind of grave-cult argument, but it does not protect it from the Qurʾānic critique of invocation. The issue is not whether the invoked being is made of stone, flesh, light, or fire. The issue is that a created being is addressed for rescue in a religious formula.

</ContextNote>

The Qurʾān does not build a devotional category in which believers call upon hidden created agents whenever ordinary help is absent. It directs fear, reliance, worship, and supplication back to God.

<QuranVerse verse="72:20-21" />

<QuranVerse verse="35:14" />

## 2. Touching the Minbar, the Grave, and the Problem of Tabarruk

The second report comes from <em>al-ʿIlal wa-Maʿrifat al-Rijāl</em> through ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad. In the printed wording, Aḥmad is asked about a man touching the Prophet’s minbar, seeking blessing by touching and kissing it, and doing something similar at the grave while intending nearness to God. Aḥmad replies that there is no harm in it.

<HadithBlock
  label="Report in al-ʿIlal"
  source="ʿAbd Allāh b. Aḥmad, al-ʿIlal wa-Maʿrifat al-Rijāl, no. 3243 in the printed numbering"
  arabic={`سألته عن الرجل يمس منبر النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم، ويتبرك بمسه، ويقبله، ويفعل بالقبر مثل ذلك أو نحو هذا، يريد بذلك التقرب إلى الله جل وعز، فقال: لا بأس بذلك.`}
  translation={`I asked him about a man who touches the minbar of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, seeks blessing by touching it, kisses it, and does something like that—or something similar—at the grave, intending by that to draw near to God, Mighty and Exalted. He said: “There is no harm in that.”`}
/>

This report has to be handled carefully because conservative Hanbalī and Salafi writers challenge the grave portion. They argue that the wording is strange, that the Prophet’s grave was not accessible for touching in the way suggested, and that stronger Hanbalī transmissions restrict Aḥmad’s permission to the minbar or the <em>rummāna</em>, the rounded fixture or place connected to where the Prophet sat and placed his hand.

That defense weakens one part of the polemical claim but strengthens another. Even when the grave is removed, the concession remains that Aḥmad allowed blessing to be sought through contact with a physical prophetic trace. The disagreement becomes a boundary dispute: minbar yes, grave no; original relic yes, later walls no; surviving prophetic trace yes, ordinary shrine no. A Qurʾān-first critique does not need to accept the shrine version to see the problem. Once blessing can be materially accessed through relic-contact, the logic of devotional mediation has already entered the system.

The Qurʾān repeatedly challenges exactly this kind of religious displacement. Human beings create an object, location, person, or invisible agent that becomes the practical focus of religious dependence. The name of God remains in the theology, but the devotional action is directed elsewhere.

<QuranVerse verse="46:5-6" />

## 3. Aḥmad and Tawassul Through the Prophet

The third strand is not about a grave or an emergency formula. It is legal. al-Mardāwī, summarizing the Hanbalī school in <em>al-Inṣāf</em>, states that tawassul through a righteous man is permissible according to the correct view of the madhhab, with another view saying it is recommended. He then cites Aḥmad in connection with al-Marwadhī and tawassul through the Prophet.

<HadithBlock
  label="Hanbalī Legal Position"
  source="al-Mardāwī, al-Inṣāf fī Maʿrifat al-Rājiḥ min al-Khilāf, Book of Prayer, chapter of istisqāʾ, 2:456"
  arabic={`ومنها: يجوز التوسل بالرجل الصالح، على الصحيح من المذهب، وقيل: يستحب. قال الإمام أحمد: المروذي يتوسل بالنبي صلى الله عليه وسلم في دعائه، وجزم به في المستوعب وغيره.`}
  translation={`Among the points is that it is permissible to make tawassul through a righteous man, according to the correct view of the school; and it is said that it is recommended. Imam Aḥmad said: al-Marwadhī may make tawassul through the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, in his supplication; and this was affirmed decisively in al-Mustawʿib and other works.`}
/>

A defender may reply that this tawassul is not direct worship of the Prophet because the supplication is still addressed to God. That is the standard Sunni defense. It is also why the Quraysh comparison matters. The Qurʾān does not say that the pagan error consisted only in believing idols created the universe. It says they used other beings as channels of nearness and intercession.

The Hanbalī passage also contains an internal warning sign. al-Mardāwī notes that Ibn Taymiyya treated this kind of tawassul like the question of swearing by the Prophet. In other words, the practice had to be explained, qualified, or restricted because it sat near the boundary of invoking created status in religious speech. That boundary is not invented by modern polemics. It appears inside the Sunni debate itself.

<ContextNote title="Tawassul and Istighātha Are Not Identical, but They Share a Structure">

Tawassul usually means asking God by means of someone’s status, righteousness, supplication, or relation to God. Istighātha is more direct: “O so-and-so, help me.” The distinction matters legally, but the Qurʾānic critique reaches the shared structure: a created being is inserted into the act of appeal as a religious channel.

</ContextNote>

<QuranVerse verse="1:5" />

## 4. Later Sunni Scholars Made the Logic Explicit

The earlier material around Aḥmad gives the post its historical force. Later Sunni scholars show where that logic leads when expressed openly. Taqī al-Dīn al-Subkī, in <em>Shifāʾ al-Siqām</em>, treats tawassul, istighātha, and seeking intercession through the Prophet as permissible and commendable.

<HadithBlock
  label="al-Subkī's Defense"
  source="Taqī al-Dīn al-Subkī, Shifāʾ al-Siqām fī Ziyārat Khayr al-Anām, chapter on tawassul, istighātha, and tashaffuʿ"
  arabic={`اعلم أنه يجوز ويحسن التوسل والاستغاثة والتشفع بالنبي صلى الله عليه وسلم إلى ربه سبحانه وتعالى، وجواز ذلك وحسنه من الأمور المعلومة لكل ذي دين، المعروفة من فعل الأنبياء والمرسلين، وسير السلف الصالحين، والعلماء، والعوام من المسلمين. ولم ينكر أحد ذلك من أهل الأديان ولا سمع به في زمن من الأزمان حتى جاء ابن تيمية.`}
  translation={`Know that it is permissible and good to seek tawassul, istighātha, and intercession through the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, to his Lord, exalted is He. The permissibility and goodness of this are matters known to every religious person, recognized from the actions of the prophets and messengers, the practice of the righteous predecessors, the scholars, and the common Muslims. No one from the people of religion denied this, nor was such denial heard in any age, until Ibn Taymiyya came.`}
/>

Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī gives the same logic in a more systematic form. God is the real source of help, he says, while the Prophet is a means or intermediary through whom help is sought in a secondary or causal sense.

<HadithBlock
  label="Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī's Explanation"
  source="Ibn Ḥajar al-Haytamī, al-Jawhar al-Munaẓẓam; quoted in later discussions of Shafīʿī istighātha"
  arabic={`والمستغاث به في الحقيقة هو الله تعالى، وأما النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم فهو واسطة بينه وبين المستغيث، فهو سبحانه وتعالى مستغاث به حقيقة، والغوث منه خلقًا وإيجادًا، والنبي صلى الله عليه وسلم مستغاث به مجازًا، والغوث منه تسببًا وكسبًا.`}
  translation={`The one sought for help in reality is God Most High. As for the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, he is an intermediary between Him and the one seeking help. God, exalted is He, is the one sought for help in reality, and help comes from Him by creation and bringing into existence. The Prophet is sought for help figuratively, and help comes from him by causation and acquisition.`}
/>

This is the exact theological move the Qurʾān does not allow. The defender says, “God is the real actor; the Prophet or saint is only a means.” The Qurʾān already quotes the intermediary defense and rejects it.

<QuranVerse verse="39:3" />

<QuranVerse verse="10:18" />

## 5. The Salafi Escape Route Exposes the Inconsistency

Modern Salafi writers often condemn istighātha through the dead as major shirk. On that point, their Qurʾānic instinct is closer to the argument of this article than the later devotional Sunni tradition. Their difficulty is that the Sunni hadith and legal canon they inherit contains material that does not fit their theology.

The result is a pattern of selective tightening. The “O servants of God” reports are weakened, or reinterpreted as angels and jinn. The <em>al-ʿIlal</em> grave wording becomes textually problematic, while the minbar permission is retained but fenced off. Aḥmad’s tawassul through the Prophet is explained as a disputed technical issue or analogized to swearing by the Prophet. Later Sunni istighātha is dismissed as deviation, even though its defenders cite earlier legal and devotional categories.

A Qurʾān-first critique does not need to defend Salafism against Sufism or Sufism against Salafism. Both remain inside a hadith-based framework that normalizes extra-Qurʾānic devotional categories. The Salafi tries to keep the hadith canon while cutting off its devotional consequences. The Sufi accepts the consequences and supplies theological language to defend them. The Qurʾān rejects the shared premise that created intermediaries belong inside worship and supplication.

## The Qurʾānic Verdict

The Qurʾān repeatedly returns the believer to God alone. It does not instruct believers to call upon unseen servants, seek blessing through relics, or place the Prophet’s posthumous status into the mechanics of duʿāʾ.

<QuranVerse verse="40:65" />

<QuranVerse verse="19:48" />

<QuranVerse verse="6:71" />

The final verse is especially useful because it frames the issue as a reversal after guidance. The believer has been shown the direct path to God, then is invited back into calling upon what cannot benefit or harm him. That is exactly what intermediary religion does. It keeps the language of God while multiplying channels, patrons, holy objects, and unseen helpers.

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">

The material attached to Imam Aḥmad should not be used to make calling upon created beings acceptable. It should be used to expose a contradiction inside Sunni tradition. Aḥmad is remembered as an imam of strict Sunnism, yet reports and Hanbalī legal transmissions connected to him preserve practices that depend on intermediaries: unseen servants, prophetic traces, righteous men, and the Prophet’s status. The Qurʾān cuts through the entire system by directing supplication, reliance, and devotion to God alone.

</VerdictBox>
