The Sunni tradition has a desperate motivation to depict Muḥammad ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī (d. 124 AH) as a politically neutral and perfectly trustworthy transmitter of hadith. 

Al-Zuhrī is what modern academics call a "common link"—a super-proliferator of hadith. Countless narrations that form the very bedrock of Sunni law and theology exist solely because al-Zuhrī claimed to have heard them. If you weaken al-Zuhrī's credibility, you do not just discard one man; you collapse massive sections of the hadith corpus.

For this reason, later biographical dictionaries and modern apologists have worked tirelessly to portray al-Zuhrī as an ascetic scholar who was above political influence, downplaying or outright erasing his decades-long service to the Umayyad royal court. Modern defenders insist that his proximity to power was "incidental," that he had "freedom in the courts," and that accusations of being a Umayyad agent are simply Orientalist fabrications.

But the historical record—including testimony from his own students and contemporaries—strips away this hagiography. Al-Zuhrī was deeply embedded in the Umayyad administrative and ideological apparatus for decades, playing a key role in manufacturing religious justifications for state policies.

## The Jerusalem Doctrine: A Political Masterpiece

One of the clearest examples of al-Zuhrī’s service to the Umayyad political agenda is his role in promoting the sanctity of Jerusalem during the reign of Caliph ʿAbd al-Malik.

<ContextNote>
During the Second Fitna (civil war), the rival caliph ʿAbdallāh ibn al-Zubayr controlled Mecca. This created a massive political crisis for the Umayyads in Damascus: how could they maintain legitimacy when their subjects had to travel to rebel territory to perform the Hajj pilgrimage?
</ContextNote>

To solve this, ʿAbd al-Malik sought to elevate Jerusalem as an alternative sacred center. Massive state resources were poured into constructing the Dome of the Rock and expanding the al-Aqsa Mosque complex to establish a pilgrimage site firmly under Umayyad control. 

But architecture alone cannot change theology. The Umayyads needed religious justification. Al-Zuhrī served as the principal architect for this justification by transmitting the following famous hadith:

As scholars like Ignác Goldziher and Suleiman A. Mourad have documented, this was not an organic development in early Islamic piety. It was a deliberate state policy. Al-Zuhrī was given the task of justifying this politically motivated reform by attributing it retroactively to the Prophet. 

## The Reality of the "Napkin of the Rulers"

The apologetic claim that al-Zuhrī merely visited the court "from time to time" collapses entirely when we examine his resume. He was a full-time royal employee who:

1. Served as *qāḍī* (judge) under Caliph Yazid II.
2. Acted as the personal tutor to Hishām b. ʿAbd al-Malik’s royal sons.
3. Resided directly in the Umayyad court city of al-Ruṣāfah for nearly twenty years.
4. Dictated hadith directly to caliphal secretaries (sometimes under explicit royal orders) and permitted Umayyad officials to circulate material in his name without personally verifying its accuracy.

The skepticism regarding al-Zuhrī’s independence is not a modern Orientalist invention. It was voiced loudly and clearly by respected Muslim scholars in his own era.

These were not fringe voices, nor were they anti-Umayyad agitators. They were leading figures within the early transmission networks who recognized the severe moral and academic compromise of acting as a state theologian.

## Why The Modern Defense Fails

Modern apologists often lean on a classic fallacy to defend al-Zuhrī: they point out that he narrated some pro-ʿAli hadith early in his life, and assume this proves he maintained fierce political independence forever. 

This ignores the obvious reality of human nature and political evolution. Al-Zuhrī’s career spanned multiple decades across radically different political climates. Early transmissions from his youth in Medina cannot be used to whitewash the final twenty years of his life spent entrenched in the Umayyad royal court.

<VerdictBox title="The Final Verdict">
Al-Zuhrī’s hadith transmission forms a major artery in the Sunni isnād networks. Recognizing his political servitude is not an attack for its own sake—it is a necessary historical reality check. To continue venerating him as an infallible, neutral pillar of Sunni orthodoxy is a blind theological decision. Claiming his transmission was immune to Umayyad influence requires historical whitewashing of the highest degree.
</VerdictBox>
