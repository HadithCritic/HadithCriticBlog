The bewitchment report is usually defended by pointing to two different Companion authorities. One route comes through ʿĀʾisha by way of ʿUrwa and Hishām b. ʿUrwa. Another route comes through Zayd b. Arqam by way of Yazīd b. Ḥayyān and al-Aʿmash. On the surface, this looks like corroboration: a Medinan household route and a Kūfan Companion route both remember the Prophet being bewitched by a Jewish man.

That surface impression is too neat. The transmission map does not give us two equally strong independent memories. It gives us a broad Hishām-family dossier, a shorter mursal/collective al-Zuhrī layer, and a much thinner Kūfan collateral route whose recoverable form runs through al-Aʿmash and, below him, through Abū Muʿāwiya. The key question is not whether the report entered the canonical collections. It did. The key question is whether the Kūfan route truly reaches behind the Hishām dossier or whether it represents a secondary Iraqi reshaping of the same story-world.

<ClaimBox title="Core Claim">
The Zayd b. Arqam route should not be treated as clean independent confirmation of the Hishām b. ʿUrwa bewitchment report. Its matn shares the same narrative skeleton but removes the diagnostic Medinan details, anonymizes the Jewish agent, inserts ʿAlī as the one who retrieves and unties the knots, and is transmitted through al-Aʿmash by ʿanʿana. The strongest conclusion is careful: direct borrowing from Hishām cannot be proven, but the Kūfan route is too thin, too late in recoverable form, and too exposed to Iraqi cross-contamination to function as an independent second witness.
</ClaimBox>

## The Two Reports Are Not the Same Kind of Evidence

The longest and most influential version is the ʿĀʾisha report through Hishām b. ʿUrwa. Its most revealing form is not simply “Hishām from his father from ʿĀʾisha.” In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Ibn ʿUyayna says that Ibn Jurayj first narrated it from **the family of ʿUrwa** before he asked Hishām about it. That small isnād note matters. It shows that the story was already functioning as an ʿUrwa-family dossier, and that Hishām became the main transmitter who regularized it into the famous father-from-ʿĀʾisha chain.[^1]

The Zayd b. Arqam route is a different kind of artifact. It is shorter. It has no ʿĀʾisha interior scene, no named Labīd b. al-Aʿṣam, no Banū Zurayq frame, no comb-and-hair detail, no male date-palm spathe, no named Dharwān well, and no dialogue between two bedside visitors. Instead, Gabriel directly diagnoses the magic, the object is described as knots in an unnamed well, and ʿAlī is sent to retrieve and undo them.[^2]

The two reports therefore do not stand as two full narrative witnesses saying the same thing. They share a **story grammar**, not a stable detailed memory. The grammar is simple: a Jewish agent, hidden magic in a well, divine disclosure, physical removal, recovery, and no retaliation. Everything else shifts.

## The Early Non-Hishām Layer: al-Zuhrī, ʿUrwa, and Legal Memory

One reason the old “Hishām versus al-Aʿmash” framing is incomplete is that Hishām is not the only pre-canonical Medinan entry point. ʿAbd al-Razzāq preserves reports from Maʿmar, from al-Zuhrī, from Ibn al-Musayyib and ʿUrwa, in legal chapters on whether a sorcerer from the People of the Book is killed. These reports do not give the full bedside narrative. They preserve the legal residue of the story: the Jews of Banū Zurayq bewitched the Prophet, the magic was placed in a well, knots were extracted, and no one is reported to have been killed.[^3]

<HadithBlock
  label="The Legal Residue Before the Canonical Expansion"
  arabic={abdRazzaqArabic}
  translation={abdRazzaqTranslation}
  source="ʿAbd al-Razzāq, al-Muṣannaf, no. 19764; cf. nos. 10018 and 19395"
/>

This al-Zuhrī layer is important because it complicates both apologetic and polemical readings. It does not prove the event. It does prove that the story had already entered a **Medinan legal-memory environment** before the later canonical form. In that environment, the issue was not primarily “can a prophet be bewitched?” but “if a Jewish group bewitched the Prophet, why was no sorcerer killed?” That is why ʿAbd al-Razzāq places it under chapters about killing the sorcerer of the People of the Book.

The same Muṣannaf also preserves a loose report through Maʿmar from ʿAṭāʾ al-Khurāsānī from Yaḥyā b. Maʿmar: the Prophet was held back from ʿĀʾisha for a year, two angels diagnosed the magic, the well is unnamed, and the magic is extracted.[^4] This is not a strong musnad report, but it shows that the story’s parts were circulating in modular form: ʿĀʾisha, angelic diagnosis, a well, extraction, and knots could be rearranged without the full Hishām wording.

<ContextNote title="Why This Matters">
A report can be early in circulation and still historically unstable. The al-Zuhrī and Yaḥyā b. Maʿmar material shows that the bewitchment story was not born only in Bukhārī’s final form. It also shows that the narrative was fluid: legal memory, family memory, angelic diagnosis, and well-magic motifs circulated in separable pieces.
</ContextNote>

## Matn Analysis: Same Skeleton, Different Narrative Politics

The matn comparison is where the problem becomes visible. If the Zayd route were simply an independent Companion memory of the same event, one might expect some of the highly diagnostic details to survive: Labīd’s name, Banū Zurayq, the comb, the hair-remnants, the male date-palm spathe, the well of Dharwān, or the two visitors speaking at the Prophet’s bedside. Instead, the Zayd route preserves the broad skeleton and replaces the details most tied to the ʿĀʾisha household setting.

This is not random drift. The Zayd version looks like a **Kūfanized digest** of the broader story: shorter, less sexually explicit, less tied to ʿĀʾisha’s domestic vantage point, and more ʿAlī-centered. That does not make it automatically fabricated, but it prevents it from functioning as a clean independent witness to the Hishām report.

## Hishām b. ʿUrwa: Reliable, but the Iraqi Phase Matters

Hishām b. ʿUrwa is not a weak narrator in the simple sense. Ibn Saʿd and al-ʿIjlī called him trustworthy; Abū Ḥātim called him a trustworthy imām in hadith; Ibn Ḥibbān described him as precise, devout, virtuous, and a memorizer.[^5] The question is not whether Hishām is disposable. The question is whether the exact type of report under study fits the precise zone classical critics themselves flagged: his later Iraqi transmission from his father.

Ibn Ḥajar’s entry in Tahdhīb al-Tahdhīb preserves the problem clearly. Yaʿqūb b. Shayba says that nothing was criticized from Hishām except after he went to Iraq, where he expanded his narration from his father. Ibn Kharrāsh reports that Mālik objected to Hishām’s reports for the Iraqis and describes a shift over three Kufan visits: first “my father told me: I heard ʿĀʾisha,” then “my father informed me from ʿĀʾisha,” then simply “my father from ʿĀʾisha.”[^6]

<HadithBlock label="The Iraqi-Phase Warning" source="Ibn Ḥajar, Tahdhīb al-Tahdhīb, entry on Hishām b. ʿUrwa">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وقال يعقوب بن شيبة: ثقة ثبت لم ينكر عليه شيء إلا بعد ما صار إلى العراق، فإنه انبسط في الرواية عن أبيه، فأنكر ذلك عليه أهل بلده ... وقال ابن خراش: كان مالك لا يرضاه ... قدم الكوفة ثلاث مرات: قدمة كان يقول: حدثني أبي قال: سمعت عائشة، وقدم الثانية فكان يقول: أخبرني أبي عن عائشة، وقدم الثالثة فكان يقول: أبي عن عائشة.</div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">Yaʿqūb b. Shayba said: “He was trustworthy and firm; nothing was criticized from him except after he went to Iraq, for he expanded in narrating from his father, and the people of his region objected to that.” Ibn Kharrāsh said that Mālik did not approve of him, and that Hishām came to Kūfa three times: on one visit he said, “my father narrated to me: I heard ʿĀʾisha”; on the second, “my father informed me from ʿĀʾisha”; and on the third, “my father from ʿĀʾisha.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

That quotation should be handled carefully. It does not mean Hishām was a liar. It means the family route cannot be treated as though every father-from-ʿĀʾisha report is equally early, equally stable, and equally insulated from Iraqi transmission dynamics. The bewitchment dossier is exactly the kind of report where that matters: it is a vivid household narrative, transmitted through the ʿUrwa family, then widely diffused in Iraq through students such as Ibn Numayr, Abū Usāma, Ibn ʿUyayna, ʿĪsā b. Yūnus, and others.

There is also a crucial internal clue in Bukhārī’s own version. Ibn Jurayj says he first heard it from **Āl ʿUrwa**, the family of ʿUrwa, and then asked Hishām about it. That is not a defect by itself. It is a sign of provenance. The report’s home is not “many Companions independently remembered this.” Its home is the ʿUrwa household memory-world.

## al-Aʿmash: Reliable, Kūfan, and a Real Tadlīs Problem

Al-Aʿmash is also not a weak narrator in the crude sense. The same rijāl tradition praises him sharply. Al-ʿIjlī calls him trustworthy and firm, the hadith authority of Kūfa in his time, a head in Qurʾān, and notes that he had some Shīʿī leaning. Ibn Maʿīn called him trustworthy. Al-Nasāʾī called him trustworthy and firm. The issue is narrower: al-Aʿmash was also known as a mudallis, and classical critics repeatedly limited or questioned some of his claimed links when the formula was only ʿan.[^7]

Ibn Ḥajar preserves Ibn Ḥibbān’s statement that al-Aʿmash saw Anas but heard from him only a few letters and “was a mudallis.” He also preserves Yaʿqūb b. Shayba’s report that al-Aʿmash’s reports from Mujāhid are only established when he says “I heard,” and that much of his Mujāhid material actually came through another intermediary.[^8]

<HadithBlock label="The al-Aʿmash Problem" source="Ibn Ḥajar, Tahdhīb al-Tahdhīb, entry on Sulaymān b. Mihrān al-Aʿmash">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وقال العجلي: كان ثقة ثبتا في الحديث، وكان محدث أهل الكوفة في زمانه ... وكان فيه تشيع ... وذكره ابن حبان في ثقات التابعين، وقال ... وكان مدلسا ... وقال يعقوب بن شيبة ... ليس يصح للأعمش عن مجاهد إلا أحاديث يسيرة ... لا يثبت منها إلا ما قال: سمعت.</div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">Al-ʿIjlī said: “He was trustworthy and firm in hadith, and he was the hadith authority of Kūfa in his time ... and there was some Shīʿī leaning in him.” Ibn Ḥibbān included him among the trustworthy Successors and said: “He was a mudallis.” Yaʿqūb b. Shayba reports regarding his Mujāhid material: “Only a few hadiths are sound from al-Aʿmash from Mujāhid ... none is established except what he said, ‘I heard.’”</div>
</HadithBlock>

In the Zayd b. Arqam bewitchment route, al-Aʿmash does not say “I heard Yazīd b. Ḥayyān.” The chain is **al-Aʿmash ʿan Yazīd b. Ḥayyān**. This does not automatically falsify the report. Yazīd b. Ḥayyān was a real Kūfan transmitter from Zayd b. Arqam, and Ibn Ḥajar’s entry explicitly says that al-Aʿmash narrated from him.[^9] Chronology is not the problem. The problem is source-critical: a mudallis using ʿan in a report that suspiciously overlaps with a famous Hishām family dossier cannot be used as a clean independent control.

## The Abū Muʿāwiya Bottleneck

The lower isnād of the Zayd route intensifies the problem. In the recoverable versions consulted here, the report from al-Aʿmash is not widely diffused through many independent pupils of al-Aʿmash. It comes through **Abū Muʿāwiya**. Aḥmad has Abū Muʿāwiya → al-Aʿmash → Yazīd → Zayd. Al-Nasāʾī has Hannād b. al-Sarī → Abū Muʿāwiya → al-Aʿmash → Ibn Ḥayyān, meaning Yazīd, → Zayd. Al-Ṭabarānī has Abū Bakr b. Abī Shayba → Abū Muʿāwiya → al-Aʿmash → Yazīd → Zayd.[^10]

That means the apparent spread through Aḥmad, al-Nasāʾī, and al-Ṭabarānī is partly an illusion created by compilation. It is the same lower conduit being collected in several books. Multiple books are not multiple early witnesses.

<div class="bottleneck-card" aria-label="Abu Muawiya bottleneck">
  <div>
    <span>Recoverable Kūfan route</span>
    <h3>Zayd → Yazīd → al-Aʿmash → Abū Muʿāwiya</h3>
    <p>Aḥmad, al-Nasāʾī, and al-Ṭabarānī preserve the report, but the lower strand repeatedly returns to Abū Muʿāwiya. This is not a broad al-Aʿmash fan-out; it is a narrow conduit that later compilers copied into separate collections.</p>
  </div>
  <strong>One lower conduit ≠ independent corroboration</strong>
</div>

This is especially relevant because Abū Muʿāwiya also belonged to the same wider Iraqi transmission world as Hishām. Ibn Ḥajar lists Abū Muʿāwiya among those who narrated from Hishām b. ʿUrwa, and the Zayd route itself places Abū Muʿāwiya as the pupil of al-Aʿmash in this very report.[^11] This does not prove that Abū Muʿāwiya contaminated the chain. It does show that the two dossiers were not sealed off from one another.

## What Could Have Happened?

There are three realistic models.

<section class="models-grid" aria-label="Models for the relation between the routes">
  <article>
    <span>Model 1</span>
    <h3>True independent memory</h3>
    <p>Zayd b. Arqam independently remembered the same event in a compact Kūfan form, and al-Aʿmash preserved it accurately from Yazīd b. Ḥayyān.</p>
    <b>Possible, but least explanatory</b>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>Model 2</span>
    <h3>Shared story-world</h3>
    <p>Different Medinan legal and family memories circulated early; the Kūfan route absorbed the broad skeleton and stabilized it with local features.</p>
    <b>Best fits the fluid matn evidence</b>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>Model 3</span>
    <h3>Direct Iraqi borrowing</h3>
    <p>Al-Aʿmash, or a lower Iraqi transmitter, encountered the Hishām-family report during Hishām’s Iraqi period and transmitted a reshaped version through a more Kūfan-friendly chain.</p>
    <b>Plausible, not directly provable</b>
  </article>
</section>

The first model is the apologetic one, but it leaves too much unexplained: why the diagnostic details vanish, why ʿAlī appears, why the route is so narrow below al-Aʿmash, and why a known mudallis transmits the crucial link with ʿan. The third model is attractive, especially because Hishām and al-Aʿmash were contemporaries, because Hishām came to Kūfa, and because his Iraqi transmission from his father was specifically criticized. But it should be presented as a historical hypothesis, not as a documented confession.

The second model is the strongest. The story was already circulating in multiple forms: legal residue through al-Zuhrī, family dossier through ʿUrwa/Hishām, and loose angelic-diagnosis material through Yaḥyā b. Maʿmar. The Zayd/al-Aʿmash route then looks like a Kūfan collateral crystallization of that shared story-world, not an independent second anchor.

## The Political Layer: Why ʿAlī Appears in the Kūfan Version

The Zayd route is not merely shorter. It is differently staged. In the Hishām report, the scene belongs to ʿĀʾisha’s room and the Prophet’s private exchange with her. In the Zayd route, ʿĀʾisha disappears, and ʿAlī becomes the operative who retrieves and unties the knots.

That shift matters in Kūfa. Zayd b. Arqam settled in Kūfa, transmitted there, and is described in Tahdhīb al-Tahdhīb as having witnessed Ṣiffīn with ʿAlī and as having been among his close associates.[^12] Al-Aʿmash, the Kūfan transmitter of the report, is described by al-ʿIjlī as having some Shīʿī leaning.[^13] Neither fact proves forgery. But together they explain why a Kūfan version of the story would be more likely to preserve an ʿAlī-centered action scene than an ʿĀʾisha-centered domestic scene.

This is where the older anti-Jewish polemic does not disappear, but it becomes only one layer. The Hishām dossier names a Jewish-allied Medinan culprit and situates the story in a Banū Zurayq setting. The Zayd route preserves the Jewish culprit but makes the salvation mechanics more Kūfan and ʿAlid: Gabriel discloses, ʿAlī retrieves, knots are undone, and the Prophet rises freed.

<ContextNote title="Not a Simple Sectarian Label">
Calling the Zayd route “Kūfan” does not mean every Kūfan report is Shīʿī propaganda, and noting al-Aʿmash’s Shīʿī leaning does not mean he was a sectarian fabricator. The point is subtler: regional audiences shape which details survive. A report tied to ʿĀʾisha’s household could be retold in a form where ʿAlī becomes the decisive agent without the whole story being invented from nothing.
</ContextNote>

## The Classical Critics Saw the Pieces, but Not the Whole Problem

Classical rijāl criticism gives us the materials needed for the critique, even if it does not always draw the source-critical conclusion. It tells us that Hishām was trustworthy but that his Iraqi father-reports drew criticism. It tells us that al-Aʿmash was trustworthy but a mudallis. It tells us that Yazīd b. Ḥayyān was a real Kūfan transmitter from Zayd and that al-Aʿmash narrated from him. It tells us that Abū Muʿāwiya transmitted from both worlds. What it does not do is ask the modern source-critical question: does the Zayd route independently confirm the Hishām dossier, or does it sit inside the same second-century Iraqi-Medinan circulation network?

The answer is that it does not independently confirm it in any strong sense. It is a collateral route, but a compromised one. It may preserve a real Kūfan memory of a known story. It may be a reshaped digest. It may even go back to something Zayd said. But as evidence, it does not do the work apologists often assign to it.

<div class="verdict-grid" aria-label="Final assessment">
  <article>
    <span>What is strong</span>
    <p>The Hishām dossier is widely collected and has multiple lower transmitters below Hishām.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>What is weak</span>
    <p>Above Hishām, the main canonical report still returns to the ʿUrwa family line, and Bukhārī’s own note points to Āl ʿUrwa as an early family-memory source.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>What is suspicious</span>
    <p>The Zayd route shares the skeleton but not the diagnostic details, travels through al-Aʿmash’s ʿanʿana, and is narrow below al-Aʿmash.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <span>What follows</span>
    <p>The Zayd route should be treated as a secondary Kūfan collateral, not as a secure independent witness.</p>
  </article>
</div>

## Conclusion

The bewitchment narrative is not supported by two equal pillars. Its main pillar is the ʿĀʾisha → ʿUrwa → Hishām family dossier, with an earlier Medinan legal residue visible through al-Zuhrī and ʿAbd al-Razzāq. The supposed second pillar, Zayd b. Arqam through Yazīd b. Ḥayyān and al-Aʿmash, is too narrow and too contextually exposed to carry the weight of independent corroboration.

The most responsible conclusion is not that al-Aʿmash has been caught beyond doubt fabricating an isnād. The evidence does not prove that. The responsible conclusion is more damaging to the apologetic use of the report: the Kūfan route cannot rescue the historicity of the Hishām dossier. It is exactly the kind of route source criticism should hesitate over — a vivid story already circulating in Medinan family and legal contexts, a known Iraqi expansion environment around Hishām, a Kūfan mudallis transmitting with ʿan, and a secondary matn that conveniently replaces ʿĀʾisha’s room with ʿAlī’s action.

If the report is defended, it should be defended honestly as a report canonized through Hishām’s family transmission and accepted by later hadith compilers. It should not be presented as if the Zayd b. Arqam route gives independent, multi-regional historical certainty.
