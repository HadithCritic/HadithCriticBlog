<section class="b59-opener" aria-label="Article thesis">
  <p class="b59-kicker">Kitāb Jāmiʿ al-ʿIlm</p>
  <h2>The opponent rejects reports, but then argues like someone already inside al-Shāfiʿī’s system.</h2>
  <p>
    The issue is not that al-Shāfiʿī answers a Quran-alone position. The issue is that the opponent in the dialogue repeatedly grants the assumptions a Quran-alone critic would normally contest: that <em>ḥikmah</em> means Sunnah, that obedience to the Messenger requires accepting later reports, and that hadith may specify, limit, or abrogate Qurʾānic law.
  </p>
</section>

I had been familiar with many of al-Shāfiʿī’s arguments in defense of reports, but I wanted to read how he engaged the Quran-alone position in his own words. His _Kitāb Jāmiʿ al-ʿIlm_ is useful for that because it contains a dialogue with an unnamed opponent who rejects reports from the Prophet. The exchange is often treated as if it preserves a live debate between al-Shāfiʿī and a real Quran-centered critic. A closer reading makes that difficult to accept.

I am using “Quran-alone” as a modern shorthand. Al-Shāfiʿī himself frames the first group as **“the faction that rejected all reports”** (_al-ṭāʾifah allatī raddat al-akhbār kullahā_). That distinction matters because the text is not giving us a named school, a signed manifesto, or a transcript. It gives us al-Shāfiʿī’s presentation of an opponent for the purpose of defending the legal authority of reports.

## What the text is doing

Before the dialogue begins, al-Shāfiʿī explains that he will present examples of the arguments held by different groups. This already tells us to read the debate as a constructed representation rather than as a verbatim record.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">Primary text · al-Shāfiʿī, <em>Jāmiʿ al-ʿIlm</em></div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وسأمثل لك من قول كل فرقة عرفتها مثالا يدل على ما وراءه إن شاء الله تعالى.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “I will present to you, from the statement of each group I have known, an example that indicates what lies behind it, God willing.”</p>
</div>

The first chapter is then introduced as a report of the position of **“the faction that rejected all reports.”** The wording is not neutral. Al-Shāfiʿī’s project is not to preserve the opponent’s position for its own sake. He is arranging the argument so that the reader can see why rejecting reports collapses into error.

<div class="b59-source b59-source--compact">
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">باب حكاية قول الطائفة التي ردت الأخبار كلها.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “Chapter: Relating the statement of the faction that rejected all reports.”</p>
</div>

That does not mean the passage is useless. It is actually valuable because it shows what al-Shāfiʿī believed needed to be answered: the sufficiency of the Qurʾān, the uncertainty of individual transmitters, the problem of turning fallible reports into law, and the danger of placing reports beside the Book of God.

## The strongest objection appears at the beginning

The opponent’s opening challenge is much stronger than the later concessions. He appeals to the Qurʾān as _tibyān li-kull shayʾ_ — an explanation of all things — and then presses the epistemic problem of reports: transmitters can err, forget, and contradict one another, yet jurists still use their reports to permit and prohibit.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">The opponent’s opening objection</div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وقد قال الله عز وجل في القرآن: ﴿تبيانا لكل شيء﴾، فكيف جاز عند نفسك أو لأحد في شيء فرض الله أن يقول مرة الفرض فيه عام ومرة الفرض فيه خاص؟</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">وأكثر ما فرقت بينه من هذا عندك حديث ترويه عن رجل عن آخر عن آخر حتى تبلغ به رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، وقد وجدتك ومن ذهب مذهبك لا تبرئون أحدا لقيتموه وقدمتموه في الصدق والحفظ من أن يغلط وينسى ويخطئ في حديثه.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “God Almighty has said in the Qurʾān that it is an explanation of all things. How, then, is it permissible for you or anyone else to say about something God made obligatory that the obligation is general in one case and specific in another? Most of this distinction, according to you, rests on a report you transmit from one man, from another, from another, until it reaches the Messenger of God. Yet you and those who follow your method do not absolve even those you meet and advance as truthful and accurate from error, forgetfulness, and mistake in their reports.”</p>
</div>

This is the kind of objection a Quran-centered critic would actually make. It does not merely ask whether hadith are useful. It questions the entire move from fallible historical reports to binding divine law. The problem is that the dialogue does not keep that pressure. Once al-Shāfiʿī begins building his case, the opponent starts conceding the very premises he would need to contest.

## Where the dialogue begins to feel controlled

### 1. The opponent accepts al-Shāfiʿī’s definition of ḥikmah

Al-Shāfiʿī argues from verses that mention “the Book and the Wisdom.” Because the Book is the Qurʾān, he identifies the Wisdom with the Sunnah. A Quran-alone critic would normally stop the argument here and ask where the Qurʾān defines _ḥikmah_ as a later body of extra-Qurʾānic reports. Instead, the opponent moves toward al-Shāfiʿī’s conclusion.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">The ḥikmah concession</div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قلت: قال الله عز وجل: ﴿واذكرن ما يتلى في بيوتكن من آيات الله والحكمة﴾، فأخبر أنه يتلى في بيوتهن شيئان.</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قال: فهذا القرآن يتلى، فكيف تتلى الحكمة؟</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قلت: إنما معنى التلاوة أن ينطق بالقرآن والسنة كما ينطق بها.</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قال: فهذه أبين في أن الحكمة غير القرآن من الأولى.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “I said: God Almighty said, ‘And remember what is recited in your houses of the signs of God and the wisdom,’ so He informed us that two things were recited in their houses. He said: ‘This Qurʾān is recited, so how is wisdom recited?’ I said: ‘The meaning of recitation is simply that the Qurʾān and the Sunnah are spoken aloud, as they are spoken.’ He said: ‘This is clearer than the first in showing that wisdom is other than the Qurʾān.’”</p>
</div>

The concession is too convenient. The entire argument depends on sliding from “wisdom is mentioned alongside the Book” to “wisdom is the Sunnah” and then from “Sunnah” to legally binding prophetic reports. The opponent accepts the first move instead of challenging the semantic assumption. A stronger response would have pressed the Qurʾān’s own usage of _ḥikmah_: divine judgment, instruction, insight, and admonition, not a separately preserved legal archive.

### 2. Obedience to the Messenger becomes obedience to reports

Al-Shāfiʿī then moves from verses commanding obedience to the Messenger to the necessity of accepting reports about the Messenger. The logic is important: because later Muslims did not witness the Prophet directly, their route to obeying him must be through reports.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">From obedience to reports</div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قلت: لقد فرض الله عز وجل علينا اتباع أمره فقال: ﴿وما آتاكم الرسول فخذوه وما نهاكم عنه فانتهوا﴾.</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قال: إنه لبين في التنزيل أن علينا فرضا أن نأخذ الذي أمرنا به وننتهي عما نهانا رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم.</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قلت: فهل تجد السبيل إلى تأدية فرض الله عز وجل في اتباع أوامر رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم ... إلا بالخبر عن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم؟</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “I said: God Almighty has imposed upon us following His command, saying, ‘Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it, and whatever he forbids you, refrain from it.’ He said: ‘It is clear in the revelation that we are obligated to take what the Messenger commanded us and refrain from what he forbade us.’ I said: ‘Do you find any way to fulfill God’s obligation of following the commands of the Messenger ... except by reports from the Messenger of God?’”</p>
</div>

This is the main hinge of the argument. Al-Shāfiʿī treats obedience to the Messenger as requiring acceptance of transmitted reports. A Quran-alone response would separate the Messenger’s binding message from later hadith transmission. The Prophet is obeyed by obeying the revelation he delivered; that does not automatically turn every later report attributed to him into a second source of law.

### 3. Hadith-based abrogation is accepted too quickly

The next move is even more revealing. Al-Shāfiʿī argues that without reports, one cannot resolve the relationship between inheritance verses and the verse of bequest. The opponent then concedes that accepting reports is obligatory.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">The collapse into report authority</div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">فزعمنا بالخبر عن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم أن آية الفرائض نسخت الوصية للوالدين والأقربين، فلو كنا ممن لا يقبل الخبر فقال قائل: الوصية نسخت الفرائض، هل نجد الحجة عليه إلا بخبر عن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم؟</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">قال: هذا شبيه بالكتاب والحكمة، والحجة لك ثابتة بأن علينا قبول الخبر عن رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم، وقد صرت إلى قبول الخبر.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “We claim, by a report from the Messenger of God, that the inheritance verse abrogated the bequest to parents and relatives. If we were among those who do not accept reports, and someone said that the bequest abrogated the inheritance shares, would we find an argument against him except by a report from the Messenger of God? He said: ‘This resembles the Book and Wisdom, and your proof is established that we must accept reports from the Messenger of God. I have come to accepting reports.’”</p>
</div>

This is where the staged character of the exchange becomes most obvious. A Quran-alone critic would not concede the necessity of a hadith to solve an alleged abrogation problem. He would challenge the premise that the Qurʾān needs a report to determine its own legal architecture. He would also challenge the broader doctrine that reports may limit or cancel the apparent force of Qurʾānic commands.

### 4. The rejection of reports is turned into an easy caricature

Once the opponent has conceded the main point, the text describes the report-rejecting positions as leading to absurd legal minimalism: any act that bears the name “prayer” or “zakat” becomes sufficient, even if someone prays two units every day or every few days.

<div class="b59-source">
  <div class="b59-source__meta">The caricature of the report-rejecter</div>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">ولقد ذهب فيه أناس مذهبين: أحد الفريقين لا يقبل خبرا وفي كتاب الله البيان.</p>
  <p class="b59-source__ar" dir="rtl" lang="ar">فقال: من جاء بما يقع عليه اسم صلاة وأقل ما يقع عليه اسم زكاة فقد أدى ما عليه، لا وقت في ذلك، ولو صلى ركعتين في كل يوم أو قال في كل أيام.</p>
  <p><strong>Translation:</strong> “Some people have gone in two directions: one group accepts no report, while the Book of God contains the clarification. So he said: whoever performs what can be called prayer, and the least of what can be called zakat, has fulfilled what is upon him; there is no fixed time for that, even if he prays two units every day — or he said every few days.”</p>
</div>

This may describe some extreme formulation known to al-Shāfiʿī, but as an argument against Quran-centered criticism it is too easy. It assumes that rejecting hadith as a source of divine legislation leaves only lexical minimalism. A Quran-alone response can distinguish between the Qurʾān, inherited communal practice, public ritual continuity, and later hadith books. Rejecting hadith as a law-making authority does not require pretending that Muslims had no inherited practice of prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, or almsgiving.

## What a real Quran-alone response would have pressed

<div class="b59-ledger" aria-label="Quran-alone counterpoints">
  <div>
    <span>01</span>
    <h3>Define ḥikmah from the Qurʾān, not from legal theory.</h3>
    <p>Al-Shāfiʿī’s argument works only if <em>ḥikmah</em> is equated with Sunnah and then Sunnah is equated with binding reports. The Qurʾān itself uses the word more broadly than that.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>02</span>
    <h3>Do not smuggle hadith books into “obey the Messenger.”</h3>
    <p>Obedience to the Messenger is not the same claim as obedience to a later report-corpus. The first is Qurʾānic. The second requires separate proof.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>03</span>
    <h3>Keep certainty and probability separate.</h3>
    <p>Al-Shāfiʿī repeatedly moves from God’s command to obey the Messenger into probabilistic historical transmission. A Quran-alone critic would refuse to let probability become divine legislation.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>04</span>
    <h3>Challenge abrogation by report.</h3>
    <p>The claim that reports can settle, specify, or abrogate Qurʾānic rulings is not a minor detail. It is the entire authority structure of later jurisprudence.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>05</span>
    <h3>Distinguish public practice from private reports.</h3>
    <p>Prayer and pilgrimage do not become unknowable merely because later hadith collections are rejected as divine law. Communal practice and hadith authority are different categories.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The problem with the dialogue is therefore not that al-Shāfiʿī has no argument. He clearly has a sophisticated argument for his system. The problem is that the opponent is made to fight on al-Shāfiʿī’s ground. He accepts the terms, grants the definitions, agrees to the legal framework, and then retreats at the crucial moments.

## Why this matters

_Kitāb Jāmiʿ al-ʿIlm_ is one of the earliest and most important defenses of report authority in Islamic legal theory. It shows the moment when hadith is not merely being used as supporting material, but being theorized as a binding proof without which the Qurʾān supposedly cannot be properly applied. That makes the work historically important. It also makes the staging of the opponent important.

A reader should not come away thinking, “This is what a Quran-alone advocate would say.” A better conclusion is: this is how al-Shāfiʿī wanted the report-rejecting position to appear inside his own legal argument. The opponent is useful because he shows what al-Shāfiʿī needed to neutralize. He is less useful as evidence for what an actual Quran-alone critic would have argued if allowed to define his own premises.

## Conclusion

After reading the exchange with the Arabic text in view, my objection is sharper than before. Al-Shāfiʿī is not simply refuting a Quran-alone position; he is presenting a controlled opponent who gradually accepts the foundations of hadith authority. The strongest challenge appears at the beginning: the Qurʾān claims explanatory sufficiency, reports are transmitted through fallible men, and jurists use those reports to permit and prohibit. Once those issues are on the table, the debate should become a dispute over epistemology, revelation, and legal authority.

Instead, the opponent concedes that wisdom is other than the Qurʾān, accepts that obedience to the Messenger requires reports, and agrees that reports are needed to resolve Qurʾānic law. That is why the dialogue reads less like a real Quran-alone debate and more like a legal demonstration. Al-Shāfiʿī builds a foil who rejects reports at the start, but by the end speaks as though the Shāfiʿī framework has already won.
