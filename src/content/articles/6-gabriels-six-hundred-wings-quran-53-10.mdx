## The Problem

The Qur’an describes angels as messengers with wings, but it does not say that Gabriel has six hundred wings. That number comes from hadith. The report is usually presented as if it were a broad, independent description of Gabriel’s created form. But when the isnāds are mapped, the picture is much narrower. The main “six hundred wings” reports pass through one Kūfan exegetical route:

**Ibn Masʿūd → Zirr b. Ḥubaysh → Abū Isḥāq al-Shaybānī.**

After al-Shaybānī, the report fans out through several later transmitters and enters Bukhārī, Muslim, and Tirmidhī. That later spread can make the report look wider than it is. But above the spread, the tradition is compact.

<ClaimBox>
The “Gabriel had six hundred wings” report is not a mass-transmitted description of Gabriel. It is a Qur’an-exegesis report clustered through one Kūfan route: Ibn Masʿūd → Zirr b. Ḥubaysh → Abū Isḥāq al-Shaybānī.
</ClaimBox>

This matters because the report is not just giving a detail about angels. It is explaining Qur’an 53:9–10. The hadith tells us that Ibn Masʿūd understood the passage as a reference to Muhammad seeing Gabriel. The problem is that this explanation pushes the verse away from its simpler Qur’anic grammar.

## The Verse Being Explained

<QuranVerse verse="53:10" />

The verse says that **He** revealed to **His servant** what He revealed. The natural reading is that God is the revealer and Muhammad is the servant. The hadith explanation shifts the focus. It reads the surrounding scene as Muhammad seeing Gabriel in his true form, and then adds that Gabriel had six hundred wings.

The issue is not only the number of wings. The issue is the exegetical move. The report turns a verse about revelation from God to His servant into a report about Gabriel’s appearance.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">قَالَ أَخْبَرَنِي ابْنُ مَسْعُودٍ أَنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ رَأَى جِبْرِيلَ لَهُ سِتُّمِائَةِ جَنَاحٍ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“He said: Ibn Masʿūd informed me that the Prophet saw Gabriel, and he had six hundred wings.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

The wording is simple, but its function is large. It is not a free-floating angelology report. It is a tafsīr report. Zirr is asked about Qur’an 53, and he answers by transmitting Ibn Masʿūd’s statement about Gabriel. That is why the chain and the context matter.

For a lay reader, this means the report has a bottleneck. A bottleneck is the point in a transmission map where many later roads collapse into one earlier road. Here, the later collectors preserve several routes, but they are not independent at the level that matters. They all return to the same Kūfan exegetical strand.

That does not automatically prove the report is invented. But it prevents an exaggerated claim. One cannot simply say, “Bukhārī, Muslim, and Tirmidhī all have it, therefore it is broadly established.” The real question is where the report becomes recoverable before the collectors. The answer is al-Shaybānī from Zirr from Ibn Masʿūd.

## The Matn Problem

The matn is short. But the interpretive problem is large.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">سَأَلْتُ زِرَّ بْنَ حُبَيْشٍ عَنْ قَوْلِ اللَّهِ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ: فَكَانَ قَابَ قَوْسَيْنِ أَوْ أَدْنَى. قَالَ: أَخْبَرَنِي ابْنُ مَسْعُودٍ أَنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ رَأَى جِبْرِيلَ وَلَهُ سِتُّمِائَةِ جَنَاحٍ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“I asked Zirr b. Ḥubaysh about the words of God, Mighty and Majestic: ‘He was at a distance of two bow-lengths or nearer.’ He said: Ibn Masʿūd informed me that the Prophet saw Gabriel, and he had six hundred wings.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

Notice the structure. The report begins with a question about a Qur’anic verse. It ends with a claim about Gabriel’s wings. That means the report is doing tafsīr work. It is telling the reader how to understand the vision scene in Sūrat al-Najm.

The problem is that Qur’an 53:10 reads as God revealing to His servant. The servant is Muhammad. The revealer is God. The hadith explanation places Gabriel at the center and makes the point of the verse the Prophet’s vision of Gabriel. That is not the most natural reading of the verse itself.

This does not mean a reader must deny that Gabriel appears elsewhere in Sūrat al-Najm. The point is narrower. The hadith report is not merely preserving a neutral historical memory. It is intervening in the meaning of a verse. It supplies an exegetical explanation and then builds angelology on top of that explanation.

## Qur’an 35:1 and the Wings of Angels

<QuranVerse verse="35:1" />

Qur’an 35:1 describes angels as messengers with wings: two, three, and four. The same verse also says that God increases in creation what He wills. That last clause matters because it is often used as the traditional answer to the objection. A defender can say, “The verse mentions two, three, and four, but then says God increases in creation, so six hundred is possible.”

That response is possible, but it does not remove the critical problem. The Qur’an itself does not say Gabriel has six hundred wings. The hadith supplies that number through one exegetical chain. The question is not whether God could create an angel with more wings. The question is whether this specific report has strong enough textual and transmission evidence to impose six hundred wings onto Gabriel and then use that as the explanation of Sūrat al-Najm.

The better argument is therefore precise. Qur’an 35:1 does not directly teach six hundred wings. Qur’an 53:10 does not naturally require Gabriel as the subject of “His servant.” The six-hundred-wings claim comes from a narrow hadith-tafsīr strand, not from the Qur’an itself.

## Why the Bottleneck Matters

Hadith reports can look stronger than they are when later branches are counted as if each branch were an independent origin. Here, the report appears in Bukhārī, Muslim, and Tirmidhī, but the major routes converge through al-Shaybānī. That means the collector spread is real, but the early spread is limited.

The key question is not, “How many books contain this?” The key question is, “Where does the report become recoverable?”

For this family, the answer is:

**Abū Isḥāq al-Shaybānī → Zirr b. Ḥubaysh → Ibn Masʿūd.**

That makes al-Shaybānī the visible bottleneck of the preserved family. Zirr is also central because he is the one being asked about the verse. Ibn Masʿūd is the Companion authority used to anchor the explanation. But the later literary spread does not create multiple independent Companion-level witnesses.

<ContextNote>
The report’s strength should not be exaggerated by counting Bukhārī, Muslim, and Tirmidhī as though they were separate early origins. They are separate collector witnesses to one shared upper route.
</ContextNote>

## The Report as Exegesis, Not Independent Angelology

The most important correction is conceptual. The report is often treated as if it were mainly about the physical form of Gabriel. But the report itself begins as an explanation of Sūrat al-Najm. The question is about Qur’anic wording. The answer is a hadith explanation.

That changes how the report should be evaluated. If it were an independent report from multiple Companions saying, “The Prophet saw Gabriel with six hundred wings,” the issue would be one thing. But the recoverable family is specifically Ibn Masʿūd’s interpretation transmitted through Zirr and al-Shaybānī. The number belongs to that exegetical tradition.

It is also important that the Qur’an does not need the six-hundred-wings report to make sense. Qur’an 53:10 can be read as God revealing to His servant. Qur’an 35:1 already gives a restrained description of angels as winged messengers. The hadith adds a dramatic number and then attaches it to a disputed reading of the vision passage.

## What the Diagram Shows

The diagram does not show that every individual in the chain was unreliable. That is not the point. It shows that the report is structurally narrow. The chain does not branch at Ibn Masʿūd into multiple students. It does not branch at Zirr into many independent transmitters. It branches below al-Shaybānī.

That means the “six hundred wings” tradition is not a broad early memory. It is a compact Kūfan tafsīr report that later became widely cited because major compilers preserved its branches.

The same pattern appears in many hadith studies. A report can have many later witnesses but still depend on one earlier bottleneck. Once that bottleneck is identified, the report has to be argued from that bottleneck, not from the number of later books.

## Final Verdict

The “Gabriel had six hundred wings” report is best understood as a narrow exegetical hadith family, not as Qur’anic angelology. Its main preserved routes all converge through Abū Isḥāq al-Shaybānī from Zirr b. Ḥubaysh from Ibn Masʿūd. The later branches into Bukhārī, Muslim, and Tirmidhī show that the report circulated well below al-Shaybānī, but they do not create independent early origins above him.

The Qur’anic issue is also important. Qur’an 35:1 describes angels as having wings in twos, threes, and fours, while also saying God increases in creation what He wills. That means the verse should not be used in a simplistic way, as if it mechanically forbids all increase. But it also does not teach six hundred wings. The six-hundred number is supplied by hadith, not by the Qur’an.

Qur’an 53:10 is even more central. The verse naturally reads as God revealing to His servant. The hadith turns the passage into a statement about Muhammad seeing Gabriel and then adds the six-hundred-wings detail. That is an exegetical move, and the isnād map shows that the move is preserved through one compact Kūfan route.

So the strongest conclusion is careful but clear: the Qur’an does not require the six-hundred-wings doctrine, and the hadith evidence for it depends on a narrow tafsīr bottleneck. The report is not a broad independent memory of Gabriel’s form. It is Ibn Masʿūd’s Qur’an-exegesis tradition as transmitted through Zirr and al-Shaybānī.
