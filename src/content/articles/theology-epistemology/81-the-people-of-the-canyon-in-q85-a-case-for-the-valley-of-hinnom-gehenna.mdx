Early in the Meccan period, when the Prophet’s followers were small in number and faced beatings, boycotts, and resentment for their faith, a short surah arrived that opened with an oath sworn on the stars, then turned to a scene of fire.[^1]

<QuranVerse verse="85:1-10" />

Muslim commentaries since the eighth century identified this scene with one of two events: either the massacre of Christians at Najrān in 523 CE, or Nebuchadnezzar’s furnace from the Book of Daniel.[^2] Both readings have problems. This article makes the case for a third option, one built from the Quran’s vocabulary and from a judgment tradition that the Quran’s name for hell already carries inside it: the Valley of Hinnom, the biblical site that gave Arabic the word *Jahannam* (Hell).

<ClaimBox title="Central Thesis">
This article argues for a third reading of Q85: that the “people of the canyon” and the fire scene are best understood against the biblical Valley of Hinnom, the site whose Hebrew name passed into Arabic as *Jahannam*. The case rests on the surah’s sequence of celestial imagery and valley fire, the Hinnom tradition of fire, judgment, and public witnessing, and the Quran’s own use of *Jahannam* as the name of Hell.
</ClaimBox>


## I. The Oaths (vv. 1–3): What the Surah Swears By
The surah opens with three oaths, and they are not decorative. It swears by the sky and its *burūj*—a word usually translated as “great stars” or “*constellations*”—and by “*the promised Day,*” and then, third, by “*the witness and what is witnessed*” (*wa-shāhidin wa-mashhūd*). Commentators have long treated this as a standalone cosmic flourish, an appeal to the grandeur of the heavens before the main event begins. But the pairing of star-imagery with a valley-fire narrative two verses later has a precise biblical antecedent, and it is not a one-off.[^3]

The Deuteronomistic history presents the same two-part formula twice, attached to the very Topheth material this article will argue Q85 is echoing. The account of King Manasseh, the ruler under whom the fire cult in the Valley of Hinnom is first introduced, opens with him worshipping “*all the host of heaven*” and building altars to them inside the Temple courts. The very next verse says he “*made his son pass through the fire,*” the standard biblical shorthand for child sacrifice at Topheth.

<BibleVerse reference="2 Kings 21:1-6">
<p>Manasseh was twelve years old when he began to reign; he reigned fifty-five years in Jerusalem. His mother’s name was Hephzibah. He did what was evil in the sight of the Lord, following the abominable practices of the nations that the Lord drove out before the people of Israel. For he rebuilt the high places that his father Hezekiah had destroyed; <mark style="background-color: rgba(214, 154, 83, 0.42); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">he erected altars for Baal, made a sacred pole, as King Ahab of Israel had done, worshiped all the host of heaven and served them.</mark> He built altars in the house of the Lord, of which the Lord had said, “In Jerusalem I will put my name.” He built altars for all the host of heaven in the two courts of the house of the Lord. <mark style="background-color: rgba(235, 208, 67, 0.48); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">He made his son pass through fire</mark>; he practiced soothsaying and augury and dealt with mediums and with wizards. He did much evil in the sight of the Lord, provoking him to anger … Moreover, <mark style="background-color: rgba(235, 208, 67, 0.48); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">Manasseh shed very much innocent blood, until he had filled Jerusalem from one end to another</mark>, besides the sin that he caused Judah to sin so that they did what was evil in the sight of the Lord.</p>
</BibleVerse>

Two chapters later, King Josiah reverses the same sin in the same order. He first destroys the objects made for the sun, moon, constellations, and for all the host of heaven, and only then in the next paragraph defiles Topheth itself in the Valley of Hinnom:

<BibleVerse reference="2 Kings 23:4-10">
<p>The king Josiah commanded the high priest Hilkiah, the priests of the second order, and the guardians of the threshold to bring out of the temple of the Lord all the vessels made for Baal, for Asherah, and <mark style="background-color: rgba(214, 154, 83, 0.42); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">for all the host of heaven</mark>; he burned them outside Jerusalem in the fields of the Kidron and carried their ashes to Bethel. He deposed the idolatrous priests whom the kings of Judah had ordained to make offerings in the high places at the cities of Judah and around Jerusalem, those also who made offerings <mark style="background-color: rgba(214, 154, 83, 0.42); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">to the sun, the moon, the constellations, and all the host of the heavens.</mark> He brought out the image of Asherah from the house of the Lord, outside Jerusalem, to the Wadi Kidron, burned it at the Wadi Kidron, beat it to dust, and threw the dust of it upon the graves of the common people. He broke down the houses of the illicit priests who were in the house of the Lord, where the women did weaving for Asherah. He brought all the priests out of the towns of Judah and defiled the high places where the priests had made offerings, from Geba to Beer-sheba; he broke down the high places of the gates that were at the entrance of the gate of Joshua the governor of the city, which were on the left at the gate of the city. The priests of the high places, however, did not come up to the altar of the Lord in Jerusalem but ate unleavened bread among their kindred. He defiled Topheth, which is in the valley of Ben-hinnom, <mark style="background-color: rgba(235, 208, 67, 0.48); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">so that no one would make a son or a daughter pass through fire as an offering to Molech.</mark></p>
</BibleVerse>

Star-worship above, fire-sacrifice below. The same two-part formula appears at both ends of the story, describing the king who commits the idol-worship, and the king who undoes it. That is not a coincidence confined to one verse; it looks more like an intentional fixed idiom. Q85 opens with an oath on the sky’s constellations and then, without transition, narrates a fire in a ground-level depression where believers are killed for their faith. The sequence (celestial imagery, then valley fire) is already built into the biblical tradition the Quran’s vocabulary for hell carries with it.

The third oath, “*the witness and what is witnessed,*” has no explicit referent in the text, but the Josiah narrative supplies a possible echo. When Josiah undertakes his reform, he first gathers the elders and all the people, reads the Book of the Covenant aloud, and only then proceeds to destroy the religious order that has been condemned.

<BibleVerse reference="2 Kings 23:1-3">
<p>Then the king directed that <mark style="background-color: rgba(116, 159, 220, 0.36); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">all the elders of Judah and Jerusalem should be gathered to him.</mark> The king went up to the house of the Lord, <mark style="background-color: rgba(116, 159, 220, 0.36); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">and with him went all the people of Judah, all the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the priests, the prophets, and all the people, both small and great;</mark> he read in their hearing all the words of the book of the covenant that had been found in the house of the Lord. The king stood by the pillar and made a covenant before the Lord, to follow the Lord, keeping his commandments, his decrees, and his statutes, with all his heart and all his soul, to perform the words of this covenant that were written in this book. All the people joined in the covenant.</p>
</BibleVerse>

The sequence is unusually concentrated: a public assembly, a solemn declaration, the heavenly cult, its destruction, and finally the Hinnom fire cult. The “witness” could plausibly evoke the witnessing party (the king and assembled covenant community) while the “witnessed” could refer to the public destruction of the idolatrous order itself. That remains an interpretation, not something the wording of Q85:3 establishes by itself. The important point is that the otherwise unexplained pair of “witness and witnessed” appears at the head of a surah whose following scene is itself organized around spectatorship and judgment.

## II. The Scene (vv. 4–7): What Happened in the Canyon
Let’s turn to the seven verses in question, because the argument depends on noticing what is present and what is missing:

<QuranVerse verse="85:4-11" />

There is no mention of who built the *ukhdūd*, when, or why. The text simply names a group of people after a feature of the landscape: “the people of the *ukhdūd*.” Everything else—the identity of the persecutor, the location, the century—comes from the exegetical tradition layered on top of these seven verses, not from the verses themselves. That gap between text and tradition is where this argument lives.[^4]

### Two Standard Answers, Two Standing Problems
The majority Muslim reading points to Najrān, a town in what is now southern Saudi Arabia. In 523 CE, the Jewish Himyarite king Dhū Nuwās moved against Najrān’s Christian population after a period of religious conflict in South Arabia, which resulted in the deaths of thousands.[^5] The event is documented from the Christian side in the Syriac *Book of the Ḥimyarites*, in a letter attributed to Simeon of Beth Arsham, in documented inscriptions in the ancient Himyaritic language, and the story itself reached the wider late-antique world through Byzantine chroniclers.[^6] It’s real history, and it happened close enough to Muhammad’s own lifetime that stories about it could have plausibly reached Mecca. But it carries a theological problem the tradition itself never quite resolves. Dhū Nuwās was not a pagan forcing idol-worship on monotheists. He was a Jewish convert, a monotheist himself, hunting down Christians. Q85 frames the persecution as punishment for believing in “*God, the Almighty, the Praiseworthy,*” language that reads more naturally as idolaters against believers than as one scriptural community against another.[^7] The Oxford scholar Josef Horovitz raised a second objection decades ago: the earliest Christian accounts of Najrān, written by people close to the event, describe burned churches and mass executions, but not the specific image of a fire-filled trench.[^8] That detail is not absent from every later account of Najrān, and scholars continue to debate how much weight the silence in the earliest sources should carry.[^9] Still, the trench/canyon as the defining instrument of the massacre is a distinctly Quranic emphasis, and pinning it to Najrān requires glossing over these inconsistencies.

The second candidate, argued most fully by Adam Silverstein in a 2019 study, points instead to Daniel 3: Nebuchadnezzar throws Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego into a furnace for refusing to worship his golden image. This solves the theology cleanly. An idolatrous king demands worship of something other than God, and three monotheists refuse and burn for it, exactly the shape of Q85’s account.[^10] The trouble is the furnace itself. In every surviving version of Daniel 3, in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek, the instrument of execution is a kiln, not a trench, pit, or anything resembling a valley.[^11] Silverstein’s research demonstrated how early Christian Arabic translations of Daniel occasionally render the Aramaic word for furnace as *ukhdūd*, showing that the Arabic word could be pulled toward “furnace” under translation pressure, but that pull runs in the opposite direction from what a plain reading of Q85 suggests. Q85 describes people sitting beside the fire, watching it happen to others, a spectator’s vantage point that fits a wide, walkable feature of terrain far better than the mouth of a kiln.[^12]

Several further problems compound these two. Most basically, Daniel 3 is a deliverance story, and Q85 is not. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego walk out of the furnace unharmed, joined inside it by a mysterious fourth figure while the flames kill only the men who threw them in; nothing burns the three believers themselves. Q85 states the opposite outcome: the persecutors sit around the fire “*to watch the burning of the believers*” (v. 7)—it is the believers who burn, not the persecutors.

That reading is not undercut by a separate ambiguity in verse 4, where the people of the canyon could in principle denote either the victims or the persecutors who dug the pit and lit it. Silverstein and other proponents of the Daniel 3 reading favor the latter, taking the verb *qutila* as an imprecation against the persecutors (“cursed/woe be…”) rather than a report that the victims had already died. Verse 5’s “*they ignited a blazing fire*” has no antecedent, but “*the people of the canyon*” in verse 4, which makes the persecutors the ones the phrase names.[^13] But settling verse 4 in Silverstein’s favor does not rescue the parallel to Daniel 3:22 where the men who cast the three youths into the furnace are killed by the flame within the hour. Verse 4’s “woe” is an imprecation aimed at a future reckoning, not a report that the persecutors have already perished in their own fire—and even granting every possible reading of verse 4, verse 7 still states plainly that the believers burned. A deliverance narrative and a martyrdom narrative are not two tellings of the same event; they resolve in opposite directions, and nothing in the ambiguity over who “the people of the canyon” are changes what verse 7 says happened to the believers.

The identity of the persecuted does not match either. Daniel names three specific, high-ranking Judean officials serving in Nebuchadnezzar’s court. Q85 speaks of an unnamed, collective group, “*the believing men and women.*” One is a story about three courtiers; the other is about a persecuted community.

Nor does the occasion for persecution match. Nebuchadnezzar’s furnace is the consequence of a specific, staged act. He erects a golden image sixty cubits high and decrees that everyone bow to it on pain of the furnace. Q85 never mentions an idol, an image, or a decree to worship anything. Its stated motive is that the persecutors “*hated them for no other reason than believing in God*.” It was due to a hostility of monotheism, not refusal of a royal command.

There is a curiosity in Silverstein’s etymological argument that sits uneasily beside his furnace reading. Elsewhere in the same study, he proposes that Ukhdūd (or, on a South Arabian revocalization, *Akhdūd*) may combine *akh*, “furnace,” with *dūd*, a word he traces either to Second Temple *Dūdael*—“*God’s cauldron,*” the place to which Azazel is consigned in near-contemporary apocalyptic material—or to a Middle Persian word for “smoke.” Both options point toward an eschatological pit rather than a domesticated oven, in some tension with the historical furnace Silverstein wants Q85 to be describing in the first place.[^14]

So the two standard answers each solve, at best, part of the puzzle, and both solve less of it than their proponents suggest. Najrān supplies a trench but not idolatry, and its exegetical support turns out to be borrowed from the very Daniel material it competes against. Daniel 3 supplies idolatry but not a trench, not an anonymous collective, and not the outcome of burned believers. Neither delivers all three things Q85 needs: an idolater persecuting monotheists, a fire in a ground-level depression rather than an oven, and a scene where the persecutors sit and watch the massacre of their own creation.

## III. The Pivot (vv. 8–9): Why They Burned
The scene shifts abruptly from narrative to theology. “*They hated them for no other reason than believing in God, the Almighty, the Praiseworthy. To Him belongs the kingship of the heavens and the earth. And God witnesses all things.*” This is the theological heart of the surah. The persecutors are not rival monotheists with a doctrinal dispute. They are idolaters who hate believers for their allegiance to the one God. The dominion belongs to God alone. And God is witness—not the human spectators around the fire, but the divine witness who sees all.

The witnessing motif, introduced in verse 3, now returns. The surah surrounds its judgment scene with the language and imagery of witnessing. An unidentified ‘witness’ and ‘witnessed’ at the opening, human spectatorship at the center, and divine witnessing at the conclusion. The specific biblical parallel may lie in the Josiah narrative. When Josiah undertakes his reform, he first gathers the elders and then *all the people*, reads the Book of the Covenant aloud, and only after this public covenantal act does he proceed to destroy the religious order that has been condemned. It’s not simply that both passages contain witnesses. In Josiah’s narrative, the destruction of the Hinnom cult is itself embedded within a publicly witnessed act of religious judgment.

Jeremiah then supplies the other side of the Hinnom tradition. The elders and priests brought to the valley in Jeremiah 19 are summoned to hear the indictment and witness Jeremiah’s symbolic breaking of the potter’s jug.

<BibleVerse reference="Jeremiah 19:1-13">
<p>Thus said the Lord: Go and buy a potter’s earthenware jug. <mark style="background-color: rgba(116, 159, 220, 0.36); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">Take with you some of the elders of the people and some of the senior priests, and go out to the valley of the son of Hinnom at the entry of the Potsherd Gate, and proclaim there the words that I tell you.</mark> You shall say: Hear the word of the Lord, O kings of Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem. Thus says the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel: I am going to bring such disaster upon this place that the ears of everyone who hears of it will tingle. <mark style="background-color: rgba(235, 208, 67, 0.48); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">Because the people have forsaken me and have profaned this place by making offerings in it to other gods whom neither they nor their ancestors nor the kings of Judah have known, and because they have filled this place with the blood of the innocent and gone on building the high places of Baal to burn their children in the fire as burnt offerings to Baal, which I did not command or decree, nor did it enter my mind, therefore the days are surely coming, says the Lord, when this place shall no more be called Topheth or the valley of the son of Hinnom but the valley of Slaughter.</mark> And in this place I will make void the plans of Judah and Jerusalem and will make them fall by the sword before their enemies and by the hand of those who seek their life. I will give their dead bodies for food to the birds of the air and to the wild animals of the earth. And I will make this city a horror, a thing to be hissed at; everyone who passes by it will be horrified and will hiss because of all its disasters. And I will make them eat the flesh of their sons and the flesh of their daughters, and all shall eat the flesh of their neighbors in the siege and in the distress with which their enemies and those who seek their life afflict them. <mark style="background-color: rgba(116, 159, 220, 0.36); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">Then you shall break the jug in the sight of those who go with you</mark> and shall say to them: Thus says the Lord of hosts: So will I break this people and this city as one breaks a potter’s vessel, so that it can never be mended. In Topheth they shall bury until there is no more room to bury. Thus will I do to this place, says the Lord, and to its inhabitants, making this city like Topheth. And the houses of Jerusalem and the houses of the kings of Judah shall be defiled like the place of Topheth—all the houses <mark style="background-color: rgba(214, 154, 83, 0.42); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">upon whose roofs offerings have been made to the whole host of heaven and libations have been poured out to other gods.</mark></p>
</BibleVerse>

The significance of the passage demonstrates that the site of Hinnom could function as a deliberately staged site of public judgment. Jeremiah declares that the place has been filled with the blood of the innocent and announces that it should be called the “Valley of Slaughter.” The judgment reverses the meaning of the site. The valley where innocent blood was shed becomes the place where judgment falls upon the society responsible for it. The same Hinnom tradition appears in Jeremiah 7, where Topheth is associated with the burning of sons and daughters and is again destined to become the Valley of Slaughter:

<BibleVerse reference="Jeremiah 7:30-33">
<p>For the people of Judah have done evil in my sight, says the Lord; they have set their abominations in the house that is called by my name, defiling it. And they go on building the high place of Topheth, which is in the valley of the son of Hinnom, <mark style="background-color: rgba(235, 208, 67, 0.48); color: inherit; border-radius: 2px; padding: 0 0.08em;">to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire</mark>—which I did not command, nor did it come into my mind. Therefore the days are surely coming, says the Lord, when it will no more be called Topheth or the valley of the son of Hinnom but the valley of Slaughter, for they will bury in Topheth until there is no more room.</p>
</BibleVerse>

In the Hinnom tradition, the powerful use fire against the innocent; the same place is then transformed into an image of punishment awaiting those responsible. Q85 follows the same movement. The persecutors sit around the fire while believers are burned, but the surah then declares that God witnesses all things.

## IV. The Reversal (v. 10): Gehenna and the Gardens
<QuranVerse verse="85:10" />

The fire the persecutors lit becomes their own inheritance. The word the Quran uses is *Jahannam* (Gehenna/Hell) and that word is not a native Arabic coinage. Its lineage is uncontroversial in reference scholarship: Hebrew *Gēʾ (ben-) Hinnōm*, “*the Valley of* (the son of) *Hinnom*,” passes into Aramaic as *Gēhinnām*, then into Greek and Syriac as *Gehenna*, and finally into Arabic as *Jahannam*.[^15] Classical exegetes like al-Ṭabarī already knew this, connecting *Jahannam* directly to the historical valley outside Jerusalem’s walls.[^16] The Quran’s name for hell is, linguistically, a specific valley.

That valley has a long biblical history, and it is a history of fire, judgment, and spectators—the same three elements Q85 needs. In the Hebrew Bible, the Valley of Hinnom held Topheth, a site associated with child sacrifice by fire to a god named Molech. King Manasseh instituted the practice, and 2nd Kings reports that after building altars “*to all the host of heaven,*” he made his son pass through the fire of offering. Generations later, as mentioned earlier, King Josiah reverses it. He destroys the objects made “*for the sun, the moon, the constellations, and all the host of heaven,*” then defiles Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom so it can never be used again.

Isaiah, describing Topheth directly, calls it “*deep and large,*” its burning pile fed by “*fire and much wood.*” A few centuries later, the apocalyptic text known as 1 Enoch elaborates the same site into “*the accursed valley,*” a place of judgment where the condemned are gathered and where the punishment becomes “*the spectacle of a just judgment... in the presence of the just.*”[^17]

The direction of the fire has changed. The fire once inflicted by the powerful becomes an instrument of divine judgment against the powerful. Isaiah develops the image further by describing Topheth as “deep and large,” with a burning pile prepared for the king. Q85 follows the same move in that the persecutors sit around the fire while believers are burned, but the surah then declares that those who persecute believing men and women and fail to repent have incurred the retribution of Gehenna and burning. The fire they administered is about to become the punishment awaiting them.

### The Guardian’s Name: Mālik and the Ghost of Molech
The Valley of Hinnom’s fire cult, per the Hebrew Bible, was dedicated to a figure the text calls Molech. That name has a strange history. Biblical scholars have argued since the nineteenth-century work of Abraham Geiger that Molech is very unlikely to have been the deity’s real name.[^18] The Hebrew consonants are simply m-l-k, the root behind the ordinary word *melekh*, “king.” The vowels that turn *melekh* into the sinister-sounding ‘Molech’ are borrowed from a different word entirely, *boshet*, “shame,” the same technique the biblical text uses elsewhere to deform the name of the god Baal’s consort into “Ashtoreth.”[^19] Strip away that layer of deliberate mockery, and what remains underneath ‘Molech’ is simply ‘the King,’ a title, not a foreign proper name. That same title, without the shame-vowel distortion, is independently attested across the wider West Semitic world, as Malik or Muluk in the Mari archive as early as the eighteenth century BCE, and in later Northwest Semitic sources that a number of Semitists, including John Day in his study of the cult, connect to older traditions of an underworld deity bearing this title.[^20]

The Quran’s name for the angel who guards *Jahannam* is Mālik, mentioned by name in Q43:77. Same root, same core sense: the one who rules, holds power, possesses. This does not prove that the Quran is drawing directly on the Molech material; a shared triliteral root across Semitic languages is common enough that a coincidence cannot be ruled out on this point alone. But it’s more than a coincidental root match. It is continuity with a specific, ancient, pre-Islamic title for a fire-and-judgment figure, a title that predates the rabbinic distortion of it into ‘Molech’ and that the Quran’s vocabulary for hell happens to reproduce almost exactly. In a way, the Quran’s hell has a ‘king’ (Malik) with the same name as the valley’s own king once carried.

## V. Summary
Q85 describes persecutors who dig a fire filled trench, sit around it, and watch believers burn inside. Muslim tradition has offered two explanations for this scene. The massacre at Najran and the furnace from the Book of Daniel each solve only part of the puzzle. Najran supplies a real trench, but the wrong kind of persecutor, since Dhu Nuwas was a monotheist rather than an idolater. Daniel supplies the right kind of persecutor, but the wrong kind of fire, since every version of that story describes an oven rather than a valley. This article proposes a different source for the scene. It points to the Valley of Hinnom outside ancient Jerusalem, where kings once burned children as offerings to a fire god the Bible calls Molech. That valley’s Hebrew name passed through Aramaic and Greek into Arabic as *Jahannam*. The Quran’s own word for Hell already points back to this specific place.

Taken together, these threads point to a single conclusion. The people of the canyon in Q85 are best read not as Najran or Daniel’s furnace, but as the Valley of Hinnom itself, the same valley whose name became *Jahannam*: the Quran’s own word for Hell.
