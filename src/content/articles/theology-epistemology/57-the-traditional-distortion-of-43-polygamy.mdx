  <div class="article-opening__kicker">Quran 4:3 · Marriage · Orphan Justice</div>
  <h2>The verse begins with orphans. Later law often begins with men.</h2>
  <p>Quran 4:3 is usually introduced as “the polygamy verse.” That label already shifts the reader’s attention. The verse does not open with male desire, household expansion, or a general right to multiple wives. It opens with fear of injustice toward orphans.</p>
  <p>The traditional reading then creates a second problem. It treats 4:3 as a clear Quranic limit of four wives, while hadith and sira reports portray the Prophet with nine wives at the same time. The usual solution is to create a prophetic exception. But that solution has to be tested against the Quran's own language about the Prophet following revelation, not standing above it.</p>
</div>

<ClaimBox>
The argument is not that every historical form of plural marriage is identical, or that 4:3 has no numerical force. The argument is that the verse is conditional, justice-centered, and tied to vulnerability. Turning it into a broad male entitlement already distorts the verse before the number four is even discussed.
</ClaimBox>

<QuranVerse verse="4:3" />

## The Shape of the Verse

The verse has a clear internal movement:

<div class="verse-flow">
  <div>
    <span>Condition</span>
    <h3>If you fear injustice toward orphans</h3>
    <p>The opening concern is not sexual access or male privilege. It is fear of failing the vulnerable.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Permission</span>
    <h3>Then marry two, three, or four</h3>
    <p>The permission appears inside a social problem, not as a detached marital ideal.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Restriction</span>
    <h3>If you fear injustice, then one</h3>
    <p>The verse ends by pushing the reader back toward reduced harm, not expanded desire.</p>
  </div>
</div>

That structure matters. If the fear of injustice is removed from the verse, the whole moral logic changes. The verse becomes a permission slip. Inside its own wording, however, the permission is surrounded by caution.

## The Early Tafsir Did Not Forget the Orphans

The earliest exegetical record does not read 4:3 as a free-floating celebration of polygamy. It repeatedly connects the verse to orphan property, orphan girls, and fear of injustice.

In Sahih al-Bukhari, Urwa asks Aisha about the verse. Her answer places the verse in the setting of an orphan girl under the care of a guardian who shares her property. Her wealth and beauty tempt the guardian to marry her without giving her the dowry she would receive from someone else.[^1]

<HadithBlock label="Aisha on Quran 4:3">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
هَذِهِ الْيَتِيمَةُ تَكُونُ فِي حِجْرِ وَلِيِّهَا، تُشَارِكُهُ فِي مَالِهِ، فَيُعْجِبُهُ مَالُهَا وَجَمَالُهَا، فَيُرِيدُ وَلِيُّهَا أَنْ يَتَزَوَّجَهَا بِغَيْرِ أَنْ يُقْسِطَ فِي صَدَاقِهَا
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“This is about the orphan girl who lives under her guardian and shares his property. Her wealth and beauty please him, so her guardian wants to marry her without being fair in her bridal gift.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

Al-Tabari preserves several explanations. Some connect the verse to men taking orphan property to fund numerous marriages. Others say that just as people feared injustice with orphans, they should also fear injustice toward women. Qatada says men in the pre-Islamic period would marry ten or fewer women, and that God permitted four and reduced them to four. Al-Tabari's preferred explanation is that the verse warns against injustice in women just as the previous verse warned against injustice in orphan wealth.[^2]

<div class="source-ledger">
  <div>
    <span>Aisha</span>
    <p>The verse addresses a guardian tempted to marry an orphan girl under his care without giving her a fair dowry.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Ikrima / Ibn Abbas strand</span>
    <p>Some reports connect the verse to men taking orphan wealth while marrying many women.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Qatada / al-Tabari</span>
    <p>The verse moves from fear of injustice toward orphans to fear of injustice toward women, limiting pre-Islamic excess.</p>
  </div>
</div>

These are not identical interpretations. That is important. The early record itself shows that the verse was being explained through a cluster of justice concerns: orphan girls, orphan wealth, excessive wives, and unequal treatment of women. None of those readings makes the verse a simple general entitlement.

## Is “Two, Three, or Four” a Hard Limit?

Traditional law treats the verse as establishing a maximum of four wives. It also uses reports in which men who entered Islam with more than four wives were told to keep four and separate from the rest. The report of Ghaylan b. Salama is the most famous example: he accepted Islam with ten wives, and the Prophet reportedly commanded him to choose four of them.[^3]

<HadithBlock label="The Ghaylan report">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
أَنَّ غَيْلَانَ بْنَ سَلَمَةَ الثَّقَفِيَّ أَسْلَمَ وَلَهُ عَشْرُ نِسْوَةٍ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ، فَأَسْلَمْنَ مَعَهُ، فَأَمَرَهُ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ أَنْ يَتَخَيَّرَ أَرْبَعًا مِنْهُنَّ
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Ghaylan b. Salama al-Thaqafi accepted Islam while he had ten wives from the pre-Islamic period, and they accepted Islam with him. The Prophet ﷺ commanded him to choose four of them.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

There is a real debate here. The phrase <em>mathna wa-thulatha wa-rubaʿ</em> is distributive: twos, threes, and fours. The same pattern appears in 35:1 for the wings of angels.

<QuranVerse verse="35:1" />

That parallel matters because 35:1 immediately says that God adds to creation what He wills. So the phrase itself is not always a mathematical ceiling by its own grammar. In 4:3, the traditional ceiling comes from the verse's legal reading, the justice condition, and the hadith reports used by jurists. It is not simply the grammar alone.

<div class="comparison-panel">
  <div>
    <span>Quran 4:3</span>
    <h3>mathna wa-thulatha wa-rubaʿ</h3>
    <p>Plural marriage appears inside a warning about justice and orphans.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Quran 35:1</span>
    <h3>mathna wa-thulatha wa-rubaʿ</h3>
    <p>The same numerical form describes angelic wings, then adds that God increases creation as He wills.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This creates a problem for simplistic traditional claims. If four is a Quranic ceiling, then the later reports about the Prophet's nine wives need a Quranic explanation. If four is not a ceiling by grammar alone, then traditionalists should stop pretending that 4:3 is a simple proof-text for their later legal package.

## The Hadith Problem: Nine Wives

Sahih al-Bukhari preserves the report from Anas that the Prophet would go around to all his wives in one night and that he had nine wives.[^4]

<HadithBlock label="Sahih al-Bukhari 5068">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
كَانَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ يَدُورُ عَلَى نِسَائِهِ فِي اللَّيْلَةِ الْوَاحِدَةِ، وَلَهُ تِسْعُ نِسْوَةٍ
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“The Prophet ﷺ used to go around to his wives in one night, and he had nine wives.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is where the tension becomes unavoidable. The same tradition that reads 4:3 as a four-wife limit also preserves reports in which the Prophet has nine wives. Traditional law answers by saying the Prophet had special rules. The Quran does give the Prophet some specific marriage rulings in 33:50.

<QuranVerse verse="33:50" />

But 33:52 then says:

<QuranVerse verse="33:52" />

Whatever one does with these verses, they do not allow a lazy answer. The Quran does not present the Prophet as an independent legislator who may simply override God's boundaries.

<QuranVerse verse="10:15" />

The traditional solution therefore has to do more than say, “The Prophet is an exception.” It has to show that the exception is clear in the Quran itself, and that the reports about nine simultaneous wives match the Quran's own sequence and restrictions.

## The Actual Dilemma

<div class="dilemma-grid">
  <div>
    <span>Option 1</span>
    <h3>4:3 is a hard Quranic cap of four.</h3>
    <p>Then hadith reports of nine simultaneous wives require a clear Quranic exception. The burden is on the tradition to show that exception rather than assume it.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Option 2</span>
    <h3>4:3 is not a cap by grammar alone.</h3>
    <p>Then the familiar four-wife rule depends heavily on juristic tradition and reports like Ghaylan, not on an obvious standalone reading of the verse.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Option 3</span>
    <h3>4:3 is conditional and justice-centered.</h3>
    <p>This reading does not begin with male entitlement. It begins with vulnerability, fairness, and fear of harm.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The third option is the one most faithful to the verse's moral movement. It does not need to solve every later legal question at once. It simply refuses to let later law detach the verse from its own opening condition.

## Justice Is Not a Decorative Clause

The verse does not merely say, “marry two, three, or four.” It says that if you fear you will not be just, then one.

<QuranVerse verse="4:3" />

Then later in the same surah, the Quran says that people will not be able to be fully just between wives, even if they desire to do so.

<QuranVerse verse="4:129" />

Traditional law often distinguishes between material justice and emotional justice. A jurist may say that 4:3 requires fair maintenance, time, and outward treatment, while 4:129 says perfect emotional equality is impossible. That distinction is coherent as a legal harmonization. But it should not erase the force of the warning. The Quran itself places plural marriage under the shadow of injustice.

<div class="warning-panel">
  <h3>The verse does not encourage expansion. It warns against harm.</h3>
  <p>Every movement in the verse narrows the permission when justice is feared: from plural marriage to one, and from male initiative to accountability before God. That is why the common presentation of polygamy as a standing male right is a distortion of emphasis even before one reaches the legal details.</p>
</div>

## How the Distortion Happens

The distortion has three steps.

<div class="distortion-steps">
  <div>
    <b>1</b>
    <h3>Detach the phrase from the condition</h3>
    <p>“Two, three, or four” is lifted out of the orphan-justice frame and treated as a general male allowance.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <b>2</b>
    <h3>Minimize the justice warning</h3>
    <p>The verse's own movement toward one wife is treated as a technical footnote rather than the ethical center of the passage.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <b>3</b>
    <h3>Use hadith to manage contradiction</h3>
    <p>Reports of the Prophet's nine wives are preserved, then handled by an assumed exception that many readers never test against the Quran.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This is why the popular discourse is so often backwards. The Quranic passage is cautious and conditional. The later discourse becomes expansive and defensive.

## What the Verse Actually Gives

Quran 4:3 gives a limited permission in a setting of vulnerability. It is not an ideal. It is not a command. It is not a male status symbol. It is not a tool for humiliating a first wife or collecting women. The verse says that if justice is feared, reduce. It does not say that if desire increases, expand.

<ContextNote>
A Quran-centered reading does not need to romanticize the classical practice of polygamy. It also does not need to deny that the verse contains a numerical permission. It needs to keep the number inside the condition, and the condition inside the broader Quranic demand for justice.
</ContextNote>

## Final Reading

<HadithBlock label="Quran 4:3 — explanatory rendering">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“If you fear that you will not deal justly with the orphans, then marry from among women in twos, threes, or fours, but if you fear that you will not be just, then one. This is nearer to avoiding injustice.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

The rendering leaves the verse's tension intact. It does not pretend that the verse has no plural-marriage permission. It also does not let the permission swallow the condition.

<VerdictBox title="Final Verdict">
The traditional distortion of Quran 4:3 is not simply the claim that four is the maximum. The deeper distortion is the removal of the verse from its orphan-justice setting. The Quran frames plural marriage as a guarded response to vulnerability and injustice. Later tradition often reframed it as a broad male entitlement, then used prophetic-exception claims to manage reports that appear to strain the four-wife limit.
</VerdictBox>

## Conclusion

Quran 4:3 begins with fear of injustice toward orphans and ends with the instruction to marry one if justice is feared. The verse is cautious from beginning to end. Early tafsir preserved that concern, even while jurists developed the four-wife ceiling. The hadith tradition then introduced another pressure by portraying the Prophet with nine wives, forcing later scholars to create an exception.

A Quran-centered reading does not have to follow that path. It can keep the verse where it begins: with justice, vulnerability, and restraint. If the condition is absent, the verse is being misused. If justice is feared, the verse points to one. And if later reports or legal customs turn this warning into a broad entitlement, then the problem is not with the Quranic text. The problem is with the tradition that moved the center of the verse from the orphan to the man.
