  <h2>The paradox is not disagreement. The paradox is takfir.</h2>
  <p>Salafis can disagree with al-Nawawi, al-Subki, Ibn al-Jazari, al-Shawkani, or later Sunni manuals. Disagreement by itself is normal. The problem begins when the disagreement is turned into a doctrine of major shirk while those same scholars remain part of the inherited Sunni library.</p>
  <p>If asking the Prophet or the righteous dead for intercession, aid, or mediation is major shirk in the obvious foundations of religion, then the historical implications do not stop with ordinary shrine visitors. They reach jurists, hadith commentators, Quran reciters, devotional authors, and legal authorities whom Salafis still read, cite, and depend on.</p>
</div>

<ClaimBox>
This article is not defending posthumous tawassul or istighatha from the Quran. The Quranic standard remains God alone. The point here is narrower: the modern Salafi accusation of major shirk does not sit comfortably with the actual Sunni tradition that Salafis claim to preserve.
</ClaimBox>

## Terms Before the Argument

The debate becomes confused when several different practices are forced under one word. Classical writers, modern Salafis, and ordinary Muslims do not always mean the same thing by <em>tawassul</em> and <em>istighatha</em>.

<div class="term-grid">
  <div class="term-card">
    <span>Tawassul</span>
    <h3>Asking God by a means</h3>
    <p>This usually means asking God through the Prophet, a righteous person, a good deed, a rank, a blessing, or a living person’s supplication. Salafis often treat some forms as bidʿa, but Ibn Baz himself says asking God by the rank, blessing, or right of someone is a bidʿa, not shirk.[^1]</p>
  </div>
  <div class="term-card">
    <span>Istishfaʿ</span>
    <h3>Seeking intercession</h3>
    <p>This can mean asking God to grant the Prophet’s intercession, or directly addressing the Prophet after death and asking him to intercede. Ibn Baz classifies the latter as major shirk.[^2]</p>
  </div>
  <div class="term-card">
    <span>Istighatha</span>
    <h3>Seeking aid</h3>
    <p>The word can mean asking a living person for help in what he can do, asking God by means of a beloved servant, or calling upon a dead or absent figure for aid. Salafi polemics often treat the last category as the controlling meaning.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The article is about the place where those categories collide. If a man says, “O Messenger of God, intercede for me with your Lord,” is he a sinner, an innovator, a mistaken jurist, or a polytheist? Modern Salafi answers often move quickly to the last category. The older Sunni record is not that tidy.

## The Salafi Ruling

Ibn Baz states the modern Salafi position clearly. Asking the Prophet, or any other dead figure, for intercession is not merely impermissible. It is major shirk.

<HadithBlock title="Ibn Baz on asking the Prophet for intercession after death">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
فَالْحَاصِلُ: أَنَّ طَلَبَ الشَّفَاعَةِ مِنَ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ، أَوْ مِنْ غَيْرِهِ مِنَ الأَمْوَاتِ؛ أَمْرٌ لا يَجُوزُ، وَهُوَ عَلَى القَاعِدَةِ الشَّرْعِيَّةِ مِنَ الشِّرْكِ الأَكْبَرِ؛ لأَنَّهُ طَلَبٌ مِنَ المَيِّتِ شَيْئًا لا يَقْدِرُ عَلَيْهِ.
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“In summary, requesting intercession from the Prophet ﷺ, or from anyone else among the dead, is not permissible. According to the legal principle, it is major shirk, because it is asking the dead for something they cannot do.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

The same fatwa says there is no difference between asking this from the Prophet, from ʿAbd al-Qadir, from al-Badawi, from al-Husayn, or from anyone else. Ibn Baz includes requests for aid, intercession, victory, and relief among the actions of pre-Islamic polytheism.[^2]

This is not a small claim. If the category is **major shirk**, then the issue is no longer a disputed devotional etiquette. It becomes a boundary of Islam itself.

Ibn Baz also speaks strongly about ignorance in creed. He says that a Muslim living among Muslims, hearing the Quran and hadith, is not excused in the foundations of the religion, and he places calling upon grave-dwellers, asking them for healing, and seeking their aid among the greatest forms of shirk.[^3]

<div class="paradox-map">
  <div class="paradox-node">
    <span>1</span>
    <p>Posthumous request for intercession or aid is major shirk.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="paradox-arrow">→</div>
  <div class="paradox-node">
    <span>2</span>
    <p>Ignorance is not easily excused in obvious foundations of tawhid.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="paradox-arrow">→</div>
  <div class="paradox-node paradox-node--final">
    <span>3</span>
    <p>Classical Sunni authorities who endorsed the practice become a theological problem.</p>
  </div>
</div>

That is the paradox. It is not enough to say, “They were wrong.” Salafi rhetoric often says much more than that.

## Muhammad b. Abd al-Wahhab Was More Careful About Tawassul

There is a point that should not be skipped. Muhammad b. Abd al-Wahhab himself distinguished tawassul from direct supplication to created beings. In a response about asking God through the righteous, he says the difference is very clear. He describes tawassul through the righteous as a matter of fiqh, says the correct view for him is that it is disliked, and then says there is no harsh condemnation of the one who does it because disputed ijtihad matters are not treated that way.[^4]

<HadithBlock title="Muhammad b. Abd al-Wahhab on tawassul through the righteous">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
فَكَوْنُ بَعْضٍ يُرَخِّصُ بِالتَّوَسُّلِ بِالصَّالِحِينَ، وَبَعْضُهُمْ يَخُصُّهُ بِالنَّبِيِّ ﷺ، وَأَكْثَرُ العُلَمَاءِ يَنْهَى عَنْ ذَلِكَ وَيَكْرَهُهُ، فَهَذِهِ المَسْأَلَةُ مِنْ مَسَائِلِ الفِقْهِ ... فَلا نُنْكِرُ عَلَى مَنْ فَعَلَهُ؛ وَلا إِنْكَارَ فِي مَسَائِلِ الاجْتِهَادِ.
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Some permit tawassul through the righteous, some restrict it to the Prophet ﷺ, and most scholars forbid it and dislike it. This issue is among the issues of fiqh ... We do not condemn the one who does it, for there is no condemnation in matters of ijtihad.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

He then contrasts that with a person who goes to the grave of ʿAbd al-Qadir or someone else and asks for relief from distress, rescue, and worldly needs. For him, those are not the same case.[^4]

This matters because it exposes a later tightening. Some modern Salafi speech collapses the distinction between disliked tawassul, asking a dead person to pray, and worshipful supplication to other than God. Once everything is called major shirk, the inherited Sunni record becomes almost impossible to handle honestly.

## The Classical Sunni Record

The strongest argument is not that every Sunni scholar had the same view. They did not. The stronger argument is that posthumous tawassul, istishfaʿ, and related devotional language are found inside major Sunni manuals, not only in marginal folk practice.

<div class="source-ledger">
  <div class="source-card">
    <div class="source-card__top">
      <span>Shafiʿi law</span>
      <b>al-Nawawi</b>
    </div>
    <p class="source-card__arabic">ثُمَّ يَرْجِعُ إِلَى مَوْقِفِهِ الأَوَّلِ قُبَالَةَ وَجْهِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ وَيَتَوَسَّلُ بِهِ فِي حَقِّ نَفْسِهِ وَيَسْتَشْفِعُ بِهِ إِلَى رَبِّهِ</p>
    <p>In the visitation section of <em>al-Majmuʿ</em>, al-Nawawi says the visitor returns to face the Messenger of God, seeks tawassul through him for himself, and seeks his intercession with his Lord.[^5]</p>
  </div>

  <div class="source-card">
    <div class="source-card__top">
      <span>Anti-Ibn Taymiyya polemic</span>
      <b>Taqi al-Din al-Subki</b>
    </div>
    <p class="source-card__arabic">يَجُوزُ وَيَحْسُنُ التَّوَسُّلُ وَالاسْتِغَاثَةُ وَالتَّشَفُّعُ بِالنَّبِيِّ ﷺ إِلَى رَبِّهِ</p>
    <p>In <em>Shifa al-Siqam</em>, al-Subki treats tawassul, istighatha, and seeking intercession through the Prophet as known and commendable, then criticizes Ibn Taymiyya for denying it.[^6]</p>
  </div>

  <div class="source-card">
    <div class="source-card__top">
      <span>Supplication manual</span>
      <b>Ibn al-Jazari / al-Shawkani</b>
    </div>
    <p class="source-card__arabic">وَيَتَوَسَّلُ إِلَى اللهِ سُبْحَانَهُ بِأَنْبِيَائِهِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ</p>
    <p>Ibn al-Jazari’s devotional manual lists tawassul through God’s prophets and righteous servants among the etiquette of supplication. Al-Shawkani comments on this line and supports it through the blind man report and the Companions’ rain-prayer through al-ʿAbbas.[^7]</p>
  </div>

  <div class="source-card">
    <div class="source-card__top">
      <span>Historical reception</span>
      <b>Ibn Hajar al-ʿAsqalani</b>
    </div>
    <p class="source-card__arabic">وَمِنْهُمْ مَنْ يَنْسِبُهُ إِلَى الزَّنْدَقَةِ لِقَوْلِهِ إِنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ لا يُسْتَغَاثُ بِهِ</p>
    <p>Ibn Hajar records that some of Ibn Taymiyya’s opponents accused him of heresy for saying that the Prophet is not sought for aid. That does not make the accusation true, but it shows how abnormal the denial sounded to some of his contemporaries.[^8]</p>
  </div>
</div>

The point is not that every citation above proves the same legal category. The point is that the devotional world which modern Salafism condemns was not sitting outside Sunni scholarship. It was written into law books, visitation manuals, supplication manuals, and polemical works by scholars who are still treated as part of the Sunni inheritance.

## The al-Nawawi Problem

Al-Nawawi is not an obscure Sufi storyteller. He is one of the most used Sunni scholars in hadith, law, and devotional literature. His commentary on Muslim, his <em>Riyad al-Salihin</em>, and his forty-hadith collection remain everywhere.

That is why his visitation passage matters. The visitor is not told merely to send peace upon the Prophet and leave. He is told to face the Prophet, seek tawassul through him, and seek his intercession with God. Al-Nawawi then preserves the famous al-ʿUtbi story under Quran 4:64, where a Bedouin comes to the Prophet’s grave and says he has come seeking forgiveness and seeking intercession through him.[^5]

<HadithBlock title="The al-ʿUtbi wording as preserved by al-Nawawi">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
وَقَدْ جِئْتُكَ مُسْتَغْفِرًا مِنْ ذَنْبِي مُسْتَشْفِعًا بِكَ إِلَى رَبِّي
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“I have come to you seeking forgiveness for my sin, seeking intercession through you with my Lord.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

A hadith critic can say the al-ʿUtbi story is not a proof-text with a sound isnad. That is fair. But this article is not using the story as proof that the practice is true. It is using al-Nawawi’s legal placement of the story as evidence of what a major Sunni authority considered acceptable devotional language. If Ibn Baz’s category is applied without qualification, al-Nawawi’s manual has a major problem.

## The al-Subki Problem

Al-Subki’s statement is even more direct. He explicitly names tawassul, istighatha, and tashaffuʿ through the Prophet and says that their permissibility and goodness are known in the religious community. He then says no one denied it until Ibn Taymiyya.[^6]

<HadithBlock title="Taqi al-Din al-Subki, Shifa al-Siqam">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
اعْلَمْ أَنَّهُ يَجُوزُ وَيَحْسُنُ التَّوَسُّلُ وَالاسْتِغَاثَةُ وَالتَّشَفُّعُ بِالنَّبِيِّ ﷺ إِلَى رَبِّهِ سُبْحَانَهُ وَتَعَالَى ... وَلَمْ يُنْكِرْ أَحَدٌ ذَلِكَ ... حَتَّى جَاءَ ابْنُ تَيْمِيَّةَ
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Know that it is permissible and good to seek tawassul, istighatha, and intercession through the Prophet ﷺ with his Lord, Glorified and Exalted ... No one denied this ... until Ibn Taymiyya came.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

One can argue that al-Subki overstated the historical consensus. That is possible. But even if the consensus claim is reduced, the paradox remains. A major Shafiʿi authority publicly treated Ibn Taymiyya’s denial as a novelty, while modern Salafi discourse often treats the opposite practice as obvious major shirk.

That is a serious gap in historical memory.

## The Ibn al-Jazari and al-Shawkani Problem

Ibn al-Jazari is central to Quranic recitation. Sunni and Salafi circles do not discard him. Yet the devotional manual associated with him includes tawassul through the prophets and righteous among the etiquette of supplication.

Al-Shawkani’s commentary is important because he was not simply repeating Shafiʿi grave-visit literature. He explains the line by citing the blind man report for tawassul through the Prophet and the rain-prayer through al-ʿAbbas for tawassul through the righteous.[^7]

<ContextNote>
Al-Shawkani is useful here because many modern reformist and Salafi-leaning readers respect him. His discussion does not prove every later shrine practice. It does show that tawassul through prophets and righteous figures could remain inside serious hadith-legal discussion without being treated as major shirk.
</ContextNote>

## The Three Escape Routes

Once these sources are on the table, the Salafi position has only a few ways out.

<div class="escape-grid">
  <div>
    <span>Escape 1</span>
    <h3>Reinterpret the scholars</h3>
    <p>One may argue that al-Nawawi, al-Subki, Ibn al-Jazari, and al-Shawkani meant only lawful tawassul, not direct calling upon the dead. Sometimes this works. Sometimes the wording resists it, especially when the visitor addresses the Prophet at the grave and seeks intercession through him.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Escape 2</span>
    <h3>Excuse them</h3>
    <p>One may say they were mistaken, confused, relying on weak stories, or using inherited language. But if the practice is major shirk in the obvious foundations of religion, the excuse has to be explained. Ibn Baz’s own framework makes that difficult.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Escape 3</span>
    <h3>Downgrade the ruling</h3>
    <p>One may say the issue is bidʿa, disliked tawassul, or a mistaken devotional practice unless the person believes the dead independently control benefit and harm. That is more historically workable, but it weakens the modern habit of calling the whole field major shirk.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The third route is the most coherent historically. It allows a person to reject these practices from a Quranic or Salafi perspective without pretending that much of Sunni scholarship was sitting in obvious polytheism.

## The Real Paradox

The paradox is not that Salafis reject some classical practices. Every reform movement does that. The paradox is that Salafis often claim to represent the saved Sunni inheritance while making theological accusations that, if applied consistently, would place major Sunni authorities under the shadow of major shirk.

<div class="paradox-ledger">
  <div class="ledger-row ledger-row--head">
    <div>Claim</div>
    <div>Historical pressure</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Asking the Prophet after death for intercession is major shirk.</div>
    <div>Al-Nawawi’s visitation passage explicitly uses posthumous tawassul and istishfaʿ language.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Istighatha through the Prophet was obviously forbidden.</div>
    <div>Al-Subki says its permissibility was known and that Ibn Taymiyya’s denial was a novelty.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Tawassul through persons is a route to shirk.</div>
    <div>Ibn al-Jazari lists tawassul through prophets and righteous servants among supplication etiquette, and al-Shawkani explains it as a recognized devotional form.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>The issue was always obvious in Sunni Islam.</div>
    <div>Ibn Hajar records that some contemporaries viewed Ibn Taymiyya’s denial of istighatha through the Prophet as religiously shocking.</div>
  </div>
</div>

This does not prove that al-Nawawi or al-Subki were right. It proves that the Salafi historical story is too clean. The practice was not merely the religion of ignorant grave-worshippers. It existed in the learned tradition, sometimes in places where Salafis still go for law, hadith, Quranic recitation, and devotional vocabulary.

## The Quranic Baseline

From a Quran-centered perspective, there is a separate question: should any of this have authority over the believer? The Quran’s answer is simpler than the Sunni inheritance. Worship and supplication belong to God.

<QuranVerse verse="10:18" />

<QuranVerse verse="39:3" />

These verses are why the article does not need to defend al-Nawawi or al-Subki as correct. The argument is about consistency. A Quranic critique can reject all devotional intermediaries without inheriting the Salafi need to protect Sunni authority. The Salafi has a harder task. He wants to reject the practice as major shirk while still claiming the tradition that preserved it as his own.

<VerdictBox title="Final Verdict">
The Salafi paradox is not solved by saying that some classical scholars were wrong. The problem is that modern Salafi rhetoric often calls these practices major shirk while continuing to depend on a Sunni scholarly inheritance where posthumous tawassul, istishfaʿ, and related devotional language were accepted, defended, or normalized by major authorities. A consistent position must either soften the takfir category, openly demote large parts of the Sunni tradition, or admit that the modern framing is historically much narrower than it claims.
</VerdictBox>

## Conclusion

The sources do not support the simple story that Salafism is just the old Sunni tradition stripped of later excess. On this issue, modern Salafi theology cuts against large parts of the classical record. Al-Nawawi’s visitor faces the Prophet and seeks intercession through him. Al-Subki defends tawassul, istighatha, and tashaffuʿ through the Prophet. Ibn al-Jazari places tawassul through prophets and righteous servants in a supplication manual. Al-Shawkani explains that line through hadith and Companion precedent. Ibn Hajar records how controversial Ibn Taymiyya’s denial of istighatha through the Prophet sounded to some in his own world.

A Muslim may still reject all of this on Quranic grounds. That is a separate argument. But the Salafi claim is different. It says this rejection is the obvious Sunni position, the inherited way of the early community, and that the opposite is major shirk. The historical record does not make that claim easy to maintain.

The cleaner conclusion is that modern Salafism is not simply preserving Sunni orthodoxy. On tawassul and istighatha, it is judging much of that orthodoxy.
