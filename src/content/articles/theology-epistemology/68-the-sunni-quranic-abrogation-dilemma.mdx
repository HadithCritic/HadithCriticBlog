  <h2>The problem is not merely that scholars disagreed. The problem is that the doctrine gives them permission to retire revelation.</h2>
  <p>The Sunni doctrine of Quranic abrogation says that some Quranic rulings were replaced by later rulings. In its narrowest form, it means that a later revealed command cancels an earlier command when both cannot be practiced together. In its wider historical use, however, it became a way to explain away verses that sat uneasily with later law, later warfare theory, or inherited legal assumptions.</p>
  <p>That is where the dilemma begins. If a verse from God can remain in the mushaf while its ruling is treated as no longer operative, then ordinary believers need a reliable way to know which verses still bind them. The classical tradition never produced a universally agreed Quranic index of abrogated verses. It produced lists, counter-lists, reductions, reclassifications, and debates over whether many alleged cases were abrogation at all.</p>
</div>

<ClaimBox>
The argument is not that no Muslim scholar ever tried to define naskh carefully. Some did. The argument is that once intra-Quranic abrogation is accepted, the believer is forced to rely on contested post-Quranic scholarship to know which Quranic commands are still operative.
</ClaimBox>

## The Quranic Baseline

The Quran repeatedly describes God's words as complete, true, just, and not subject to alteration. Any doctrine that says a verse is present in recitation but no longer operative has to be measured against that baseline.

<QuranVerse verse="6:115" />

<QuranVerse verse="18:27" />

<QuranVerse verse="10:64" />

These verses do not disappear because later scholars developed a technical category called naskh. A Muslim can try to argue that abrogation is not "changing God's words" because God Himself replaces one ruling with another. But then the evidence must be clear. It cannot rest on later discomfort, broad contradiction claims, or an inherited habit of declaring difficult verses mansukh.

  </div>

## The Verses Usually Used for Abrogation

The two verses most often invoked are 2:106 and 16:101.

<QuranVerse verse="2:106" />

<QuranVerse verse="16:101" />

These verses mention replacing or causing an <em>aya</em> to be forgotten. The traditional argument reads <em>aya</em> here as a Quranic verse. That reading is possible, but it is not the only possible reading of the word. In the Quran, <em>aya</em> can mean a sign, proof, miracle, or revealed communication. The context of 16:101 also includes the accusation that Muhammad is fabricating, which means the verse is defending God's authority over revelation rather than giving later jurists a blank license to cancel difficult verses.

The safer reading is this: if God replaces one <em>aya</em> with another, that is God's action. It does not follow that later scholars may take every apparent tension, every historical sequence, or every legal inconvenience and declare one Quranic passage inactive.

## Al-Suyuti Tried to Narrow the Chaos

Al-Suyuti did not invent the abrogation problem. He inherited a field already swollen with claims. In <em>al-Itqan fi Ulum al-Quran</em>, he reports that people had greatly multiplied the number of alleged abrogated verses, then he gives a reduced list. He says the refined list has twenty cases and puts it into verse so it can be remembered.[^1]

<HadithBlock label="Al-Suyuti's reduction of the list">
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
قَدْ أَكْثَرَ النَّاسُ فِي الْمَنْسُوخِ مِنْ عَدَدٍ<br />
وَأَدْخَلُوا فِيهِ آيًا لَيْسَ تَنْحَصِرُ<br />
وَهَاكَ تَحْرِيرَ آيٍ لَا مَزِيدَ لَهَا<br />
عِشْرِينَ حَرَّرَهَا الْحُذَّاقُ وَالْكُبَرُ
</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“People have greatly multiplied the number of abrogated verses and have included verses beyond counting. Here is a refined list of verses, with no addition to it: twenty, refined by the skilled and senior scholars.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

This reduction is often presented as a sign of discipline. It is also evidence of the problem. If earlier scholars could multiply abrogation claims beyond measure, and a later scholar has to reduce them, then the method was never self-evident. The Quran did not come with a visible label on each verse saying "operative" or "abrogated."

Al-Suyuti also preserves a stricter methodological warning from Ibn al-Hassar: abrogation should only be known through an explicit report from the Messenger, a Companion saying that one verse abrogated another, or a clear historical contradiction where the earlier and later rulings are known. He says that one does not rely on the opinions of common exegetes or on mere juristic reasoning, because abrogation involves removing one established ruling and affirming another.[^2]

<div class="method-card">
  <div class="method-card__label">The standard al-Suyuti preserves</div>
  <p>Abrogation requires explicit transmission or clear chronological contradiction. It is not supposed to be a tool for every exegete who sees tension between two verses.</p>
</div>

The problem is that this stricter standard undercuts many casual abrogation claims. If the proof must be explicit and historically controlled, then most claims become doubtful. If the proof can be loose and interpretive, then the Quran becomes vulnerable to legal editing by later scholars.

## Ibn al-Jawzi Shows the Same Problem from Another Angle

Ibn al-Jawzi wrote a full work on the abrogating and abrogated verses, <em>Nawasikh al-Quran</em>. The book does not present a simple, universally agreed list. It discusses a large field of alleged abrogation cases. In the printed arrangement available online, the section on verses claimed to be abrogated introduces a discussion of **247 issues across 62 surahs**.[^3]

That number should not be confused with the number of cases Ibn al-Jawzi finally accepts. A serious reader has to distinguish between:

<div class="count-grid">
  <div>
    <span>Claimed cases</span>
    <b>Many</b>
    <p>Verses earlier writers or interpreters alleged to be abrogated.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Accepted cases</span>
    <b>Fewer</b>
    <p>Cases a given scholar actually accepts after applying his criteria.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Rejected cases</span>
    <b>Many again</b>
    <p>Cases later scholars deny because they are better explained as specification, context, exception, or no conflict at all.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This distinction actually strengthens the critique. The tradition itself testifies that large numbers of abrogation claims were made, contested, reduced, and corrected. The doctrine did not simply clarify the Quran. It generated a second-order discipline whose job was to decide which verses people had wrongly retired.

Ibn al-Jawzi himself lists conditions for valid abrogation. One of them is that the two rulings must truly contradict in a way that makes combined practice impossible. Another is that the later ruling must be established by a path equal to or stronger than the earlier one. He also rejects the idea that ordinary custom being replaced by revelation counts as abrogation.[^4]

<ContextNote>
That matters because many alleged abrogations are not real abrogations even by classical standards. A verse may be general and later specified. It may be conditional. It may describe one circumstance while another verse describes another. Calling all of that naskh turns interpretation into cancellation.
</ContextNote>

## The Categories Make the Dilemma Worse

The doctrine did not stop at ordinary legal replacement. Classical manuals discuss several kinds of abrogation, including:

<div class="category-ledger">
  <div class="ledger-row ledger-row--head">
    <div>Type</div>
    <div>Meaning</div>
    <div>Why it creates pressure</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Ruling abrogated, recitation remains</div>
    <div>The verse is still in the Quran, but its legal force is gone.</div>
    <div>The reader encounters God's words but is told not to apply the apparent ruling.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Recitation abrogated, ruling remains</div>
    <div>The words are no longer recited as Quran, but the law supposedly remains.</div>
    <div>A binding law is attached to wording no longer present in the mushaf.</div>
  </div>
  <div class="ledger-row">
    <div>Both recitation and ruling abrogated</div>
    <div>The words and ruling are both said to have been removed.</div>
    <div>The doctrine depends almost entirely on reports outside the present Quranic text.</div>
  </div>
</div>

Al-Suyuti preserves these categories and gives examples, including the famous report attributed to Aisha about ten known sucklings being abrogated by five known sucklings, while some people still recited the latter after the Prophet's death.[^5] He also reports examples of material said to have been revealed and then lifted from recitation, such as reports about the people of Bir Mauna and other alleged missing or lifted passages.[^6]

This is not just a legal theory. It becomes a theory about the history of the Quranic text itself. For a Quran-centered theology, that is not a small matter.

## The Table Problem

The difficulty becomes visible when later scholars and editors build tables of alleged abrogated verses. In one table attached to Ibn al-Jawzi's work, a verse such as 2:109 is listed with scholars who claimed abrogation and scholars who denied it. The same table identifies al-Nahhas among those who claimed abrogation while listing Ibn al-Jawzi, al-Suyuti, and others among those who denied it.[^7]

<div class="case-panel">
  <div class="case-panel__verse">Quran 2:109</div>
  <div class="case-panel__claim">
    <span>Claimed abrogation</span>
    <p>Some treated “forgive and overlook until God brings His command” as abrogated by the fighting verses.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="case-panel__counter">
    <span>Counter-reading</span>
    <p>Others denied abrogation because the verse contains its own limit: “until God brings His command.” A time-limited command is not necessarily cancelled by contradiction.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This kind of case shows why the doctrine is unstable in practice. One scholar sees abrogation. Another sees a verse with a built-in endpoint. Another sees specification. Another sees contextual sequencing. The result is not a revealed index. It is a scholastic sorting process.

## The Epistemological Crisis

A doctrine about divine guidance should not make divine guidance harder to identify. If God intended some verses to remain in the Quran while their rulings no longer apply, then the believer needs more than scholarly confidence. He needs certainty.

The classical tradition often answers by saying that only qualified scholars can know these matters. But that answer shifts the problem. The scholars themselves disagreed. Some expanded the list. Others reduced it. Some called cases abrogation. Others called the same cases specification, restriction, explanation, or time-limited command.

<div class="dilemma-grid">
  <div>
    <span>Option 1</span>
    <h3>Accept broad abrogation</h3>
    <p>The Quran becomes dependent on later scholarly lists to know which commands still operate.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Option 2</span>
    <h3>Accept narrow abrogation</h3>
    <p>Most classical claims collapse, and many famous examples have to be reclassified.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <span>Option 3</span>
    <h3>Reject intra-Quranic cancellation</h3>
    <p>The Quran remains internally binding, while apparent tensions must be solved by context, scope, and careful reading.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The third option fits the Quranic self-description best. It does not require pretending that scholars never noticed tension. It simply refuses to let later inherited tension cancel God's words.

## What Naskh Often Does in Practice

In practice, many abrogation claims do not solve contradictions. They avoid interpretation. A verse about patience is treated as cancelled by a verse about fighting. A verse about forgiveness is treated as cancelled by a verse about combat. A verse about bequest is treated as cancelled by inheritance law. A verse about private consultation is treated as cancelled after one incident. Sometimes this is defended through chronology. Sometimes it is defended by legal need. Sometimes the proof is much weaker than the claim.

The Quran gives a different reading discipline:

<QuranVerse verse="4:82" />

If the Quran contains no contradiction, the first task is reconciliation, not cancellation. A contradiction should not be manufactured by ignoring conditions, context, audience, genre, and scope.

<QuranVerse verse="41:42" />

If falsehood does not come to it from before it or behind it, the burden is on the person who claims that one Quranic statement has legally neutralized another. That burden is rarely met with the kind of certainty the doctrine requires.

<VerdictBox title="Final Verdict">
The Sunni doctrine of Quranic abrogation creates a theological and epistemic problem. It asks believers to accept that some Quranic verses remain recited while no longer ruling, but it does not provide a universally agreed Quranic index for identifying them. The classical tradition preserves reduction, disagreement, and reclassification. A Quran-centered approach should treat every verse as binding within its proper context unless the Quran itself clearly limits it.
</VerdictBox>

## Conclusion

The doctrine of abrogation was supposed to protect the coherence of revelation. In practice, it often made the Quran dependent on later scholarly sorting. Al-Suyuti had to reduce inflated lists. Ibn al-Jawzi had to discuss and sift through large numbers of alleged cases. Classical authorities warned that abrogation cannot rest on loose opinion. Yet the doctrine continued to be used as a way to retire verses from active force.

A book that calls itself complete and unalterable should not require believers to wonder which of God's words are no longer operative. Apparent tension should be handled through context, qualification, and careful interpretation. It should not be handled by building a second book beside the Quran, one that tells the reader which parts of the first book no longer count.
