Sean Anthony has proposed that the Qur'an's account of the end of Jesus's earthly mission is best read against late ancient Jewish polemics against, and parody of, the life of Jesus of Nazareth.[^1] His case rests on a simple observation: Q 4:157 does not argue with a Christian confession. It answers a hostile claim placed in the mouths of Jesus's own opponents — "we killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of God." Anthony shows that the shape of this claim, and of the Qur'an's rebuttal, tracks the anti-Passion narrative preserved in the earliest Aramaic recensions of the *Toledot Yeshu*, the Jewish counter-Gospel in which the sages of Israel try, execute, and bury Jesus, then produce his corpse before Pilate to refute his followers' claim that he ascended to heaven.[^2]

This piece extends Anthony's reorientation of the discussion toward Jewish sources by turning to a different kind of text: the Wisdom of Solomon, a Hellenistic Jewish wisdom book composed centuries before Muḥammad's mission and read as scripture by both Jewish and Christian communities of the late antique Near East. Wisdom 2–5 supplies a scriptural grammar in which the elements of the qur'anic dispute over Jesus's end already appear together, in sequence, applied to an unnamed righteous man rather than to a fictionalized biography of Jesus.

<ClaimBox title="Central Argument">
Wisdom of Solomon 2–5 satisfies, in a single continuous unit, five criteria drawn directly from the Qur'an's own account of Jesus's end: hostile plotting, testing of divine sonship, condemnation to a shameful death, hostile misperception mistaken for knowledge, and divine taking beyond hostile reach. Since Wisdom 2's mockery scene is also the acknowledged literary source of the Synoptic crucifixion taunt in Matthew 27:39–43, the Qur'an's denial in Q 4:157 can be read as answering both a Jewish anti-Gospel narrative of Jesus's execution and the older scriptural idiom in which that execution scene was first cast as a test of divine sonship. Wisdom of Solomon and the Toledot Yeshu traditions are not competing sources — they are two moments in a single stream of Jewish righteous-sufferer literature that the Gospel Passion narratives drew on and that the Qur'an independently answers.
</ClaimBox>

## The Qur'anic Criteria

Q 3:54–55 supplies the earlier of the Qur'an's two accounts of the end of Jesus's mission, in compressed form: the Israelites plot against Jesus, God plots in response and plots best of all, and God then tells Jesus that He will take him, raise him to Himself, and purify him from the disbelievers.

<QuranVerse verse="3:54-55" />

Q 4:156–159 supplies the second account, embedded in an extended polemic against the People of Scripture in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ. Here the Qur'an cites the hostile claim directly, denies both the killing and the crucifixion, states that the matter was made to appear otherwise to the opponents, that those who differed over it were in doubt with no real knowledge, and concludes that God raised Jesus to Himself.

<QuranVerse verse="4:157" />

<QuranVerse verse="4:158" />

Q 5:117 supplies a third, retrospective fragment in Jesus's own voice: as long as he remained among his people he was a witness over them, and once God took him, God alone remained watchful over them.

<QuranVerse verse="5:117" />

Read together, these three passages yield five criteria a Jewish-scriptural analogue to the Qur'an's account should satisfy.[^3] These criteria deliberately set aside Q 4:156's slander against Mary (*buhtān ʿaẓīm*) and its connection to the Panthera legend, since Wisdom of Solomon has no analogue to a charge of adultery against the righteous man's mother — that motif belongs to a different polemical tradition already traced in detail by Anthony.[^4]

### 1. Hostile Plotting against the Righteous Figure

Q 3:54 opens with the plot itself — "*they plotted, and God plotted, and God is the best of plotters.*" The verse gives no content to the Israelites' scheme and lets the divine response define its shape. A relevant analogue needs a righteous figure opposed by hostile agents whose violence or intrigue becomes the occasion for a divine reversal rather than a defeat.

### 2. The Testing of Divine Sonship or Protection

His opponents elsewhere dismiss Jesus's signs as sorcery:

<QuranVerse verse="5:110" />

<QuranVerse verse="61:6" />

which is to say they contest the divine source of his authority rather than merely his person. Anthony reads these two verses as the Qur'an's rebuttal of a specific accusation preserved in the *Toledot Yeshu*, where Jesus's miracles are explained away as learned sorcery.[^5] The two readings are not exclusive — discrediting a claimant's miracles as sorcery and discrediting his claim to divine sonship are two ways of attacking the same thing, since a miracle is precisely the evidence a claim to divine sonship would call on. Wisdom's wicked test the righteous man's sonship through torture and death rather than by explaining away a miracle, but the target in both cases is the same: the reality of the figure's relationship to God, not just his safety or the facts of his biography. Wisdom's wicked never accuse the righteous man of sorcery — the charge has no place in Wisdom 2's anthropology of predation. The sorcery charge belongs to the *Toledot* line of transmission alone; Wisdom supplies the sonship-testing logic behind Q 5:110 and 61:6.

### 3. Condemnation to a Shameful Death

<QuranVerse verse="4:157" />

The addition of crucifixion beyond mere killing matters. Crucifixion in the ancient Mediterranean and Near East was a punishment built for public humiliation as much as for death, and the opponents' claim asserts mastery over Jesus's body and reputation together. A relevant analogue needs the wicked to condemn the righteous figure specifically to a shameful, public death.

### 4. Hostile Misperception Mistaken for Knowledge

Q 4:157 moves from *shubbiha lahum* ("it was made to appear so to them") to *shakk* ("doubt"), *ʿilm* ("knowledge"), *ẓann* ("conjecture"), and *yaqīn* ("certainty"), stripping the opponents' claim of the status of knowledge and reducing it to conjecture. A relevant analogue needs observers who take an appearance for certainty and are shown, within the text itself, to have mistaken the one for the other.

### 5. Divine Taking and Preservation beyond Hostile Reach

<QuranVerse verse="4:158" />

Q 3:55 gives the fuller triad of taking, raising, and purification, and Q 5:117 supplies Jesus's own retrospective description of the moment God took him. Q 39:42 anchors the sense of the verb *tawaffī* used throughout this material, naming God's taking of souls both at death and during sleep and leaving the precise mode of the taking to context rather than to the verb itself.

<QuranVerse verse="39:42" />

<ContextNote title="Why All Five Together">
Plotting, testing, shameful death, misperception, and preservation each recur separately across a wide range of Second Temple and rabbinic literature, and on their own they prove little. What licenses treating a text as a genuine analogue, rather than a coincidental parallel, is that it supplies all five elements in the same sequence, applied to the same kind of figure, within one continuous unit — not scattered across independent compositions.
</ContextNote>

## Control Texts: Other Candidates for the Righteous-Sufferer Analogue

Four other texts belong to the same broad current of Second Temple and post-Temple Jewish literature and have each been proposed as background to the Gospel Passion or related qur'anic material: Isaiah 53, Psalm 22, the court tales of Daniel 3 and 6, and the martyrdom narratives of 2 and 4 Maccabees. Running each against the five criteria shows why none is a rival candidate to Wisdom of Solomon.

**Isaiah 53** satisfies the shameful-death and misperception criteria more clearly than any other candidate. The servant is "cut off from the land of the living" and given "his grave... with the wicked" (Isa 53:8–9), and the community's initial judgment — "we accounted him stricken, struck down by God, and afflicted" (Isa 53:4) — is corrected within the poem itself. But Isaiah 53 supplies no equivalent to the first and second criteria: no named hostile agents are shown plotting against the servant, and his status as God's son is never at issue. Isaiah 53 has no sonship language at all.[^6]

**Psalm 22** comes closer than Isaiah 53 to the sonship-testing criterion, since the psalmist's tormentors mock him in terms that anticipate both Wisdom 2 and the Gospel taunt at the cross: "He committed his cause to the Lord, let him deliver him, let him rescue him, for he delights in him" (Ps 22:8) — the same logic that organizes Wisdom 2:18 and Matthew 27:43, a connection well established in Passion-narrative scholarship.[^7] The psalm stages hostile plotting too, with the psalmist encircled by "a company of evildoers" (Ps 22:16). What it lacks is a narrated misperception for the text to correct — the psalm moves directly from lament to vindication — and its resolution is deliverance within the psalmist's own lifetime, not a taking or raising after the fact.

**Daniel 3 and 6** supply the hostile-plot criterion as directly as any text considered here, and something like the sonship-testing criterion, since the officials' scheme doubles as a test of whether the protagonists' God can and will save them (Dan 3:15, 6:20).[^8] But Nebuchadnezzar and Darius witness the deliverance directly and in real time — a fourth figure in the furnace, the lions' mouths shut before onlookers — rather than a hidden reality later revealed. There is no death, no misperception, and no taking.

**2 and 4 Maccabees** supply the hostile-plot and shameful-death criteria as fully as Wisdom does. Antiochus's persecution is a sustained hostile plot, and the martyrs voice a hope for bodily resurrection — "the King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life" (2 Macc 7:9) — gesturing toward the fifth criterion.[^9] But the martyrs are tested on fidelity to the ancestral law, not on a claim to be God's son, and the narrative never stages a reversal of perception; Antiochus is never shown mistaking apparent defeat for real defeat.

No single control text satisfies more than three of the five criteria, and none combines the sonship test with corrected misperception — the pairing structurally closest to the Qur'an's own rhetorical move in Q 4:157, where the opponents' claim is dismantled by stripping their certainty of the status of knowledge. Wisdom of Solomon 2–5 is the only text that supplies all five elements together, in the same sequence, applied to the same figure, within a single continuous unit.

## The Wisdom of Solomon as Jewish-Scriptural Analogue

The Wisdom of Solomon is a work of Alexandrian diaspora Judaism, composed in literary Greek and voiced through the persona of Solomon, most often dated to the late first century BCE or early first century CE.[^10] Its author combines biblical narrative, wisdom exhortation, and Hellenistic philosophical vocabulary in service of a single argument: the righteous who appear to suffer and die are in fact secure in God, while the wicked who appear to triumph over them are already living under judgment.[^11] Karina Hogan has shown that the book's retelling of Israel's history treats biblical narratives as paradigms of righteous and wicked conduct rather than continuous history tied to a single ethnic community, which gives its opening chapters a portable, generalized cast rather than a biography of a named hero.[^12] Larry Wills describes chapters 2–5 as staging a contest between two worldviews — the wicked, vocal and violent, and the righteous man, who says nothing and waits for a reversal that arrives only after his apparent defeat.[^13] That structure is what makes the passage useful for the Qur'an's account, since Q 4:157–158 is likewise organized around a hostile claim, an unanswering silence on its target's part, and a divine reversal delivered after the fact rather than staged as a real-time rescue.

### Wisdom 2: The Wicked Plot and Test the Righteous Man

Wisdom 2 opens with the reasoning of the ungodly: life is short, death final, the soul no more than breath that disperses. From this anthropology the wicked derive an ethic of predation. Their hostility settles on one figure in particular — a righteous man who exposes their conduct as sin against the law, calls himself a child of God, and boasts that God is his father.

<BibleVerse reference="Wisdom of Solomon 2:17-18" eyebrow="Apocrypha">
<p>"Let us see if his words are true, and let us test what will happen at the end of his life, for if the righteous man is God's son, he will help him, and will deliver him from the hand of his adversaries."</p>
</BibleVerse>

They move from proposition to action, deciding to examine his patience with insult and torture and to condemn him to a shameful death, reasoning that his own claim of divine protection will be tested by whether that protection actually arrives (Wis 2:19–20). The sequence answers the first three criteria at once. The wicked plot, as the Israelites plot against Jesus in Q 3:54. Their test targets the righteous man's claim to be God's son, not simply his life — the same way the sorcery charge in Q 5:110 and Q 61:6 targets Jesus's claim to divine backing rather than simply his safety. Their chosen instrument is a shameful death, exactly the qualifier the Qur'an adds in Q 4:157 by naming crucifixion alongside killing.

### Sonship Reworded: The Wicked's Boast in the Qur'an

Wisdom's wicked do not attack an anonymous holy man. They quote him: he "professes to have knowledge of God, and calls himself a child of the Lord" (Wis 2:13), and "boasts that God is his father" (Wis 2:16). The wicked's own summary of his claim supplies the theology they intend to test — sonship of God, stated as directly as Hellenistic Greek allows.

The Qur'an's opponents make the same structural move in Q 4:157 — they too are quoted boasting about a man they claim to have killed — but the content of the boast has changed. They do not say "we killed the son of God." They say "we killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of God." The title that would answer to Wisdom's "child of the Lord" is absent. Two other titles stand in its place: son of Mary, and messenger of God.

<QuranVerse verse="4:171" />

This is not an isolated turn of phrase. Q 4:171 makes the identical substitution outside any hostile speech, addressing the People of Scripture directly. The formula the Qur'an places in the mouths of Jesus's killers is the same formula the Qur'an uses for its own confession of Jesus.

This kind of move — folding a doctrinal correction into a reported claim rather than refuting it afterward — is a documented feature of how the Qur'an handles material drawn from Jewish and Christian tradition. Much of the Qur'an's engagement with biblical narrative proceeds by subtext: it assumes the audience already knows a prior text, then reshapes that text's details to make a theological point rather than arguing with it directly, as Gabriel Reynolds has shown.[^14] Neal Robinson's study of qur'anic Christology treats "son of Mary" as a title chosen in deliberate contrast to Christian confessional language about Jesus, not a neutral genealogical marker.[^15] Sidney Griffith's account of the Bible's presence in the Qur'an's Arabic environment describes the same pattern: a text speaking in the vocabulary of the biblical tradition it addresses while redirecting that vocabulary toward its own claims.[^16] Set against Wisdom 2's "child of the Lord" and "God is his father," the Qur'an's "son of Mary, the messenger of God" reads as exactly this kind of move.

### Wisdom 3: Apparent Death and the Reversal of Perception

Wisdom 3 opens by placing the fate of the righteous beyond the reach of the wicked's test.

<BibleVerse reference="Wisdom of Solomon 3:1-3" eyebrow="Apocrypha">
<p>"The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them. In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died, and their departure was thought to be an affliction, and their going from us to be their destruction, but they are at peace."</p>
</BibleVerse>

The chapter states its opposition between perception and reality as plainly as any text in Second Temple literature could state it — the foolish read a departure as a death and a death as a destruction, and the text corrects them from outside their own perspective, in the same gesture by which Q 4:157 moves from denying the hostile act to denying that the opponents possessed any knowledge of what they claimed to have witnessed.

Karina Hogan's study of what she calls the "ambiguity of death" in Wisdom, building on Michael Kolarcik's earlier analysis of the book's literary structure, shows this reversal is deliberate rather than incidental. Wisdom exploits the same vocabulary of death for two audiences at once: for the wicked, physical death confirms their belief in annihilation; for the righteous, the identical event marks the end of suffering and the beginning of nearness to God.[^17] That ambiguity is the interpretive space the Qur'an occupies in Q 4:157, where the opponents' certainty about Jesus's death is exposed as conjecture rather than corrected by a competing set of facts.

### Wisdom 4: The Righteous Man Taken from among Sinners

Wisdom 4 turns from apparent death to the logic of removal: the one who pleases God is loved by Him and taken away while still living among sinners.

<BibleVerse reference="Wisdom of Solomon 4:10-11" eyebrow="Apocrypha">
<p>"There was one who pleased God and was loved by him, and while living among sinners was taken up. He was caught up so that evil might not change his understanding or guile deceive his soul."</p>
</BibleVerse>

The people who witness this fail to understand it, unaware that grace and mercy belong to God's elect and that He watches over His holy ones (Wis 4:14–15). This passage corresponds most closely to Q 3:55.

## Wisdom 2 and the Gospel Passion Narrative

The correspondence between Wisdom and the Qur'an's Jesus material gains a further layer once Wisdom 2's place in the New Testament's own Passion tradition is taken into account. Matthew's account of the mockery at the cross has long been read as drawing directly on Wisdom 2's scene of the wicked testing a self-proclaimed son of God.

<BibleVerse reference="Matthew 27:43">
<p>"He trusts in God; let God deliver him now, if he wants to; for he said, 'I am God's Son.'"</p>
</BibleVerse>

This reproduces the logic, and much of the vocabulary, of Wisdom 2:18. Douglas Moo's study of the Old Testament and deuterocanonical background of the Gospel Passion narratives treats the Matthean taunt as dependent on Wisdom 2 alongside Psalm 22, and commentators on Matthew have long placed the two texts side by side for exactly this reason.[^18]

This connection changes what a comparison between the Qur'an and Wisdom of Solomon can claim. The Qur'an's opponents in Q 4:157 boast that they killed and crucified Jesus in circumstances that, on the Gospel's own telling, were narrated in the language Wisdom 2 had already supplied for the mockery of a righteous man tested as God's son. Anthony's *Toledot Yeshu* material gives the Qur'an's audience a Jewish counter-narrative built to answer the Christian Gospel's account of Jesus's death and resurrection.[^19] Wisdom of Solomon gives the same audience the older scriptural idiom in which the Gospel's own crucifixion scene had first been cast. The Qur'an's denial in Q 4:157–158 therefore reaches, whether directly or through the mediation of the Passion tradition it was answering, back to the same righteous-sufferer grammar standing behind both the Christian narrative of Jesus's death and the Jewish narrative built to mock and invert it.

## Wisdom 3, 5, and the Motifs Absent from the Toledot Yeshu

Anthony's own comparison supplies a further test of this argument. He scores nine motifs shared by the *Toledot Yeshu*, Q 3:54–55, and Q 4:156–159, and three of the qur'anic motifs take a minus sign under *Toledot Yeshu*: God's cleansing of Jesus from his disbelieving opponents, the exaltation of Jesus's followers over the disbelieving Israelites, and Jesus's role as an eschatological witness against his own community.[^20] Anthony's Jewish source does not account for these three motifs. Wisdom of Solomon does.

Wisdom 3:8 answers the second gap only in a qualified sense — the verse belongs to the poem's shift from the singular righteous man of chapter 2 to the collective "righteous" of chapters 3 through 5, so its promise exalts the righteous as a class rather than naming a teacher's own followers.[^21] The structural correspondence nonetheless holds: in both texts, a hostile plot against a righteous figure ends in the elevation of those aligned with him over those who opposed him, a widening of scope Q 3:55 itself performs when it moves from Jesus's individual purification to the promise that God will place those who follow him above those who disbelieve.

The third gap calls for Wisdom 5:1–8, which completes the confrontation staged in chapter 2. The wicked, who had condemned the righteous man to a shameful death, now stand before him at the reckoning.

<BibleVerse reference="Wisdom of Solomon 5:1-5" eyebrow="Apocrypha">
<p>"Then the righteous will stand with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed them... When they see him, they will shudder with terrible dread, and they will be amazed at his unexpected salvation. They will speak to one another in repentance, and in anguish of spirit they will groan, and say, 'This is the one whom we once held in derision and made a byword of reproach — fools that we were! We thought that his life was madness and that his end was without honor. Why has he been numbered among the sons of God? And why is his lot among the saints?'"</p>
</BibleVerse>

Q 4:159 states that "not one of the People of Scripture will fail to believe in him before his death, and on the Day of Resurrection he shall bear witness against them," and Q 5:117 has Jesus describe his own role among his people in the same terms — a witness present while he remained among them and absent once God took him. Wisdom 5 stages this same reversal: the people who condemned the righteous man are made to stand before him, recognize him, and confess in his presence that their earlier judgment was wrong. The *Toledot Yeshu*, built to prove the sages' judgment of Jesus correct, supplies no such scene — its disciples claim only that God rescued Jesus by raising him to heaven, and the sages who condemned him are never made to recognize their error.[^22] Wisdom of Solomon supplies it because vindication before one's accusers, not merely rescue from them, is the outcome its argument has been building toward since chapter 2.

## The Wisdom of Solomon in the Palestinian-Syriac Scriptural Contact Zone

A comparison of this kind depends on Wisdom of Solomon having a plausible route into the Qur'an's late antique environment. The book's transmission history supplies one.

Wisdom of Solomon began as a Greek Jewish composition from Hellenistic Alexandria, written in a monotheistic, Exodus-centered idiom for a Greek-reading Jewish community for whom the Septuagint already functioned as scripture.[^23] Its later transmission moved that idiom into the Christian biblical world. J. A. Emerton's study of the Peshitta Wisdom remains the standard account of that Syriac textual history.[^24] A second, later channel is Paul of Tella's Syro-Hexaplar translation, datable by its subscriptions to 613–617 CE, rendering Wisdom from Greek into Syriac within a generation of the Qur'an's own proclamation — evidence of active Syriac Christian scholarly investment in the book at exactly the relevant horizon.[^25]

<ContextNote title="Palestinian Manuscript Evidence">
Reinhart Ceulemans and Gertjan Verhasselt have published Greek fragments of Wisdom 10 recovered from Khirbet Mird, the monastery of Kastellion in the Judean Desert, alongside other biblical fragments from the same site including portions of Mark, John, Matthew, and Acts.[^26] The find places a Septuagint copy of Wisdom inside a Palestinian monastic library of the kind that also transmitted Gospel texts. A separate Semitic Palestinian witness survives in Christian Palestinian Aramaic, attested in fragments of Wisdom 9–10 preserved in a Cairo Geniza palimpsest.[^27]
</ContextNote>

This Palestinian-Syriac contact zone already carries weight elsewhere in the study of the Qur'an's Jesus and Mary traditions. Stephen Shoemaker has argued that the nativity scene in Q 19, with Mary sent to a remote place, resting beneath a palm, and given water from a spring, intersects with Palestinian local tradition connected to the Kathisma church between Jerusalem and Bethlehem, where late antique Christian memory had already linked Mary's rest, a spring, and a palm tree at a single site.[^28] Nicolai Sinai's survey of the debate over Dye, Tesei, and Shoemaker's work identifies exactly this kind of Palestinian Christian material as central to reconstructing the Qur'an's rhetorical environment.[^29] Anthony's *Toledot Yeshu* argument occupies the Jewish side of the same contact zone, proposing that the Qur'an's account of Jesus's final days answers a narrative that had likely circulated orally among the Jews of the Hijaz by the early seventh century.[^30]

The Kathisma church supplies the material anchor for that intersection: built in the mid-fifth century on the Jerusalem–Bethlehem road and enlarged with an octagonal plan in the sixth, it commemorated the tradition that Mary rested there during her journey to Bethlehem, and Byzantine pilgrim accounts and liturgical calendars associate the same location with a spring and, eventually, a palm tree said to have bent down to offer her its fruit.[^31] Kathisma's location matters beyond its relevance to Shoemaker's thesis: the shrine sat on the same Jerusalem–Bethlehem–Judean Desert corridor as Khirbet Mird, where the Greek fragments of Wisdom 10 were recovered, less than a day's journey apart. This is not evidence any single community read both texts side by side, but two separate lines of access converge on the same ground — Shoemaker's reconstruction places the qur'anic milieu within reach of Palestinian Christian sacred geography, and the evidence assembled here places it within reach of Palestinian Christian scriptural transmission.

## Conclusion

Wisdom of Solomon 2–5 satisfies, in a single continuous unit, all five criteria drawn from the Qur'an's own account of Jesus's end. The wicked plot against the righteous man. They test whether his claim to be God's son is genuine by condemning him to insult, torture, and a shameful death. They believe, once he is gone, that they have destroyed him. The text answers them from outside their own perspective, placing the righteous man's soul in God's hand and describing his removal as protection rather than defeat. Q 3:54–55, Q 4:157–159, and Q 5:117 move through the same sequence: hostile plotting, a claim of killing and crucifixion, the exposure of that claim as conjecture rather than knowledge, and God's own taking and raising of Jesus.

The *Toledot Yeshu* supplies a hostile narrative built to answer the Christian Gospel directly, scene by scene, and it is this narrative, or something like it, that the Qur'an's opponents in Q 4:157 appear to be citing. Wisdom of Solomon supplies the older interpretive grammar that had already shaped how a scene of that kind could be told and understood, since it is Wisdom 2's mockery of a righteous son of God that stands behind Matthew's own account of the taunting at the cross. What the Qur'an adds to this inherited grammar is its own account of what Jesus was in the first place. Wisdom lets its righteous man call God his father and claim sonship as his own boast; the Qur'an gives Jesus none of that language, having the wicked in the text say only that they killed the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, the messenger of God. The wicked in Wisdom are wrong to think a righteous son of God can be destroyed by a shameful death. The Qur'an's opponents are wrong for the same reason and for a further one — even if a death had occurred, it would have marked the end of a servant's mission rather than the defeat of a divine son.

<VerdictBox title="Final Verdict">
Wisdom of Solomon and the Toledot Yeshu are not competing explanations for Q 4:157. They are two layers of the same tradition. The Toledot Yeshu gives the Qur'an's audience a scene-by-scene Jewish rebuttal of the Christian Passion. Wisdom of Solomon gives that same audience the older scriptural grammar — a righteous son of God, tested by a hostile crowd, condemned to a shameful death the crowd mistakes for victory, and secretly preserved in God's own hand — in which both the Gospel's crucifixion scene and its Jewish inversion were already cast. The Qur'an's denial in Q 4:157–158 answers a claim with a history reaching past the Gospel Passion narrative itself, into the wisdom literature that gave Second Temple Judaism its language for how a story like this was supposed to end.
</VerdictBox>
