<ClaimBox title="Abstract">Early Arabic sources explain ṣabaʾa as leaving one religion for another, and describe a ṣābiʾ as someone who has made that transition. Al-Ṭabarī, Abū ʿUbayd, and the lexicographical tradition preserve this definition, while hadith and sīrah reports show the term being applied to Muḥammad and to those who embraced Islam. This study examines the Qur’anic al-Ṣābiʾūn in 2:62, 5:69, and 22:17 in light of that early usage. The Qur’anic term may therefore designate religious converts—those who have left one religious affiliation for another—rather than the proper name of a distinct sect. The existence of later communities known as Sabians does not, by itself, establish that this was the Qur’anic meaning.</ClaimBox>

## The Problem with Translating al-Ṣābiʾūn as “the Sabians”

The Qur’an uses the plural form **<Arabic>الصَّابِئُونَ</Arabic> / <Arabic>الصَّابِئِينَ</Arabic>** three times:

1. Qur’an 2:62: **<Arabic>وَالصَّابِئِينَ</Arabic>**
2. Qur’an 5:69: **<Arabic>وَالصَّابِئُونَ</Arabic>**
3. Qur’an 22:17: **<Arabic>وَالصَّابِئِينَ</Arabic>**

Most English translations render this word as **“Sabians,” “Sabaeans,”** or **“Sabeans.”** These spellings differ, but they do not represent different interpretations. Each is essentially a transliteration of the same Arabic word, not a translation of its lexical meaning.

Rashad Khalifa takes a notably different approach. In his *Authorized English Translation* of the Qur’an, he consistently renders the word as **“the converts”** in all three passages. That is a significant translation choice because it translates the term rather than simply reproducing its conventional English label. The comparison below reproduces each translator’s complete rendering of each verse, with only the expression used for *al-ṣābiʾūn* bolded.

### How does Khalifa’s translation stand out?

Among the standard, widely circulated English translations compared here, Khalifa is an exception and one of the few translators to render the term semantically and consistently as **“converts”** in all three passages. *The Clear Quran* by Mustafa Khattab retains **“Sabians”** in each verse, as do the other mainstream translations listed above. 

“Sabians” presents the term as the name of a community; “converts” presents it as a description of people who have changed religious affiliation. The former may be correct if the Qur’an is referring to a historically identifiable sect, but this doesn't seem to be the case. Modern translations effectively leave the Arabic word unexplained and allow the reader to assume that the Qur’an is naming a specific religious group.

The lexical evidence must therefore be considered before the traditional English label is accepted as the meaning. The root **<Arabic>ص ب أ</Arabic>** (*ṣ-b-ʾ*) is used for departure, emergence, and, in religious usage, leaving one religion for another. Early Arabic sources explicitly apply the word to people who converted to Islam. In the early Islamic narrative record, it is a label used by the former religious community for those who have abandoned its inherited religion.

The question isn't just “Who were the Sabians?” First: **What did the word *ṣābiʾ* mean in Arabic, and what does that meaning contribute to the interpretation of the Qur’an?**

A word can have an ordinary descriptive meaning and later become the name of a particular group. A community can also be given a label because it fits that description. The existence of later communities called “Sabians” does not, by itself, prove that the Qur’an used the word as the proper name of one particular community.

The argument developed here is that the Qur’anic **al-ṣābiʾūn** should be understood as **people who have departed from one religious affiliation and entered another**. “Converts” is a concise English rendering, also “religious defectors” or “those who have changed religious allegiance.”

  translation={"Surely, those who believe, those who are Jewish, the Christians, and the converts; anyone who (1) believes in GOD, and (2) believes in the Last Day, and (3) leads a righteous life, will receive their recompense from their Lord. They have nothing to fear, nor will they grieve."}

The verse identifies several religious categories and then gives a condition: **<Arabic>مَنْ آمَنَ بِاللَّهِ وَالْيَوْمِ الْآخِرِ وَعَمِلَ صَالِحًا</Arabic>**—whoever believes in God and the Last Day and acts righteously. On the proposed reading, *al-ṣābiʾīn* identifies people by their religious transition: they have left the religious affiliation in which they were previously situated and entered another.

The rendering “those who have left one religious affiliation for another” makes the semantic structure visible. “Converts” is shorter, but it should not be mistaken for a word that necessarily means “converts to Islam.” The Arabic term is directional only in the sense that it describes a departure from a former religion and entry into another. It does not identify the destination in isolation.

  translation={"Those who believe, those who are Jewish, the converts, the Christians, the Zoroastrians, and the idol worshipers, GOD is the One who will judge among them on the Day of Resurrection. GOD witnesses all things."}
  highlightArabic={["وَالصَّابِئِينَ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["the converts"]}

This verse is especially important because it names six categories. The list includes (1) believers, (2) Jews, (3) the *ṣābiʾūn*, (4) Christians, (5) Magians, and (6) polytheists. The proposed reading treats *ṣābiʾūn* as a category defined by religious departure rather than as the proper name of a single sect.

A descriptive category can function as a group designation. “Those who have left one religion for another” can identify people whose defining feature is a break with a previous religious affiliation, even when the verse does not specify the particular religion they left or the one they entered. The term can therefore stand beside more familiar communal labels without itself being the name of a separate denomination.

The three verses do not individually explain the history of the people being described. The strongest basis for the proposed reading comes from the lexical definition and from early Arabic usage, considered alongside the way the term appears in the Qur’anic lists.[^1]

## The Root Ṣ-B-ʾ: Leaving One Religion for Another

The essential lexical point is that **<Arabic>صَبَأَ</Arabic> (*ṣabaʾa*)** is not just a name for membership in a sect (this is a group that arose after the Quran claiming to be the aforementioned "Sabians" mentioned in the verses). In religious usage, it means to leave the religion one previously held and enter another. The active participle **<Arabic>صَابِئ</Arabic> (*ṣābiʾ*)** describes the person who has made that transition; its plural is **<Arabic>الصَّابِئُونَ</Arabic> / <Arabic>الصَّابِئِينَ</Arabic>**.

A particularly explicit definition appears in al-Ṭabarī’s commentary on Qur’an 2:62:

This is the central passage for the argument. Al-Ṭabarī isn't just reporting that one late sect was called "Sabian." He first explains the word itself. His definition states **every person who leaves one religion for another** can be called a *ṣābiʾ*.

From the perspective of the religion left behind, the person has defected or departed. From the perspective of the new religion, that person has converted. The word’s emphasis is on the rupture with the former affiliation, rather than on the name of the destination religion.

Al-Ṭabarī then immediately records disagreement over which groups the Qur’anic name applies to. This distinction between the word’s lexical meaning and the identity of the people intended by the verse is crucial. The lexical definition is broad; the reported identifications are diverse.[^2]

This passage directly connects the lexical definition to the religious vocabulary of the Prophet’s lifetime. It establishes two related points:

1. The ordinary religious meaning of *ṣabaʾa* was to leave one religion and enter another.
2. The polytheists used this language for people who embraced Islam in the Prophet’s time.

Abū ʿUbayd does not treat the name as semantically opaque. He explains the designation of the Sabians through the same act of religious departure. They left the religions of the Jews and Christians and entered a third religion. Even where the word refers to a particular community, its naming is explained through the meaning “those who have left one religion for another.”[^3]

## Quraysh’s Use of *Ṣābiʾ* for Converts: The Hadith Evidence

The lexicographical definitions become much more concrete when read alongside the hadith and sīrah narratives. In these reports, *ṣābiʾ* is not used as a precise theological description of the Mandaeans or Harranians. It is a social label applied to people who have abandoned the religion they inherited and followed Muḥammad’s message.

The reports show the word in several grammatical forms:

- **<Arabic>صَابِئ</Arabic> (*ṣābiʾ*)** — one who has departed from a religion.
- **<Arabic>صَبَأَ</Arabic> (*ṣabaʾa*) / <Arabic>صَبَا</Arabic> (*ṣabā*)** — he departed from his former religion.
- **<Arabic>صَبَوْتَ</Arabic> (*ṣabawta*) / <Arabic>أَصَبَوْتَ</Arabic> (*aṣabawta*)** — have you departed from your former religion?
- **<Arabic>صَبَأْنَا</Arabic> (*ṣabaʾnā*)** — we have departed from our former religion.
- **<Arabic>الصُّبَاة</Arabic> (*al-ṣubāt*)** — a plural designation for those who have departed from the inherited religion.

The narratives provide a living context for the lexical definition. It consistently demonstrates that the term marks the change from one religious allegiance to another.

Each report below is given in full. The label itself is marked in gold; the reply, or the wording the report sets against the label, is marked in green.

The account appears in [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4372](https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:4372) and [Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 1764a](https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim:1764a).

This exchange is a particularly clear illustration of the word’s pragmatic force. The questioner does not ask whether Thumāma has joined the Mandaeans or another named religious sect. He asks whether Thumāma has defected from his previous religious allegiance. Thumāma responds by naming his new affiliation: Islam with Muḥammad.

### 5. Banū Jadhīma: “Ṣabaʾnā, ṣabaʾnā”

The report concerning Banū Jadhīma is among the strongest examples because the converts themselves use the expression.

When Khālid b. al-Walīd invites the tribe to Islam, the report says:

<HadithBlock
  label={"Banū Jadhīma before Khālid b. al-Walīd"}

This is preserved in [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4339](https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:4339) and a parallel report in Bukhārī 7189.

The report explicitly contrasts **<Arabic>أَسْلَمْنَا</Arabic>** (*aslamnā*, “we have embraced Islam”) with **<Arabic>صَبَأْنَا</Arabic>** (*ṣabaʾnā*). The latter is the expression the tribe uses to communicate that it has left its former religious position and entered a new one.

This is not a case in which an opponent applies the label as an insult. The converts themselves employ the verb to describe the transition. The report therefore demonstrates that the word could communicate the act of changing religious allegiance, even when the speakers did not know the standard Islamic formula.

### 7. The collective form al-Ṣubāt

The term is not confined to individuals. In [Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3950](https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3950), Abū Jahl confronts Saʿd b. Muʿādh and says:

<HadithBlock
  label={"Abū Jahl to Saʿd b. Muʿādh"}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3950"}
  url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3950"}
  arabic={"وَقَدْ أَوَيْتُمُ الصُّبَاةَ"}
  translation={"You have given shelter to the ṣubāt."}
  highlightArabic={["الصُّبَاةَ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["the ṣubāt"]}
/>

Here *al-ṣubāt* is a collective designation for the people who have left their former religion and joined Muḥammad. The plural usage is also preserved in other reports concerning the Muslims as a group.

Ibn Durayd records that Quraysh used this plural for the Prophet’s companions in the early period of Islam. He writes that the hamzated *ṣābiʾ* is the person who has departed from one thing to another, and that Quraysh called the Prophet’s companions *al-ṣubāt*.[^8]

## Direct Reports Calling Muḥammad al-Ṣābiʾ

The term is also applied directly to Muḥammad. These reports are important because they show that the word was used by his opponents as a description of his departure from Quraysh’s religion.

### ʿUmar’s description of Muḥammad

In Ibn Isḥāq’s account of ʿUmar’s conversion, ʿUmar tells Nuʿaym b. ʿAbd Allāh that he is looking for:

## The Hamza, the Unhamzated Form, and the Meaning of “Departure”

The relevant word is normally written **<Arabic>صَابِئ</Arabic>** with hamza. The root is **<Arabic>ص ب أ</Arabic>**. The verbal form *ṣabaʾa* is explained as leaving one religion and entering another.

The lexicographical record also distinguishes the hamzated verb **<Arabic>صَبَأَ</Arabic>** from the unhamzated verb **<Arabic>صَبَا يَصْبُو</Arabic>**, which can mean to incline, lean, or be drawn toward something. Some Qur’anic reading traditions omit the hamza in the word, and the classical grammarians discuss whether this represents a lightened hamza or a relation to the unhamzated root.

Al-Rāzī, commenting on 2:62, records the hamzated and unhamzated readings. He notes that the majority reading has the hamza and says that the hamzated interpretation is preferable because it accords more closely with the explanation given by scholars in which the *ṣābiʾ* is the person who leaves one religion for another.

The explicit hamzated form is associated in the lexicons with religious departure. Where the hamza is omitted, the classical sources discuss both a possible relation to “inclining” and the possibility that the hamza has simply been dropped in pronunciation.

The semantic relation between departure and emergence is also reflected in examples such as a star rising from its place of rising or a tooth emerging. Al-Ṭabarī mentions the expression **<Arabic>صَبَأَتِ النُّجُومُ</Arabic>** for the stars appearing or rising. The underlying image is one of coming out or emerging. In the religious expression, the person has come out of the religion previously held and entered another.

The forms without hamza in the hadith material—such as **<Arabic>صَبَا عُمَرُ</Arabic>**, **<Arabic>صَبَوْتَ</Arabic>**, and **<Arabic>الصُّبَاة</Arabic>**—are explained by lexicographers as part of the same historical usage. Abū Mūsā al-Madīnī and Ibn al-Athīr note that Quraysh did not pronounce the hamza in these expressions, which accounts for the forms without it.

## Does the Qur’an Name a Sect, or Describe Religious Converts?

The evidence so far establishes that *ṣābiʾ* had a general Arabic meaning connected with departure from one religion to another. The next question is how that meaning should be applied to the Qur’anic occurrences.

There are two possibilities that must be distinguished.

### 1. The word as a descriptive category

On the first reading, **al-ṣābiʾūn** means “*those who have left one religion for another.*” It describes an action taken by individuals, not by the name of a particular sect. In this sense, “converts” or “religious defectors” is a translation of the Arabic meaning.

This reading is supported by:

- al-Ṭabarī’s explicit general definition of a *ṣābiʾ*;
- Abū ʿUbayd’s definition and his explanation of Quraysh’s usage for converts to Islam;
- al-Zajjāj’s direct gloss on the Qur’anic word;
- al-Qurṭubī’s explicit statement that Arabs said *ṣabaʾa* of someone who embraced Islam;
- Bukhārī’s own gloss after the waterskins report;
- the reports in which Muḥammad and his companions are called *ṣābiʾ / ṣubāt*;
- the use of *ṣabaʾnā* by Banū Jadhīma to express their religious transition.

The Qur’anic plural can therefore be read as referring to a category of people who have broken with a previous religious affiliation (such as those who broke off/converted away from the ancestral Qurayshi religion to join Muhammad's community). Such a category need not be identical with the Mandaeans or Harranians, and it need not specify one common destination religion.

### 2. The word as the name of an identifiable religious community

On the second reading, the Qur’an refers to a particular community known as the Sabians. Classical exegetes often attempted to identify such a group. Their descriptions vary considerably and are rather inconsistent:

- Mujāhid is reported as saying that the Sabians were neither Jews nor Christians and had no established religion; in another version, they were between the Magians and Jews.
- Qatāda is reported as describing them as people who worshipped angels, prayed toward the qiblah, and read the Psalms.
- Abū al-ʿĀliyah identifies them as a group among the People of the Book who read the Psalms.
- al-Suddī is reported as describing them as a group among the People of the Book.
- Ibn Zayd describes people in the region of Mosul who said “There is no god but God” but, in his account, had no book or prophet.
- Other reports associate them with particular regions or with mixtures of religious practices.

Al-Ṭabarī records these views after giving his lexical definition. He first explains the general meaning of the word, then records disagreement over which people are intended by the Qur’anic designation.

The diversity of these identifications may reflect the fact that later exegetes were trying to identify a group from a word whose basic meaning described religious departure.

### The Mandaeans and the name “Sabians”

The *Encyclopaedia Iranica* entry on the Mandaeans notes that the Muslims gave them the name **ṣābeʾun**, the “Sabians,” a designation known from the Qur’an and early Arabic literature. It also notes a proposed alternative etymology: the Mandaic/Aramaic root *ṣeba*, meaning to immerse, baptize, or wash. On that explanation, the designation may have been associated with the Mandaeans’ baptismal practices.

This is a significant competing explanation, but it does not settle the Qur’anic question. It describes the later use of the label for a historical community. If Muslims applied the name “Sabians” to the Mandaeans, that application could be related to the community’s perceived religious difference, its baptismal practices, the Qur’anic category, or a combination of factors. It cannot simply be assumed that the Qur’an originally used the term as the Mandaeans’ proper name.

Kurt Rudolph’s *Encyclopaedia Iranica* entry also notes that the Mandaeans’ own traditional self-designations were different, including “elect of righteousness” and “guardians / possessors” of rites and knowledge. The name “Mandaean” itself relates to *manda*, “knowledge” or “gnosis.”[^15]

### The Ḥarrānian Sabians

A separate group known in Islamic sources as the Sabians of Ḥarrān was associated with astral or planetary worship and came to prominence in later Islamic religious and legal discussions. This is another reason to avoid treating “Sabians” as if it had one stable referent across every period. The label was applied in different settings to different communities, and the legal need to classify non-Muslim groups could encourage the adoption or extension of an existing designation.

### A modern academic argument distinguishing the terms

Cağfer Karadaş’s article, “Ṣābī Matter: The Issue of Whether the Concept ṣābī in the Qur’an Signifies Ṣābiʾī/Ṣābiʾa” (*İlahiyat Studies* 1/1 [2010], pp. 65–90), directly addresses this issue. Its abstract argues that the Qur’anic *ṣābī* is an Arabic adjective used for religious conversion and for people who left the prevalent belief, and that this is not the same as the later communal designation *Ṣābiʾa* used for Mandaeans and Ḥarrānians.

This is important because it articulates the distinction that the early Arabic lexical evidence makes possible, which is that the Qur’anic term can be understood descriptively, while “Sabian” can become a later name for communities to whom the label was applied.[^16]

The argument is cumulative. The Qur’anic word belongs to a lexical family whose religious sense is “to leave one religion for another.” Early Arabic authorities apply this word to converts. The hadith and sīrah evidence shows that the label was used for Muḥammad and for people who entered his religious community. Early tafsir and lexicography explicitly connect the Qur’anic plural with the same semantic idea.

The conventional translation “Sabians” is therefore not accurate as a transliteration. It invites a particular historical identification that the Arabic word does not necessarily require. A translation that preserves the lexical meaning—“converts,” “religious defectors,” or “those who have left one religion for another”—understands this term in its lexical and historical context.

## Conclusion: Recovering the Descriptive Meaning of al-Ṣābiʾūn

The Qur’anic **al-ṣābiʾūn / al-ṣābiʾīn** should not be assumed to be the proper name of one particular religious sect simply because “Sabians” has become the conventional English translation. The earliest and most explicit Arabic explanations preserve a general religious meaning; a *ṣābiʾ* is one who leaves the religion previously held and enters another.

Al-Ṭabarī states that every person who leaves one religion for another is called a *ṣābiʾ* by the Arabs. Abū ʿUbayd explains that polytheists used the word for anyone who embraced Islam in the Prophet’s time. Bukhārī preserves the same gloss. The accounts of ʿUmar, Abū Dharr, Thumāma, Banū Jadhīma, and other converts demonstrate the terminology in practice. Quraysh called Muḥammad *al-ṣābiʾ* because he had departed from their religion; they called his followers *al-ṣubāt* because they had done the same.

The meaning is therefore not *“a member of the Mandaean religion”* or “*a member of the Harranian religion.*” Those are later historical applications or identifications.

The strongest formulation of the thesis is the following:

<VerdictBox title="Thesis">

Al-ṣābiʾūn in the Qur’an should be read as “the converts” or “those who have departed their inherited faith.” The word is a descriptive label rooted in religious departure, not the proper name of a distinct sect. The early Arabic lexical record, Quraysh’s designation of Muḥammad and his followers, and the conversion narratives preserved in the literature provide a coherent basis for this reading.

</VerdictBox>

### D. Explanatory glosses in hadith and lexicographical works

- Bukhārī, after 344: **<Arabic>صَبَأَ: خَرَجَ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى غَيْرِهِ</Arabic>**.
- Abū ʿUbayd, *Gharīb al-ḥadīth* 1:305–306: the *ṣābiʾ* is one who has left one religion for another; Quraysh said of a new Muslim **<Arabic>قَدْ صَبَأَ فُلَانٌ</Arabic>**.
- Al-Ḥumaydī, *Tafsīr gharīb mā fī al-ṣaḥīḥayn* 1:200: *ṣabaʾa* means leaving one religion for another, and the polytheists applied it to one who believed in Muḥammad.
- Ibn al-Athīr, *Jāmiʿ al-uṣūl* 2:284: **<Arabic>يُقَالُ صَبَأَ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى دِينٍ إِذَا خَرَجَ مِنْ هَذَا إِلَى هَذَا</Arabic>**.
- Ibn al-Athīr, *Jāmiʿ al-uṣūl* 8:190: **<Arabic>الصُّبَاةُ جَمْعُ صَابِئٍ، وَهُوَ الَّذِي فَارَقَ دِينَهُ إِلَى غَيْرِهِ</Arabic>**.
- *Fatḥ al-ghaffār* 3:1727, on Banū Jadhīma: the phrase **<Arabic>صَبَأْنَا</Arabic>** is explained in connection with entering a new religion, and the people of the Jāhiliyyah are said to have called one who embraced Islam a *ṣābiʾ*.

## Further Notes on Meaning and Method

1. **“Convert” is accurate in substance, but “religious defector” preserves the older perspective.** The basic definition is **<Arabic>خَرَجَ مِنْ دِينٍ إِلَى دِينٍ</Arabic>**—he left one religion and entered another. The emphasis is on leaving. From the perspective of the former community, the convert is a renegade; from the perspective of the new community, he is a convert.
2. **The term does not specify the destination.** In the hadith examples, the narratives tell us that the destination is Islam. The word by itself simply identifies a person who has left a prior religious affiliation for another.
3. **The hamzated and unhamzated forms must be distinguished.** The hamzated root **<Arabic>ص ب أ</Arabic>** carries the religious departure meaning. The related unhamzated root **<Arabic>ص ب و</Arabic>** can mean inclination or attraction. Some readings and dialectal forms omit the hamza; classical lexicographers discuss the relationship.
4. **The word’s later use as a community name does not erase its descriptive meaning.** A label meaning “those who leave one religion for another” can be applied to a particular group and eventually become its communal name. That later proper-name usage should not be projected automatically onto the Qur’anic occurrences.
5. **The early sources do not all identify the Qur’anic referent in the same way.** Mujāhid, Qatāda, al-Suddī, Ibn Zayd, and others are represented with different descriptions. That diversity is a real limitation on any claim that the Qur’anic referent is settled beyond dispute.
6. **The corpus total is not a count of independent witnesses.** It includes repeated copies, quotations, glosses, and false positives. Its value is to map the breadth of the terminology and identify source clusters for closer study.
