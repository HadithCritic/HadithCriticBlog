## The Question

How long did the Prophet stay in Mecca after receiving revelation?

This sounds like a basic historical question. It should not be difficult. The common outline says the Prophet received revelation at around forty, preached in Mecca, migrated to Medina, and then lived in Medina for about ten years. On that outline, the Meccan period after revelation is usually counted as roughly thirteen years.

But when we turn to the hadith corpus, the answer is not stable. Reports in major accepted collections give different numbers: ten years, thirteen years, and fifteen years. Some of these reports are attached to the same Companion, Ibn ʿAbbās. That creates a real historical problem. If accepted reports disagree on a basic chronological question, then “it is in Bukhārī” or “it is in Muslim” cannot by itself settle the matter.

<ClaimBox>
The problem is not that one weak report disagrees with a strong report. The problem is that accepted hadith collections preserve multiple incompatible durations for the Prophet’s Meccan period after revelation.
</ClaimBox>

This article does not argue that we know nothing about the Prophet’s Meccan period. It argues something more precise. The hadith transmission of this chronological detail is unstable. The reports preserve competing calculations, and the isnād map shows that the disagreement is not a simple matter of one isolated error.

The reports are not merely contradictory. They preserve competing chronological systems.

## The Three Durations

The basic conflict is simple.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">أَقَامَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ بِمَكَّةَ ثَلَاثَ عَشْرَةَ سَنَةً، وَتُوُفِّيَ وَهُوَ ابْنُ ثَلَاثٍ وَسِتِّينَ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The Messenger of God stayed in Mecca for thirteen years, and he died when he was sixty-three.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

That is the familiar version. But it is not the only version.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">مَكَثَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ بِمَكَّةَ عَشْرَ سِنِينَ يُنْزَلُ عَلَيْهِ الْقُرْآنُ، وَبِالْمَدِينَةِ عَشْرًا</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The Prophet stayed in Mecca for ten years while the Qur’an was being revealed to him, and in Medina for ten years.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

Another report gives fifteen years.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">أَقَامَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ بِمَكَّةَ خَمْسَ عَشْرَةَ سَنَةً، يَسْمَعُ الصَّوْتَ وَيَرَى الضَّوْءَ سَبْعَ سِنِينَ، وَلَا يَرَى شَيْئًا، وَثَمَانِيَ سِنِينَ يُوحَى إِلَيْهِ، وَأَقَامَ بِالْمَدِينَةِ عَشْرَ سِنِينَ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The Messenger of God stayed in Mecca for fifteen years. For seven years he heard the voice and saw the light but saw no visible form, and for eight years revelation was sent to him. He stayed in Medina for ten years.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

These are not tiny variations. Ten, thirteen, and fifteen years are different chronologies. They cannot all be the same straightforward count unless someone changes what is being counted.

The issue is not whether one can invent harmonizations. One can always say one report counts public preaching, another counts Qur’anic revelation, another counts visionary preparation, and another rounds the numbers. The issue is that the reports themselves do not present one clean chronology. The harmonization is imposed after the fact.

## Seeing the Problem at a Glance

The disagreement is easier to understand when the reports are set beside one another. The issue is not merely that one transmitter said ten and another said thirteen. The issue is that the corpus preserves different chronological models for the Prophet’s life.

## Rubin’s Point: These Are Chronological Models, Not Just Dates

Uri Rubin’s discussion is important because it helps us describe the problem with more precision. The reports are not simply scattered memories of a date. They preserve models. The simplest model is **10–10**: ten years in Mecca and ten years in Medina. This creates a neat symmetry between the two periods of Qur’anic revelation. It also fits a broader typological scheme in which revelation begins at forty and the Prophet dies at sixty.

That means the ten-year Meccan report should not be treated as a simple historical memory in isolation. It belongs to a schematic way of arranging the Prophet’s career. The two halves of his prophetic life are made equal: ten years of revelation in Mecca, then ten years in Medina. The problem is that this model collides with the more common age calculation of revelation beginning at forty and death at sixty-three.

Once the death age becomes sixty-three, the old symmetry has to be repaired. One repair is the **13–10 model**: thirteen years in Mecca, ten in Medina, death at sixty-three. This is the model that best fits the standard sīra outline. But the important point is that it looks like a correction of the earlier symmetry, not simply a neutral datum floating by itself. Three years are added to the Meccan period in order to preserve the age boundary of sixty-three.

Rubin also notes that those extra three years could be filled with sīra material about non-Qur’anic revelations before Gabriel’s normal Qur’anic revelation. In some reports, between the ages of forty and forty-three, the Prophet receives a preliminary kind of revelation through Michael or Isrāfīl. Then, at forty-three, Gabriel begins bringing the proper Qur’anic revelation for ten years in Mecca and ten years in Medina. This preserves the 10–10 model for Qur’anic revelation while still allowing the Prophet to die at sixty-three.

That is exactly the kind of thing a historian should notice. The traditions are not only reporting durations. They are solving chronological problems by inserting stages: pre-Qur’anic revelation, secret preaching, public preaching, Qur’anic revelation, and Medinan revelation. The chronology is being built out of interpretive material.

This also explains the fifteen-year reports. The fifteen-year Meccan model is not just a bigger number. It absorbs additional sīra motifs. In some versions, five years are assigned to hidden preaching and ten years to public preaching. In another version, seven years are assigned to hearing voices and seeing light, while eight years are assigned to revelation. The number fifteen therefore functions as an expanded Meccan chronology that gives more room to the Prophet’s early prophetic preparation.

The point is not that every version is equally plausible. The point is that the corpus preserves multiple chronological systems. Ten years creates symmetry. Thirteen years preserves the standard death age of sixty-three. Fifteen years expands the Meccan period by inserting earlier stages of preparation, hidden preaching, or non-Qur’anic revelation. These are not three clean eyewitness memories. They are competing ways of organizing the Prophet’s life.

## Poetry and the “Some Ten Years” Problem

Rubin also draws attention to the role of poetry. Ibn Isḥāq preserves verses attributed to the Medinan poet Abū Qays Ṣirma b. Abī Anas. In those verses, the Prophet is said to have spent “some ten years” among Quraysh, preaching and seeking support before finding refuge among the Anṣār. This poetic line later enters the chronological discussion.

That matters because **bidʿa ʿashrata hijjatan** does not mean a precise ten years. It means “some ten years,” or “a number over ten.” In later transmission, this poetic expression could be read in more than one way. Some transmitters used it to support a Meccan period longer than ten years. In one Ibn ʿAbbās-linked tradition, the wording becomes fifteen years rather than “some ten years.”

This is important because it shows how chronological reports absorbed material from outside strict historical calculation. A poetic phrase about the Prophet’s long and unsuccessful preaching among Quraysh was pulled into hadith chronology and then used to support a larger Meccan period. The date was not simply remembered. It was argued from poetry, sīra motifs, and inherited models.

That makes the conflict more serious. The reports are not just disagreeing over arithmetic. They are preserving different attempts to make sense of the Prophet’s early career.

## The Internal Dispute

The most important evidence is not merely that different reports give different numbers. It is that some reports already know about the disagreement.

In the Saʿīd b. Jubayr route, a man tells Ibn ʿAbbās that revelation lasted ten years in Mecca and ten years in Medina. Ibn ʿAbbās asks where he heard this. The man says he heard people saying it. Ibn ʿAbbās then rejects the claim and gives a longer Meccan period.

For a lay reader, the diagram shows two things. First, the reports are not one chain with one obvious mistake. They are several route families. Second, the same Companion authority, Ibn ʿAbbās, appears behind conflicting numbers. That is the core historical problem.

If Ibn ʿAbbās really gave one fixed chronological answer, then later transmission has not preserved it cleanly. Either different transmitters counted different things and later attached those counts to him, or variant chronological traditions were already circulating under his authority. In either case, the hadith record is not giving one stable memory.

## Why Ibn ʿAbbās Is Central

Ibn ʿAbbās is the main Companion authority in the cluster. That is itself interesting. He was young during the Prophet’s lifetime and was not a direct adult witness to the earliest Meccan preaching. So when a report through Ibn ʿAbbās gives a chronology for the Meccan period, it is already a transmitted historical calculation, not his personal adult memory of those years.

This does not make Ibn ʿAbbās useless as a source. It simply means his reports need to be treated as early historical tradition, not eyewitness diary material. The problem is that the same early historical authority is used for multiple answers.

<ContextNote>
The issue is not Ibn ʿAbbās as a person. The issue is the later transmission of chronological claims under his authority. The reports make him support more than one duration.
</ContextNote>

That is exactly the kind of place where isnād-cum-matn analysis matters. A hadith can have a respected Companion name at the top and still preserve a later calculation, harmonization, or exegetical scheme.

## The Thirteen-Year Reports

The thirteen-year reports are the most historically comfortable. They match the common chronology: the Prophet receives revelation around forty, stays in Mecca until the Hijrah, then lives in Medina for about ten years, and dies at sixty-three.

That does not mean every thirteen-year report is automatically original. It means the thirteen-year number fits the standard chronological framework better than the ten- or fifteen-year reports.

The thirteen-year routes appear through several lines, including the ʿAmr b. Dīnār route, the Abū Jamrah route, the Ibn Sīrīn route, the ʿIkrimah route, and the ʿĀʾishah route through Abū Salamah. That gives the number broader support than the other counts. But even here, the existence of competing reports means we should not pretend the corpus is perfectly clean.

## The Ten-Year Reports

The ten-year reports are the hardest if read literally. If the Prophet began receiving revelation at forty and died at sixty-three after ten years in Medina, then a ten-year Meccan revelation period does not fit the common arithmetic. It leaves only twenty years after forty, not twenty-three.

A defender might say the ten-year reports count only the period of Qur’anic revelation after a preparatory phase, or that they count only the public phase, or that they round the number. Those are possible harmonizations. In fact, some reports already do this kind of work by inserting a three-year period involving Michael or Isrāfīl before Gabriel begins the proper Qur’anic revelation.

That matters because it means the ten-year model is not just “bad math.” It became part of a larger harmonizing scheme. The ten years can be protected if the first three years after forty are made into another kind of prophetic preparation.

## The Fifteen-Year Reports

The fifteen-year reports are different because they often include an explanatory structure. One version says the Prophet stayed in Mecca fifteen years: seven years hearing a voice and seeing light without seeing a visible form, then eight years of revelation, then ten years in Medina.

This is not just a number. It is a narrative scheme. It divides the Meccan period into stages and explains early prophetic experience before regular revelation. That may preserve a real attempt to account for earlier visionary phenomena, but it also looks like a developed chronological explanation.

The fifteen-year report should therefore be treated carefully. It may not be simply “wrong.” It may be counting a different kind of pre-revelation or pre-public prophetic experience. But if so, it is not answering the same question as the thirteen-year report. That means the hadith corpus is mixing different counting systems under the same general historical question.

## Why This Matters

This question seems small, but it exposes a bigger problem. The Prophet’s Meccan period is foundational. It is the period of the first revelation, early preaching, persecution, migration, and the formation of the first community. If hadith reports disagree over something as basic as how long the period lasted, then we should be cautious about using hadith as if it gives direct access to a perfectly preserved biography.

The reports are not useless. They are evidence. But they are evidence that must be analyzed, compared, and historically weighed. A report in an accepted collection can preserve a real early tradition and still conflict with another accepted report.

That is why “ṣaḥīḥ” should not be confused with “historically settled.” A ṣaḥīḥ chain may mean the transmitters met the standards of later hadith critics. It does not guarantee that every chronological detail is historically exact.

<ClaimBox title="The Historical Lesson">
When accepted reports disagree on a basic chronological question, the historian cannot simply quote one and ignore the others. The reports have to be mapped, compared, and judged as historical traditions.
</ClaimBox>

## What Is the Most Plausible Duration?

The most plausible duration remains thirteen years. It fits the common chronological structure best: revelation begins around age forty, the Prophet migrates to Medina, remains there about ten years, and dies at sixty-three. That basic arithmetic supports thirteen years in Mecca after revelation.

But the important point is not simply “thirteen wins.” The important point is how the hadith corpus preserves the disagreement. The ten-year and fifteen-year reports show that chronological traditions about the Meccan period were being counted and transmitted in different ways. Some reports may count only Qur’anic revelation. Others may count public preaching. Others may include a preparation phase before normal revelation. Others may round or compress.

So the answer is:

**Historically, thirteen years is the strongest reconstruction. Textually, the hadith corpus preserves conflicting accepted reports.**

That distinction matters.

## Final Verdict

The Prophet most likely remained in Mecca for about thirteen years after the beginning of revelation. That is the count that best fits the standard sīra chronology: revelation around forty, migration before the Medinan decade, and death at sixty-three.

But the hadith record does not preserve this chronology cleanly. It preserves competing chronological systems. The **10–10 model** creates symmetry between Meccan and Medinan revelation. The **13–10 model** preserves the death age of sixty-three and fits the common sīra outline. The **15-year model** expands the Meccan period by absorbing stories of hidden preaching, early visionary experience, or non-Qur’anic revelation before Gabriel’s regular Qur’anic revelation. Poetry also enters the discussion, especially the phrase that the Prophet remained among Quraysh for “some ten years,” which could be used to push the Meccan period beyond a literal ten.

That means the disagreement is not accidental. It is not just one bad transmitter getting a number wrong. The corpus preserves different ways of organizing the Prophet’s early career. Some reports count Qur’anic revelation. Others count public preaching. Others include a preparation phase before normal revelation. Others preserve a schematic symmetry between Mecca and Medina.

So the historical conclusion and the hadith-critical conclusion must be kept separate. Historically, thirteen years remains the strongest reconstruction. Hadith-critically, the accepted corpus preserves more than one chronology, and later transmitters tried to solve the problem by building models around the numbers.

The issue is therefore larger than a date. It shows that even basic sīra chronology was shaped through transmission, interpretation, poetry, symbolic numbering, and harmonization. The reports are evidence, but they are not a single preserved stopwatch for the Prophet’s life.
