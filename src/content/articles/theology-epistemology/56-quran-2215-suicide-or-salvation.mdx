<ClaimBox>
This article is not arguing that the strangulation interpretation was invented by modern translators. It is old and widely transmitted. The argument is narrower: the same classical tradition also preserves the tools needed to read the verse without making God command suicide, and the Quran’s own use of the key words supports that cleaner reading.

## The Verse in One Sentence

A literal word-for-word rendering can sound strange:

<HadithBlock title="A literal rendering of Quran 22:15">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Whoever thinks that God will not help him in this world and the Hereafter, let him extend a means/rope toward the sky, then let him cut, then let him see whether his plan removes what enrages him.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

The dispute begins because several words can be narrowed in more than one direction. If <em>sabab</em> is narrowed to “rope,” <em>sama</em> is narrowed to “ceiling,” and <em>li-yaqtaʿ</em> is supplied with “his breath” or “himself,” the verse becomes a hanging scene. If <em>sabab</em> is read as “means” or “connection,” <em>sama</em> as heaven, and <em>li-yaqtaʿ</em> as severing dependence, the verse becomes a challenge to the person who thinks God will not support him: turn upward to God and cut off the false attachments that feed your rage.

  </div>

## Why the Suicide Reading Creates a Problem

The Quran does not allow an interpretation to be judged in isolation from the rest of the Quran. It asks the reader to notice internal consistency:

<QuranVerse verse="4:82" />

A reading of 22:15 that turns the verse into a literal command to hang oneself has to be measured against verses where the Quran says God does not command indecency and where self-killing is forbidden.

<QuranVerse verse="7:28" />

<QuranVerse verse="4:29" />

This does not mean every violent or sarcastic image in the Quran must be softened. It means we should be careful before turning a rhetorical challenge into a divine instruction. The verse can mock the futility of the doubter’s rage without telling a human being to commit suicide.

## How the Strangulation Reading Entered Tafsir

The strangulation reading is not a modern accident. It appears in classical tafsir and was inherited by many English translators. The strongest versions of that reading gloss <em>sabab</em> as a rope, <em>sama</em> as the ceiling of the house, and “cut” as strangling oneself.

  <div class="hc-fig__item">
    <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Tafsir al-Jalalayn</div>
    <h3>Ceiling and strangulation</h3>
    <p>Al-Jalalayn explains the “sky” as the ceiling of the house, the <em>sabab</em> as a rope tied to that ceiling and to the neck, and “cut” as choking oneself by cutting off connection to the ground.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hc-fig__item">
    <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Ibn Kathir</div>
    <h3>The inherited report</h3>
    <p>Ibn Kathir transmits the familiar explanation through early authorities: the man stretches a rope to the ceiling of his house and strangles himself with it.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="hc-fig__item">
    <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Fayruzabadi</div>
    <h3>The same narrowing</h3>
    <p>The verse is again explained as a man tying a rope to the ceiling and hanging himself because of rage over God’s support or over his own provision.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<ContextNote>
This reading is old, so it should not be dismissed as a translation mistake. The better criticism is that it is a commentary-driven narrowing of the verse. The Arabic words can bear that reading only after several interpretive steps have been supplied.
</ContextNote>

## What the Classical Sources Also Preserve

The strangulation reading became dominant, but the classical sources preserve more than one interpretive tool. Once those tools are collected, the verse does not have to be read as a divine command to self-harm.

### The Pronoun Was Disputed

The common reading assumes that the pronoun in <em>yanṣuruhu</em> means “God will not help Muhammad.” But exegetes also record another reading: the pronoun can return to <em>man</em>, the “whoever” at the beginning of the verse. On that reading, the verse addresses the person who thinks God will not help or provide for him in this world and the Hereafter.

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Razi on 22:15</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar">أَنَّ الْهَاءَ فِي قَوْلِهِ: لَنْ يَنْصُرَهُ اللَّهُ، رَاجِعٌ إِلَى مَنْ فِي أَوَّلِ الْآيَةِ ... فَكَأَنَّهُ قَالَ: مَنْ كَانَ يَظُنُّ أَنْ لَنْ يَرْزُقَهُ اللَّهُ فِي الدُّنْيَا وَالْآخِرَةِ</p>
  <p>“The pronoun in ‘God will not help him’ returns to ‘whoever’ at the start of the verse … as if it said: whoever thinks that God will not provide for him in this world and the Hereafter.”</p>
</div>

Al-Qurtubi records the same option, and al-Tabari preserves the explanation that “support” here means provision, with <em>yanṣuruhu</em> read in the sense of <em>yarzuquhu</em>. That reading changes the emotional scene. The verse is no longer about a man enraged by the Prophet’s victory. It can be read as a person who thinks God will not support him and is told to redirect his dependence upward.

### Sabab Means a Means or Connection

The word <em>sabab</em> can mean a rope, but “rope” is not the only sense and not always the central sense. Al-Qurtubi defines it broadly:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Qurtubi</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar">السَّبَبُ مَا يُتَوَصَّلُ بِهِ إِلَى الشَّيْءِ</p>
  <p>“A <em>sabab</em> is that by which one reaches a thing.”</p>
</div>

That is why “means,” “connection,” or “way” is often the better starting point. A rope is one kind of <em>sabab</em>, but not every <em>sabab</em> is a noose.

### The Quran Uses Sabab as a Way or Means

The Quran’s own usage matters. In Surat Ghafir, Pharaoh tells Haman to build a tower so that he may reach the means to the heavens:

<QuranVerse verse="40:36" />

<QuranVerse verse="40:37" />

Here the phrase <em>asbab al-samawat</em> cannot mean nooses to ceilings. It means ways, means, or routes of ascent toward the heavens. That makes it relevant to 22:15, where <em>sabab</em> and <em>sama</em> appear together.

The Quran also uses the same root with cutting language:

<QuranVerse verse="2:166" />

In 2:166, the <em>asbab</em> are cut off, meaning relationships, ties, or dependencies are severed. That is a stronger Quranic parallel for 22:15 than the idea of cutting off one’s breath. The Quran itself shows that <em>sabab</em> can be something relational that is severed.

### Sama Is Not Automatically a Ceiling

The word in the verse is <em>al-sama</em>. Translating it as “the ceiling of his house” is a tafsir gloss. Commentators justify that gloss by saying that Arabs may call anything above a person <em>sama</em>. But the literal sense remains sky or heaven, and the real-sky reading is preserved in the exegetical tradition.

Al-Qurtubi reports Ibn Zayd’s reading that it means the known sky. Al-Razi lists the real sky as an interpretive option. Ibn Ashur also reports the same broader possibility. So the reading “toward heaven” is not a modern rescue attempt. It is one of the possibilities already visible in the tradition.

### Even the Strangulation Reading Is Rhetorical

Even when classical exegetes adopt the strangulation reading, they do not treat the verse as practical religious instruction. They treat it as a rhetorical challenge, a way of showing that the doubter’s rage cannot overturn God’s decree. Al-Razi states the logic of that kind of interpretation:

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--extract">
  <div class="hc-fig__kicker">Al-Razi</div>
  <p class="hc-fig__ar">كُلُّ مَا كَانَ الْمَذْكُورُ أَبْعَدَ مِنَ الْإِمْكَانِ كَانَ أَوْلَى بِأَنْ يَكُونَ هُوَ الْمُرَادَ</p>
  <p>“The more impossible the mentioned act is, the more fitting it is as the intended figurative sense.”</p>
</div>

That means the worst English rendering is not merely “rope” or “ceiling.” It is the impression that the verse is a literal command to commit suicide. Even within the strangulation reading, the verse is a rhetorical taunt about futility, not a devotional instruction.

## The Reading That Makes the Most Sense

Rashad Khalifa’s translation is unusual because it does not import the ceiling/noose scenario into the verse. He reads the verse through reliance on God:

<HadithBlock title="Rashad Khalifa’s translation of Quran 22:15">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“If anyone thinks that GOD cannot support him in this life and in the Hereafter, let him turn completely to his Creator in heaven, and sever his dependence on anyone else. He will then see that this plan eliminates anything that bothers him.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

This reading does not require pretending the classical strangulation interpretation never existed. It does something more useful. It notices that the key words have broader Quranic meanings and that the verse can be read as guidance for the person who doubts God’s support.

<div class="hc-fig hc-fig--cols">
  <div class="hc-fig__item"><b>1</b><span>He thinks God will not support him.</span></div>
  <div class="hc-fig__item"><b>2</b><span>He is told to extend his means toward heaven.</span></div>
  <div class="hc-fig__item"><b>3</b><span>He cuts the false dependencies beneath him.</span></div>
  <div class="hc-fig__item"><b>4</b><span>He sees whether that plan removes what troubles him.</span></div>
</div>

The reading is not just more comforting. Comfort by itself is not evidence. It is stronger because it makes better use of the Quran’s own language. <em>Sabab</em> is a means or connection. <em>Asbab</em> can be cut off as ties and dependencies. <em>Sama</em> can remain heaven without being reduced to a ceiling. The pronoun can refer to the doubter himself. The whole sentence can function as a challenge to misplaced dependence rather than a command to self-destruction.

## What the Verse Is Saying

The verse is addressing a person who thinks God will not support him. It challenges him to test the logic of his own dependence. If he thinks support will not come from God, let him direct his means upward and cut the false attachments that keep him trapped in rage or despair. Then let him see whether that plan removes what is troubling him.

This reading turns the verse back into Quranic guidance. The person is not told to end his life. He is told to stop imagining that his frustration can defeat God’s decree or that his support must come from somewhere other than God.

<VerdictBox title="Final Reading">
Quran 22:15 is best read as a rhetorical challenge about support, dependence, and misplaced rage. The strangulation interpretation is old, but it relies on narrowing several Arabic words and flattening the verse into a literal hanging scene. The Quran’s own use of <em>sabab</em>, <em>sama</em>, and cutting language supports a reading in which the person turns toward God and severs dependence on anything else.
</VerdictBox>

## A More Coherent Translation

<HadithBlock title="Quran 22:15 — proposed rendering">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Whoever thinks that God will not support him in this world and the Hereafter, let him extend a means toward heaven, then sever it, and let him see whether his plan removes what enrages him.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

A fuller explanatory translation would be:

<HadithBlock title="Quran 22:15 — explanatory rendering">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">
“Whoever thinks that God will not support him in this life and in the Hereafter should turn his means upward to heaven and sever his dependence on anything else. Then let him see whether this plan removes what distresses him.”
</div>
</HadithBlock>

The shorter version stays closer to the Arabic. The fuller version explains the interpretation. What should not be done is to make “ceiling,” “noose,” “breath,” or “strangle himself” sound like words the verse itself required.

## Conclusion

Quran 22:15 has often been read through a hanging scene: rope, ceiling, neck, strangulation. That reading is old, but age does not make every supplied detail part of the verse. The verse says <em>sabab</em>, not noose. It says <em>sama</em>, not ceiling. It says “cut,” without saying “cut off his breath.” It asks whether the person’s <em>kayd</em>, his plan or effort, will remove what enrages him.

The Quran gives us better controls than inherited habit. It uses <em>sabab</em> for means, ways, and connections. It uses cutting of <em>asbab</em> for severed ties. It refuses the idea that God commands indecency or self-destruction. Read inside that larger pattern, 22:15 is not a divine command to suicide. It is a challenge to the person who doubts God’s support: turn upward, cut false reliance, and see whether rage survives when dependence is placed where it belongs.
