In Appendix 10 of his Quran translation, Dr. Rashad Khalifa argues that God's use of "We" in the Quran is not a plural of majesty.[^1] He states the rule directly: "Whenever the first person plural form is used by the Almighty, it invariably indicates participation of other entities, such as the angels."[^2] The claim is not that the plural *can* mark participation. It is that the plural *always* does. When the Quran says "We revealed" (15:9), the plural reflects Gabriel and Muhammad, an angel and a man, both taking part in delivering the message.[^3]

<QuranVerse verse="15:9" />

When God blows the breath of life into Adam, a creation that happens directly in heaven with no intermediary at all, the Quran shifts to the singular, "*I blew into Adam from My spirit*":[^4][^5]

<QuranVerse verse="15:29,38:72" />

When the same act happens to Jesus, mediated through Gabriel on earth, the plural returns.[^6][^7]

<QuranVerse verse="21:91,66:12" />

This is not a reading imposed backward onto the text by a modern translator. Ibn Kathir (d. 774 AH) says exactly this about Mary's conception, in his own words, with no polemical occasion prompting it. His tafsir is the single most widely used in the Sunni tradition after al-Tabari's. Commenting on 21:91, he writes:

That exact phrase, <Arabic>بِوَاسِطَةِ الْمَلَكِ وَهُوَ جِبْرِيلُ</Arabic> — "by the mediation of the angel, namely Gabriel" — recurs word for word across every printed edition of his tafsir. Khalifa's Adam-versus-Mary contrast is not an inference. It is Ibn Kathir's own stated reading of the same two passages.

When God addresses Moses directly, with no go-between of any kind, the pronoun drops to singular again (20:12–14).[^9]

<QuranVerse verse="20:12-14" />

The pattern tracks participation, not majesty. And on Khalifa's reading, the agent behind the plural is not restricted to one category of being. The standard traditionalist response is that this reading is a modern invention — something Dr. Khalifa introduced with no real footing in classical scholarship. That response does not survive contact with the sources. The idea that divine "We" marks literal participation by an agent carrying out God's command is not a fringe reading born in the twentieth century. It runs continuously through mainstream Sunni tafsir across five centuries. It runs through the classical lexicographers of Qur'anic vocabulary. It runs through the encyclopedists of Qur'anic rhetoric. It even shows up inside the work of Ibn Taymiyyah, the same scholar modern traditionalists cite as the authority for the opposite position.

<SectionDivider title="Two Explanations, Not One" />

Classical scholarship on the Quranic "We" splits into two recognized camps, not one. The first is <Arabic>جَمْعُ التَّعْظِيم</Arabic>, the plural of majesty, an honorific borrowed from royal speech. A king says "we decree" while remaining one person. This is the explanation most people encounter today, and it is treated as though it were the only explanation that ever existed.

The second is <Arabic>جَمْعٌ لِمُشَارَكَةِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ وَالْأَعْوَانِ</Arabic>, the plural of participation by angels or agents. On this reading, the act described is carried out through whoever God assigns to it, acting under His command. The plural covers God and that agent together — not as partners in His essence, but as an instrument carrying out His decree. Angels are the most frequently cited example, since most of the verses in question describe revelation, death, and cosmic events where angels are the obvious operators. A good example is found in what happens at the time of death:[^10][^11]

<QuranVerse verse="15:23,47:27" />

The first verse, 15:23, uses <Arabic>نَحْنُ نُمِيتُ</Arabic> ("We" cause death) — the plural. The second, 47:27, uses <Arabic>تَوَفَّتْهُمُ الْمَلَائِكَةُ</Arabic> / <Arabic>يَتَوَفَّى الْمَلَائِكَةُ</Arabic> ("the angels" put them to death). It is the exact same verb, <Arabic>تَوَفَّى</Arabic>, "to take [a soul]," but now with a real plural subject, <Arabic>الْمَلَائِكَةُ</Arabic>, explicitly named. Since the Quran uses the identical verb for the identical act in both cases, the pairing shows that the "We" of 15:23 is not empty grammatical majesty. It is a real plural that includes the angels as God's executing agents. This does not amount to shirk, because the angels act only by God's command: "*they do not precede Him in speech, and they act by His command*" (Q 21:27).[^12] That clause is the theological hinge. It keeps the reading from becoming multiple independent agents. This is delegated causation, not co-equal causation.

Ibn Kathir applies precisely this reasoning to the neighboring death-and-nearness verses. Commenting on "*and We are nearer to him than you*" (56:85), he glosses it flatly as "*by Our angels.*" He extends the identical gloss to "*and We are nearer to him than the jugular vein*" (50:16), citing 56:85 and 15:9 together as three instances of the same pattern.[^13] The <Arabic>نَحْنُ</Arabic> of causing death and the <Arabic>مَلَائِكَةُ</Arabic> of taking souls sit inside one continuous exegetical habit. This is not an isolated coincidence Dr. Khalifa alone noticed.

Nor is this a private theological hunch about grammar. Classical exegetes had a standing name for exactly this either/or, applied routinely to first-person-plural verbs that had nothing to do with revelation or the Trinity. Ibn 'Atiyya (d. 542 AH) explains a Qur'anic reading variant as "*either the nūn of magnification, or it belongs to the angels.*"[^14] Abu Hayyan al-Andalusi (d. 745 AH) gives the identical two options — "*the nūn of magnification, or the nūn of the company of angels*" — for a plural verb in Surat al-'Ankabut (29:55).[^15] Ibn 'Adil al-Hanbali (d. 775 AH) repeats the same formula for the same verse.[^16] Mujir al-Din al-'Ulaymi (d. 927 AH) repeats it again, word for word, a century and a half later.[^17] Four scholars, four different centuries, a Maliki, a grammarian, a Hanbali, and a Shafi'i — and none of them arguing with anyone. "Maybe it's the angels" is simply the routine second reading whenever a divine or near-divine verb shows up in the plural.

<SectionDivider title="Ibn Taymiyyah Names Both, On the Record" />

Ibn Taymiyyah is a good example of this, because he is usually invoked as the champion of the majesty-only reading. In *Bayan Talbis al-Jahmiyya*, responding to a Christian who cited the Quran's "We" as proof of the Trinity, his actual argument is more precise than a simple "there are two options," and more useful to this case. He writes that the word <Arabic>نَحْنُ</Arabic> can be used "*for the One who has [real] partners, and for the singular, magnified, obeyed one who has subjects (mamālīk) that obey him,*" and that this is exactly the ambiguity (<Arabic>تَشَابُه</Arabic>) the Christians exploited.[^18] His resolution is not "it's just majesty." It is that God's claim to the majestic plural is stronger than any king's, precisely because His subordinates are real: "*everything besides Him is created and owned by Him — from among the angels and others — so He has more right to the nūn of magnification than those kings who use this expression for it.*"[^19]

He repeats and sharpens this at least four more times across *Majmu' al-Fatawa*. Discussing the occasion of the argument — the Najran Christian delegation who cited the Qur'an's "<Arabic>إِنَّا</Arabic>" and "<Arabic>نَحْنُ</Arabic>" as evidence for three gods — he writes that these terms:

> "*are only said of a singular one who has helpers, either they are partners with him, or they are his property/subjects... and He, glory be to Him, governs the affair of the world by Himself and by His angels, who are His messengers in His creation and command, and He has the most right of anyone to say 'innā' and 'naḥnu' on this consideration.*"[^20]

That is the participatory reading stated as an argument for God's greater claim to the plural, not a concession that the plural is empty honorific. Both readings rule out the Trinity: real partners are impossible for God, but owned and commanded subordinates are not. Ibn Taymiyyah does not choose one over the other. He uses both, together, in the same breath.

<SectionDivider title="The Earliest Confirmed Layer" />

The claim that this is a late theological improvisation runs into trouble as soon as you look at how early scholars handled the Qur'an's habit of shifting between singular and plural in divine speech at all. Imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 241 AH) takes up this exact problem in *al-Radd 'ala al-Jahmiyya wa'l-Zanadiqa*. He answers doubters who pointed to God saying "*Indeed I am with you both, hearing and seeing*" (20:46, to Moses and Aaron) alongside "*Indeed We are with you, listening*" (26:15, the same event), as if the shift were a contradiction.[^21] His answer treats the alternation as ordinary linguistic idiom, not evidence of anything suspicious and not something requiring embarrassment. That establishes, in the third Islamic century, that the Qur'an's pronoun variation had already been noticed, questioned by skeptics, and answered by the tradition's founding jurists — centuries before anyone was debating Trinity proof-texts with Christian missionaries. Later Hanbali jurists, among them al-Qadi Abu Ya'la, read Ahmad's brief comment as reported by Ibn Taymiyyah to mean exactly what the participatory reading argues: "*it is permissible in the language for a single great one who has agents (a'wan) to say 'we did such-and-such.'*"[^22]

The specific two-explanation structure — majesty as one live option, agency as the other, named side by side rather than fought over — is on record by the fifth century AH. Makki ibn Abi Talib (d. 437 AH) explains the shift from singular to plural in "*My Lord, send me back*" (23:99) exactly this way in *al-Hidaya ila Bulugh al-Nihaya*: the disbeliever addresses God alone in the singular, then turns to address the angels who seize his soul, hence the plural.[^23] Both explanations are offered as ordinary, uncontroversial options, in a work of Qur'anic grammar with no theological argument to win.

<SectionDivider title="The Clearest Classical Statement: al-Raghib al-Isfahani" />

The clearest confirmation that this was never a marginal position comes directly from al-Raghib al-Isfahani (d. c. 502 AH), in his own dictionary of Qur'anic vocabulary, *al-Mufradat fi Gharib al-Qur'an*. Al-Raghib records both explanations as live options in his own time, majesty being one. He then states the second explanation in language almost identical to Khalifa's:

> "*Some of the scholars said: God, exalted is He, mentions such expressions when the act named afterward is done through the mediation of some of His angels, or some of His agents, so that 'We' becomes an expression for Him together with them.*"[^24]

He applies this exact framework to 15:9, tracing the descent of the Quran through the Pen, the Tablet, and Gabriel, and to 56:85, where "We are nearer to him than you" refers to the moment of death, when the angels are the ones present taking the soul.[^25]

This is a lexical reference work, not a polemical tract. Al-Raghib is not defending Islam from an outside challenge. He is defining how a specific grammatical form functions across the text, and angelic participation is one of the two definitions on record, stated as plainly as the majesty reading sitting right next to it. Two later lexicographers carried the same passage forward almost word for word: al-Samin al-Halabi (d. 756 AH), in *'Umdat al-Huffaz*, and al-Firuzabadi (d. 817 AH) — author of the standard Arabic dictionary *al-Qamus al-Muhit* — in his own *Basa'ir Dhawi al-Tamyiz*.[^26] Al-Firuzabadi's version adds a genuine refinement rather than simply copying. He explicitly rules the agentive reading out for "*and We are nearer to him than his jugular vein*" (50:16), insisting that verse has to be read as plain majesty instead.[^27] Three lexicographers across three centuries kept this reading alive, and they kept arguing over exactly where its boundary falls. That is the mark of a real, working grammatical category.

<SectionDivider title="Ibn Kathir Draws the Line Himself" />

Beyond Mary's conception, Ibn Kathir ties the reading together across the Qur'an in his own words. On "*and We are nearer to him than the jugular vein*" (50:16) he writes:

He is not being asked to defend this against a Christian, a Mu'tazilite, or anyone else. He is simply reading the Qur'an, verse by verse, and reaching for the same explanation each time three of Khalifa's own key verses come up.

Two later scholars independently apply the identical reading to Mary's conception specifically. Al-Khatib al-Sharbini (d. 977 AH) fuses majesty and mediation into a single clause on 66:12 in *al-Siraj al-Munir*: "*by virtue of what belongs to Us of majesty, through the mediation of Our angel Gabriel.*"[^29] He does not treat these as competing theories. The plural is majestic because it proceeds through the angel. Al-Suyuti (d. 911 AH) treats angelic mediation as a live, unremarkable reading of the same verse, 21:91, not in his famous *al-Durr al-Manthur* but in a separate work specifically on Qur'anic inimitability, *Mu'tarak al-Aqran fi I'jaz al-Qur'an*: "*that casting was either through the mediation of the angel referred to by 'the spirit,' or without it.*"[^30]

<SectionDivider title="The Mechanism Behind the Pronoun" />

None of this works unless revelation itself actually runs through angelic mediation as a matter of course, and that premise is not asserted only by implication. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 606 AH) states it as settled doctrine in *Mafatih al-Ghayb*, grounded in the Qur'an's own ordering of belief-objects at the end of Surat al-Baqara: "*the believers — all have believed in God, and His angels, and His books, and His messengers*" (2:285). He argues the order is not accidental:

> "*Revelation's arrival from God to the prophets never occurs except by the mediation of the angels... He began with God, then the angels, because they are the ones who receive the revelation from God directly, without mediation, and then convey it to the prophets.*"[^31]

This is the mechanism the participatory reading of 15:9 depends on. It is stated independently by one of the tradition's most influential rationalist exegetes, without reference to the pronoun question at all.

Badr al-Din al-Zarkashi (d. 794 AH) goes a step further and states the pronoun's connection to that mechanism as a general rule. His *al-Burhan fi 'Ulum al-Qur'an* is one of the two or three most authoritative reference works ever written on Qur'anic science. In his chapter on <Arabic>اِلْتِفَات</Arabic> — the grammatical device of shifting between persons — he explains why the Qur'an moves from third person to first person in "*God, who sent the winds, so they raise a cloud... then We drove it to a dead land*" (35:9):

> "*It is His habitual practice, glory be to Him, in all these acts, to speak of them with the nūn of magnification that indicates He has troops and creation whom He has subjected to that end.*"[^32]

He backs this "habitual practice" claim by pointing to the same first-person shift elsewhere in the Qur'an, including "*so when We recite it, follow its recitation*" (75:18), which he glosses as "*when Our messenger Gabriel recites it.*"[^33] Al-Zarkashi works this out in direct conversation with 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani and al-Zamakhshari on the mechanics of <Arabic>اِلْتِفَات</Arabic>. This is mainstream Arabic rhetorical scholarship on the Qur'an treating the participatory plural as a working assumption about how the language functions, not a theological special plea.

Al-Zamakhshari (d. 538 AH) himself does not make this exact grammatical argument on 15:9 in *al-Kashshaf*. His emphatic reading of "<Arabic>إِنَّا نَحْنُ</Arabic>" there is built around underscoring God's own decisive responsibility for sending the Qur'an down, not an explicit joint pronoun. But even so, he takes Gabriel's active role in the event for granted as part of what the verse describes: "*He is the one who dispatched Gabriel with it to Muhammad, with watchers before him and behind him, until it descended... preserved from the devils.*"[^34] Even where an exegete's own emphasis lands on majesty, Gabriel's participation in the act is never in question.

<SectionDivider title="Not Only Angels" />

Al-Raghib al-Isfahani's own definition already names the category as "*some of His angels or some of His agents,*" a phrase that opens the door beyond angelic beings without anyone having to stretch it there. Ibn Taymiyyah's version of the same joint explanation uses the same pairing in *Bayan Talbis al-Jahmiyya*: a sovereign speaking in the plural because he has subordinates under his real ownership and command, not because those subordinates are all one kind of creature.[^35] Khalifa's own reading already reflects this. His example for 15:9 names Gabriel and Muhammad together, an angel and a man, both credited inside the same plural pronoun. That is not an inconsistency in the classical category. It is the category working as designed. The relevant division in the sources was never angel versus human. It was single divine actor versus divine actor plus whoever carries the task out, whatever that agent happens to be.

<SectionDivider title="What the Objection Actually Assumes" />

The idea that Dr. Khalifa invented this reading rests on the assumption that <Arabic>جَمْعُ التَّعْظِيم</Arabic> was always the only serious explanation, and everything else is a recent workaround invented to answer Christians. The sources above make that assumption hard to hold. Ahmad ibn Hanbal was answering doubts about the Qur'an's pronoun shifts in the third Islamic century, long before organized polemics with Christian missionaries over the Trinity became a live genre. Makki ibn Abi Talib and al-Raghib al-Isfahani were writing grammar and lexicography, not apologetics, and they named the agentive reading as a live option with no argument to win. Al-Zarkashi built a standard reference work on Qur'anic rhetoric still assigned in seminaries today, and he states the participatory reading as a general rule, not a defensive maneuver. Ibn Kathir, the most-read tafsir in the Sunni world after al-Tabari's, reaches for the identical explanation unprompted, every time one of Khalifa's key verses comes up. And Ibn Taymiyyah — the scholar most often cited as the source of the majesty-only position — is on record naming both explanations and using them together against a Christian interlocutor, arguing that God has the greater claim to the plural precisely because His subordinates are real.

What actually happened, if the sources are read in order, is closer to the reverse of the traditionalist claim. The angelic-participation reading is attested, explicitly and by name, in mainstream Sunni scholarship continuously from the fifth century AH forward. It appears in lexicons. It appears in the two or three most important Qur'anic-science encyclopedias in the tradition. It appears in the most widely read tafsir after al-Tabari's. And its underlying puzzle — why does the Qur'an's divine speech vary between singular and plural at all — was already being seriously answered in the third century. The plural-of-majesty reading, borrowed from the vocabulary of kings, became the more repeated answer in later popular use, partly because it is shorter and easier to deploy in a live argument than tracing which verses involve angelic mediation and which do not. Convenience is not the same thing as precedent. On the sources that survive scrutiny, the precedent runs the other way.

<SectionDivider title="Where Khalifa's Reading Goes Further" />

Dr. Khalifa's specific contribution is not the existence of the agentic reading. The sources above already establish that. His contribution is the precision with which he applies it, and the absoluteness of the rule he states. Al-Raghib names 15:9 and 56:85. Ibn Kathir names 50:16, 56:85, and 15:9, tying them together himself. Al-Sharbini and al-Suyuti independently apply the same reading to 21:91 and 66:12. None of them lay out the full pattern across the whole Quran the way Khalifa does, and none of them claim, as he does, that the plural *invariably* signals participation rather than sometimes doing so.[^36] Singular where God acts alone and directly, as with Adam and with Moses. Plural wherever an angel is textually involved in carrying the act out, as with Jesus's creation and the Quran's descent. That is not a new theological claim. It is the same classical grammatical category, <Arabic>جَمْعٌ لِمُشَارَكَةِ الْمَلَائِكَةِ</Arabic>, checked systematically against every relevant verse instead of a handful of proof texts scattered across a dozen different books written centuries apart. The Trinity-defense angle he opens the appendix with is a modern occasion for raising the topic. The grammatical machinery underneath it is not modern at all.

Contemporary scholars of Qur'anic rhetoric still teach a version of it. Fadel Saleh al-Samarra'i, in a recorded lecture series on Qur'anic eloquence, independently lays out the identical Adam-versus-Mary contrast: "*when God's command is delivered through the mediation of an angel, it comes with the plural pronoun; and when it is not, it is singular.*"[^37] He cites 21:91 against 38:72 by name — the same pairing Khalifa uses in Appendix 10 — arrived at independently by a scholar working inside the classical tradition rather than defending it.

<SectionDivider title="Conclusion" />

The claim that God's plural "We" reflects agentic participation rather than royal self-reference is not a twentieth-century apologetic invention. Its underlying puzzle was already being answered by Ahmad ibn Hanbal in the third Islamic century. The specific two-explanation structure — majesty as one reading, angelic or agentive participation as the other, offered side by side — is on record by the fifth century in Makki ibn Abi Talib. It is stated in its clearest and most explicit form by al-Raghib al-Isfahani, and carried forward faithfully by al-Samin al-Halabi and al-Firuzabadi across three more centuries. It is applied verse by verse to exactly Khalifa's own examples by Ibn Kathir. It is restated as a general principle of Qur'anic rhetoric by al-Zarkashi, in dialogue with al-Jurjani and al-Zamakhshari. It is grounded in its theological mechanism by al-Razi. And, the sharpest confirmation of all, it is named explicitly — alongside the majesty reading rather than instead of it — by Ibn Taymiyyah himself, the very scholar modern traditionalists invoke as the authority against it.

The modern insistence that <Arabic>جَمْعُ التَّعْظِيم</Arabic> is the only classical position does not describe the actual history of the question. It describes which answer became easiest to repeat. Khalifa's reading in Appendix 10 sits inside a real, well-documented, cross-generational tradition. He applies it with more textual consistency, and states it in more absolute terms, than most of the scholars who articulated it ever had occasion to.

<VerdictBox title="Summary Verdict">
  <p>The participatory plural (<Arabic>جَمْعُ الْمُشَارَكَةِ</Arabic>) is not a modern invention. It is a live, named, continuously attested reading in mainstream Sunni scholarship from the 3rd/9th century forward. Khalifa's contribution is not creating the category but applying it with systematic consistency across the entire Quranic text.</p>
</VerdictBox>
