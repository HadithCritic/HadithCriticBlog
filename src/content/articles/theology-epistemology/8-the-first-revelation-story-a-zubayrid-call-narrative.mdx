## The Problem

The famous story of the first revelation is usually told as if it were direct memory. Muhammad withdraws to Ḥirāʾ. Gabriel appears. The angel commands him, “Read.” Muhammad says, “I cannot read.” The command is repeated. The opening verses of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq are revealed. Muhammad returns to Khadījah in fear. Khadījah consoles him. Waraqah b. Nawfal, the learned Christian relative, confirms that the visitor is the same heavenly messenger who came to Moses.

The story is powerful. It explains the beginning of revelation, the first Qurʾānic words, Khadījah’s role, Waraqah’s scriptural validation, and Muhammad’s first confrontation with the terror of prophecy. But the question for a historian is not whether the story is powerful. The question is where the story becomes visible, what its transmissions look like, and why its narrative structure resembles a known Christian call-story so closely.

<ClaimBox>
The iqraʾ narrative is early as Islamic tradition, but weak as direct cave-memory. Its recoverable forms point strongly into Zubayrid-linked transmission, with one stream through ʿUrwah → al-Zuhrī and another through the Meccan storyteller ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr at the court of ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr.
</ClaimBox>

The goal here is not to deny that Muhammad had a revelatory beginning. The goal is narrower. The polished “Read!” story, as it survives in hadith and sīrah, looks like a crafted call narrative. It has an isnād history. It has a literary shape. It has Qurʾānic interests. And it has a striking parallel in Bede’s story of Cædmon.

## The Canonical Hadith Form

The most influential form is the ʿĀʾishah report. Its upper route is:

**ʿĀʾishah → ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr → Ibn Shihāb al-Zuhrī.**

After al-Zuhrī, the report spreads through later transmitters and compilers. One early witness is ʿAbd al-Razzāq through Maʿmar from al-Zuhrī. Isḥāq b. Rāhawayh preserves the same broad form through ʿAbd al-Razzāq. Bukhārī then gives the version that becomes the standard form at the opening of his Ṣaḥīḥ.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">أَوَّلُ مَا بُدِئَ بِهِ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ مِنَ الْوَحْيِ الرُّؤْيَا الصَّادِقَةُ، فَكَانَ لَا يَرَى رُؤْيَا إِلَّا جَاءَتْ مِثْلَ فَلَقِ الصُّبْحِ، ثُمَّ حُبِّبَ إِلَيْهِ الْخَلَاءُ، فَكَانَ يَأْتِي حِرَاءً فَيَتَحَنَّثُ فِيهِ...</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The first thing with which the Messenger of God was begun from revelation was the true dream. He would not see a dream except that it came like the break of dawn. Then seclusion was made beloved to him, and he would go to Ḥirāʾ and worship there...”</div>
</HadithBlock>

The report then gives the famous scene.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">فَجَاءَهُ الْمَلَكُ فَقَالَ: اقْرَأْ. فَقَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: مَا أَنَا بِقَارِئٍ. قَالَ: فَأَخَذَنِي فَغَطَّنِي حَتَّى بَلَغَ مِنِّي الْجَهْدَ، ثُمَّ أَرْسَلَنِي فَقَالَ: اقْرَأْ...</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The angel came to him and said, ‘Read.’ The Messenger of God said, ‘I am not one who reads.’ He said, ‘Then he seized me and pressed me until I was exhausted, then released me and said, ‘Read.’”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This version is not simply a memory of a cave. It is a structured call narrative. It begins with true dreams, moves to seclusion, brings an angelic command, repeats the refusal, attaches the scene to Qurʾān 96:1–5, and ends with Khadījah and Waraqah validating the experience.

## The Rayy Recension in al-Ṭabarī

The most important source for the storyteller hypothesis is the Rayy-recension material preserved in al-Ṭabarī. The practical way to find this material is by filtering for the isnād:

**Ibn Ḥumayd → Salamah b. al-Faḍl → Ibn Isḥāq.**

In the first-revelation passage, Ṭabarī gives the chain:

**Muḥammad b. Ḥumayd al-Rāzī → Salamah b. al-Faḍl → Muḥammad b. Isḥāq → Wahb b. Kaysān, mawlā of the House of al-Zubayr → ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr → ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr.**

That chain matters because it does not merely transmit the story. It frames the story as a Zubayrid court event. Wahb b. Kaysān says that he heard ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr ask ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr to tell them how the Messenger of God first began prophethood when Gabriel came to him.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">سَمِعْتُ عَبْدَ اللَّهِ بْنَ الزُّبَيْرِ، وَهُوَ يَقُولُ لِعُبَيْدِ بْنِ عُمَيْرِ بْنِ قَتَادَةَ اللَّيْثِيِّ: حَدِّثْنَا يَا عُبَيْدُ، كَيْفَ كَانَ بَدْءُ مَا ابْتُدِئَ بِهِ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ مِنَ النُّبُوَّةِ، حِينَ جَاءَهُ جِبْرِيلُ؟</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“I heard ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr saying to ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr b. Qatādah al-Laythī: ‘Tell us, O ʿUbayd, how the Messenger of God first began prophethood when Gabriel came to him.’”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is the core of the argument. The dramatic iqraʾ story is not merely floating in Ibn Isḥāq. In this stream, it is attached to a Meccan storyteller, in a Zubayrid court setting, transmitted by a client of the House of al-Zubayr.

For a lay reader, the important point is simple. The story does not appear as a broad Companion memory with many independent early roots. Its most influential forms concentrate around Zubayrid-linked transmitters. ʿUrwah is a central figure of the Zubayrid household. ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr is the Zubayrid claimant in Mecca. Wahb b. Kaysān is attached to the House of al-Zubayr. ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr is the Meccan storyteller in the scene.

<ContextNote>
This does not prove that one person invented the story. It means the earliest recoverable forms of the polished iqraʾ narrative are not socially neutral. They cluster in a Zubayrid memory network.
</ContextNote>

## Why ʿĀʾishah Is Not an Eyewitness Here

The ʿĀʾishah attribution is important, but it must be handled carefully. ʿĀʾishah was not present at Ḥirāʾ. She was not an eyewitness to the first revelation. The report attributed to her is therefore not a direct memory of the event. It is a report about the event transmitted through family and teaching networks.

That does not make the report worthless. But it changes the question. We are not asking, “What did ʿĀʾishah see?” We are asking, “What form of the first-revelation story circulated through ʿUrwah and al-Zuhrī under ʿĀʾishah’s authority?”

That distinction matters. A report can carry an early Companion name while still being a later household narrative, shaped by theological, Qurʾānic, and literary needs.

## The Meccan Storyteller Hypothesis

The strongest candidate for the narrative crystallizer is ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr. That does not mean he definitely fabricated the story. It means he fits the social role better than almost anyone else in the surviving record.

He is not merely a transmitter. He is a **qāṣṣ**, a storyteller-preacher. His function was to narrate sacred material in public, to make hidden beginnings vivid, and to turn religious memory into memorable moral instruction. The first-revelation story has exactly that kind of shape. It dramatizes an otherwise hidden event. It turns the first revelation into dialogue. It explains why Sūrat al-ʿAlaq begins with **iqraʾ**. It gives Muhammad fear, crisis, and validation. It brings in Khadījah as moral witness and Waraqah as scriptural witness.

Schoeler’s reconstruction is important here. He argues that ʿUbayd combined a set of motifs that circulated around the beginnings of revelation: Ḥirāʾ devotion, Khadījah, iqraʾ, the horizon vision, and Waraqah. In that reconstruction, the result was a coherent, edifying, and entertaining story. As a qāṣṣ, ʿUbayd may have embellished and extended some elements. The story then spread through the Zubayrid family as a family tradition.[^1]

This is the most careful way to state the theory: ʿUbayd is not proven to be the inventor, but he is the best candidate for the narrative organizer of the dramatic form.

## The Source-Critical Debate

The qāṣṣ hypothesis is not just a modern suspicion. Görke and Schoeler explicitly discuss the possibility that ʿUrwah’s source was the same family tradition that Ibn Isḥāq transmits through Wahb b. Kaysān from ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr. They point out that ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr, ʿUrwah’s elder brother, asks ʿUbayd in a meeting to report the Prophet’s first revelation. They then state that the al-Zuhrī / Hishām b. ʿUrwah tradition from ʿUrwah and the Ibn Isḥāq tradition from Wahb b. Kaysān and ʿUbayd are so close that they must have the same origin.[^2]

That is the key. The two streams are not identical, but they are too close to be treated as fully independent. One can debate where the Qurʾānic elements entered. They may come from ʿUbayd, Wahb, Ibn Isḥāq, al-Zuhrī, or several stages together. But the broad conclusion remains: the story is a Zubayrid-linked call narrative, not a simple transcript from Ḥirāʾ.

Shoemaker is the main methodological counterweight. He argues that many attempts to reconstruct ʿUrwah’s sīrah push the evidence too far. In his view, al-Zuhrī and sometimes figures of his generation can be linked to traditions, but the reach back to ʿUrwah is often less secure.[^3] This warning matters. It prevents overclaiming. But even on a more skeptical reading, the story remains early, al-Zuhrī-linked, and Zubayrid-adjacent. That is enough for the argument here.

<ClaimBox title="The Careful Claim">
The safest conclusion is not “ʿUbayd invented the story.” The safer conclusion is that the dramatic iqraʾ narrative likely crystallized in a Meccan-Zubayrid storytelling environment and then spread through two related Zubayrid exits: ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr → Wahb → Ibn Isḥāq, and ʿUrwah → al-Zuhrī.
</ClaimBox>

## Ibn Isḥāq’s Dramatic Form

The Ibn Isḥāq / Ṭabarī form is more vivid than the shorter canonical hadith form. Muhammad is at Ḥirāʾ. Gabriel comes while he is asleep. The angel brings a written object. The command is “Read.” Muhammad resists. The words of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq are placed into his heart. He wakes frightened and considers whether he is a poet or possessed. He thinks of throwing himself from the mountain, until Gabriel appears again and confirms his prophethood.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">فَجَاءَنِي جِبْرِيلُ وَأَنَا نَائِمٌ بِنَمَطٍ مِنْ دِيبَاجٍ، فِيهِ كِتَابٌ، فَقَالَ: اقْرَأْ. فَقُلْتُ: مَا أَقْرَأُ؟</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“Gabriel came to me while I was asleep with a brocade cloth in which there was writing. He said, ‘Read.’ I said, ‘What shall I read?’”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This version matters because it is the form most closely resembling the Cædmon pattern. The visitor comes in sleep. There is a command. There is an inability response. Sacred speech follows. The content concerns creation. The experience is later validated by authorities.

## The Cædmon Story

Bede’s story of Cædmon appears in the *Ecclesiastical History of the English People*. Cædmon is an unlearned man who cannot sing. At feasts, when the harp is passed around, he leaves in shame because he has no song. One night, after leaving a feast, he goes to the cattle stable and falls asleep. In a dream, someone calls him by name and commands him to sing. Cædmon says he cannot sing. The figure insists. Cædmon asks what he should sing about. The answer is: sing about the beginning of created things. He then sings verses praising God the Creator, words he had never learned before. In the morning, he remembers the song and is taken to religious authorities who test and validate the gift.

The resemblance to the iqraʾ narrative is not just one shared motif. It is a sequence.

## Which Direction Did the Influence Run?

The direction is debated. Older comparisons often raised the possibility that the Islamic iqraʾ narrative and the Cædmon story share a direct relationship. Some scholars argued for Muhammad’s call influencing Cædmon. Others were more cautious and preferred a shared call-story pattern.

Richard North has recently argued directly for the direction **Muhammad story → Bede’s Cædmon story**. His model is that Arculf heard the Muhammad call story around Jerusalem around 680, transmitted it to Adomnán in Iona in 683–686, and Adomnán passed it to Bede with *De locis sanctis* in Jarrow in 688.[^4] Whether one accepts North’s full route or not, it matters because it shows that the Cædmon comparison is not merely a fringe observation. It is now a serious scholarly proposal.

The reverse direction, Cædmon → Meccan storyteller, is much weaker. Cædmon belongs to the Whitby world under Abbess Hild in the later seventh century, and Bede writes the story around 731. It is possible that a Cædmon-like oral story circulated before Bede, but we do not have evidence that it travelled from Northumbria to the Hijaz before the Zubayrid court. So the Meccan qāṣṣ did not need Cædmon specifically. He needed access to a broader call-story repertoire.

That broader repertoire is older. Biblical and Christian traditions already contain scenes where a chosen but inadequate person is commanded, resists, receives divine speech, or is validated by an authority. The Cædmon story and the iqraʾ story are best read inside that late antique world of sacred speech and visionary commissioning.

## The Qurʾānization of the Story

The iqraʾ story is also exegetical. It explains why Sūrat al-ʿAlaq begins with the command **iqraʾ**. The Qurʾānic word becomes the engine of the narrative. Gabriel says “iqraʾ.” Muhammad replies with a phrase built from the same root. The repeated command becomes the dramatic setup for the revelation of 96:1–5.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">اقْرَأْ بِاسْمِ رَبِّكَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ، خَلَقَ الْإِنْسَانَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ، اقْرَأْ وَرَبُّكَ الْأَكْرَمُ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“Read in the name of your Lord who created, created man from a clot. Read, and your Lord is Most Generous.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This matters because the first-revelation story may not simply preserve the first moment of revelation. It may be a narrative built around a Qurʾānic opening. Anthony notes that the iqraʾ narrative claims Q. 96 is the first revelation, but modern Qurʾānic chronology has often rejected that claim on internal literary grounds. He treats the iqraʾ narrative as early and valuable for studying how sīrah traditions formed, but not as historically trustworthy for reconstructing the earliest Qurʾānic revelation.[^5]

That is a very important distinction. The story is early as tradition. It is not necessarily early as event-memory.

## Khadījah and Waraqah

Khadījah and Waraqah are not decorative figures. They solve the narrative problem. Muhammad is terrified and uncertain. Khadījah provides moral reassurance. Waraqah provides scriptural validation. He is the learned figure who says that the being who came to Muhammad is the same heavenly messenger who came to Moses.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">فَقَالَتْ خَدِيجَةُ: كَلَّا وَاللَّهِ لَا يُخْزِيكَ اللَّهُ أَبَدًا، إِنَّكَ لَتَصِلُ الرَّحِمَ، وَتَصْدُقُ الْحَدِيثَ، وَتَحْمِلُ الْكَلَّ، وَتُقْرِي الضَّيْفَ، وَتُعِينُ عَلَى نَوَائِبِ الْحَقِّ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“Khadījah said: ‘No, by God, God will never disgrace you. You maintain kinship, speak truthfully, bear the burdens of others, host the guest, and help in the hardships of truth.’”</div>
</HadithBlock>

Waraqah then moves the experience into biblical history.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">هَذَا النَّامُوسُ الَّذِي أَنْزَلَ اللَّهُ عَلَى مُوسَى</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“This is the heavenly messenger whom God sent down to Moses.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is exactly what a call narrative needs. The chosen figure does not understand the event at first. A recognized authority interprets it. The frightening experience becomes a prophetic mission.

## The Attempted-Suicide Motif

One of the most striking features of some versions is Muhammad’s crisis after the first encounter. In Ibn Isḥāq’s version, he fears that he is a poet or possessed and considers throwing himself from the mountain. In some al-Zuhrī-related biographical variants, the attempted-suicide motif appears in connection with the lapse of revelation. In the canonical hadith collections, the most troubling forms are softened or excluded.

This pattern matters. It suggests that the story circulated in more dramatic forms before later tradition stabilized the more acceptable version. The raw literary form includes fear, doubt, and a near-catastrophic crisis. Later hadith transmission preserves a cleaner version: terror, yes, but not the same full dramatic despair.

<ContextNote>
The attempted-suicide motif is not needed for the theology of prophethood. It is needed for the drama of a call narrative. Its presence in some streams and absence in others shows that the matn was being shaped.
</ContextNote>

## What the Corpus Shows

The available corpus supports a two-stream model. The first stream is the canonical hadith family:

**ʿĀʾishah → ʿUrwah → al-Zuhrī → Maʿmar / Yūnus / later transmitters.**

This stream gives the form that becomes dominant in hadith. It contains true dreams, seclusion, Ḥirāʾ, the angelic command, the refusal, the pressing, Q. 96:1–5, return to Khadījah, reassurance, and Waraqah’s validation.

The second stream is the Rayy / Ibn Isḥāq family preserved in al-Ṭabarī:

**ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr → ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr → Wahb b. Kaysān → Ibn Isḥāq → Salamah b. al-Faḍl → Ibn Ḥumayd → al-Ṭabarī.**

This stream gives the dramatic storyteller form. It explicitly places the story in a Zubayrid court context. It contains the sleep setting, the written object, the repeated command, the “What shall I read?” reply, the placing of the words into Muhammad’s heart, and the mountain-crisis motif.

## Why the Zubayrid Setting Matters

The Zubayrid household was not just a private family. It was one of the major memory-making circles of early Islam. ʿĀʾishah was a central transmitter of prophetic household memory. ʿUrwah, her nephew, became one of the great early authorities for sīrah and maghāzī material. ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr was a political claimant in Mecca. Wahb b. Kaysān was attached to the House of al-Zubayr. This is a powerful social setting for the formation of early prophetic biography.

Anthony stresses that the Ibn Isḥāq frame places the story during the Second Civil War, when ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr and the Zubayrids were competing with the Umayyads for the leadership of the early Islamic polity.[^6] That context does not prove propaganda by itself, but it matters. A court claiming religious legitimacy has a strong reason to preserve, perform, and authorize stories about prophetic beginnings.

The first-revelation story does important work in that setting. It makes Khadījah the first human validator. It makes Waraqah, a Christian scriptural authority connected to the Meccan household world, the biblical witness. It turns Muhammad’s terrifying experience into a recognized prophetic mission. It also makes the beginning of the Qurʾān intelligible through narrative.

This does not mean the Zubayrids “invented Islam.” It means the polished first-revelation story, as a story, becomes recoverable in their memory network.

## Why the Story Looks Literary

A historical memory can be literary, but this story is highly literary. It has repeated dialogue. It has a threefold pressure scene. It has dramatic fear. It has a return home. It has a wise spouse. It has a learned scriptural interpreter. It has a prediction of rejection and victory. It has a Qurʾānic keyword turned into the center of the plot.

That is not how raw memory usually survives. It is how a community tells the origin of revelation.

The comparison with Cædmon makes this clearer. Bede’s story is not about Islamic history. It is a Christian hagiographical call story. Yet its structure matches the Ibn Isḥāq iqraʾ narrative at too many points: sleep, inability, command, sacred speech, creation theme, miraculous vernacular performance, validation. This does not prove a simple line of borrowing in every detail. But it does show that the iqraʾ story belongs to a recognizable literary world.

## What Would Change the Analysis?

This analysis would be weakened if we found many independent early Companion-level routes for the iqraʾ story that did not pass through ʿUrwah / al-Zuhrī or the Zubayrid storytelling environment. It would also be weakened if Ibn Isḥāq’s Zubayrid court frame were isolated and unrelated to the larger narrative shape. It would be weakened further if Cædmon only shared one generic motif, such as “a holy person receives inspiration.”

But that is not what we have. The canonical hadith form concentrates through ʿĀʾishah → ʿUrwah → al-Zuhrī. The Rayy / Ibn Isḥāq form is tied to ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr, ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr, and Wahb b. Kaysān. The Cædmon comparison is not one shared idea but a shared narrative sequence. The result is a strong cumulative case.

## Final Verdict

The famous first-revelation story is best understood as an early Islamic call narrative, not as a direct transcript from Ḥirāʾ. Its most influential hadith form runs through ʿĀʾishah, ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr, and al-Zuhrī. Its most dramatic sīrah form, preserved in the Rayy-recension material inside al-Ṭabarī, is explicitly tied to a Zubayrid court setting: ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr asks the Meccan storyteller ʿUbayd b. ʿUmayr to narrate the beginning of prophecy, and Wahb b. Kaysān, a client of the House of al-Zubayr, transmits the scene.

The matn is also not neutral. It is shaped around Qurʾān 96:1–5. The word **iqraʾ** becomes a scene. Gabriel commands. Muhammad refuses. The command is repeated. The revelation is attached to the first verses of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq. Khadījah and Waraqah then validate the experience. The story explains revelation, scripture, prophethood, and biblical attestation all at once.

The Cædmon parallel shows why the story cannot be treated as simple memory. Bede’s Cædmon and Ibn Isḥāq’s Muhammad both receive a nighttime visionary command to perform sacred speech despite claiming inability. Both are given a creation-themed utterance. Both are validated afterward by religious authorities. Whether one follows North’s proposal that Bede modelled Cædmon on the Muhammad story, or the more cautious view that both stories belong to a shared late antique call-story repertoire, the conclusion is similar: the iqraʾ narrative is literary and comparative, not raw cave-memory.

So the strongest conclusion is careful but pointed: the iqraʾ narrative is early, Zubayrid-linked, Qurʾānized, and literary. It may preserve the memory that Muhammad’s prophetic career began with a terrifying revelatory experience. But the polished story of Gabriel saying “Read,” as preserved in the canonical hadith and sīrah tradition, is a crafted origin narrative shaped by household transmission, Meccan storytelling, scriptural exegesis, and the wider call-story patterns of the late antique world.

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">
The first-revelation story should be treated as an early Zubayrid-linked call narrative. The most plausible source-critical model is not a direct transcript from Ḥirāʾ, but a story that crystallized in a Meccan qāṣṣ setting and then spread through Zubayrid household channels into al-Zuhrī and Ibn Isḥāq.
</VerdictBox>
