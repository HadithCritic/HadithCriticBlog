<ClaimBox title="Central thesis">

The Qurʾān says that God established twelve months and made four sacred, but it does not name them. Islamic tradition names Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, and Rajab (months 11, 12, 1, and 7).[^1] This article argues that the Qurʾān points to a different, continuous pilgrimage season: Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I (months **12–1–2–3**). It then asks how that earlier sequence may have given way to the familiar list.

</ClaimBox>

## What the Qurʾān says about the sacred months

### 1. Four sacred months fixed by God

The starting point is Q 9:36:

<QuranPassage verse="9:36">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The number of months with God is twelve in God's decree, since the day He created the heavens and the earth; four of them are sacred. That is the upright religion.”[^2]

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>إِنَّ عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ عِندَ اللَّهِ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا فِي كِتَابِ اللَّهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The verse gives us three facts: the year has twelve months, four are sacred, and this order is part of God’s decree. It does **not** tell us which four months they are.

Q 9:37 condemns *nasīʾ*: moving sacred time from one month to another. The verse says people did this while keeping “the number that God made sacred.” In other words, they kept the total at four but changed where those sacred months fell.

So the question is not only whether a proposed list contains four months. Q 9:37 makes their **placement** part of the issue. A later list cannot be assumed to preserve the original order just because it also contains four.

### 2. Q 9:1–5 reads as one continuous proclamation

The next clue appears earlier in the same sūra. Q 9:1 addresses polytheists who had agreements with the believers. Q 9:2 gives them a defined period in which to travel:

<QuranPassage verse="9:2">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“So travel freely in the land for four months.” (Q 9:2)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The proclamation does not begin with an attack. It gives them **four months** of safe movement. Q 9:3 then tells us when the announcement is made:

<QuranPassage verse="9:3">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“A proclamation from God and His messenger to the people on the Day of the Greater Pilgrimage...” (Q 9:3)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>وَأَذَانٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦٓ إِلَى ٱلنَّاسِ يَوْمَ ٱلْحَجِّ ٱلْأَكْبَرِ …</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The announcement is made during the Greater Pilgrimage, which anchors it in Dhū al-Ḥijja. Q 9:4 also says the proclamation does not cancel agreements with those who remain peaceful:

<QuranPassage verse="9:4">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“Except those of the polytheists with whom you made a treaty and who thereafter have failed you in nothing and supported no one against you; fulfill their treaty with them until its term. God loves the righteous.” (Q 9:4)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ عَٰهَدتُّم مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَنقُصُوكُمْ شَيْـًٔا وَلَمْ يُظَٰهِرُوا۟ عَلَيْكُمْ أَحَدًا فَأَتِمُّوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Q 9:5 continues the same passage:

<QuranPassage verse="9:5">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“When the sacred months have passed...” (Q 9:5)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>فَإِذَا انسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

- Q 9:2 grants **four months** of movement.
- Q 9:3 places the announcement at the pilgrimage.
- Q 9:4 keeps peaceful treaties in force.
- Q 9:5 says **“the sacred months”** have passed.

The passage introduces no other set of months. One straightforward reading is that Q 9:2 grants four months of protected movement, Q 9:3 anchors the announcement at the pilgrimage, Q 9:4 preserves peaceful treaties, and Q 9:5 marks the end of **those same sacred months**. After they pass, the special protection ends for those still in conflict.

Q 9:1–5 therefore connects a **continuous four-month period** with the pilgrimage and calls it “the sacred months.” The article argues that this fits a continuous season better than the traditional pattern of three consecutive months plus Rajab later in the year. That pattern is not stated in this passage; it comes from the later list.

### 3. Why the four months are peaceful: Q 2:217

Q 2:217 explains why the period might protect movement. It says:

<QuranPassage verse="2:217">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“They ask you about the sacred month, about fighting in it. Say: fighting in it is a grave matter.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلشَّهْرِ ٱلْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ ۖ قُلْ قِتَالٌ فِيهِ كَبِيرٌ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

We do not need to know which named month Q 2:217 refers to for its point to be clear: fighting during sacred time is a grave violation. If Q 9:2 describes the same sacred period, then the permission to “travel freely in the land” has a reason. Hostilities are restrained during sacred time, and Q 9:5 marks the end of that protection: **“when the sacred months have passed.”**

The passages fit together in this order:

- sacred time prohibits or gravely restricts fighting (Q 2:217);
- hostile polytheists are granted four months of free movement (Q 9:2);
- the announcement is made during the pilgrimage (Q 9:3);
- peaceful treaties remain binding (Q 9:4);
- the special prohibition associated with the sacred period expires only after “the sacred months” have passed (Q 9:5).

On this reading, the four months are **sacred time that protects movement**, not just a ceasefire unrelated to the calendar.

### 4. Sacred time belongs to the pilgrimage system

Two more verses connect the sacred month with pilgrimage.

Q 5:2 commands:

<QuranPassage verse="5:2">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“Do not violate God's rites, nor the sacred month, nor the offerings, nor the garlands, nor those heading toward the Sacred House...”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>لَا تُحِلُّوا۟ شَعَـٰٓئِرَ ٱللَّهِ وَلَا ٱلشَّهْرَ ٱلْحَرَامَ وَلَا ٱلْهَدْىَ وَلَا ٱلْقَلَـٰٓئِدَ وَلَآ ءَآمِّينَ ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْحَرَامَ …</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Q 5:2 lists the sacred month alongside God’s rites, offerings, garlands, and **people travelling toward the Sacred House**. This places sacred time within the wider system of pilgrimage and travel to the sanctuary.

Q 5:97 makes the connection even more explicit:

<QuranPassage verse="5:97">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“God has made the Kaʿba, the Sacred House, a support for mankind, and the sacred month, the offerings, and the garlands.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْكَعْبَةَ ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْحَرَامَ قِيَـٰمًا لِّلنَّاسِ وَٱلشَّهْرَ ٱلْحَرَامَ وَٱلْهَدْىَ وَٱلْقَلَـٰٓئِدَ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Here, sacred **space** and sacred **time** appear together with pilgrimage offerings. Other verses describe the sanctuary as a place of safety:

- Q 2:125 calls the House a place of return and security. 
- Q 3:97 says that whoever enters it is safe. 
- Q 29:67 speaks of God establishing a secure sanctuary while people are seized around it. 

Together, these verses suggest that the Sacred House provides protected **space**, while the sacred month provides protected **time**. Pilgrims need safety on the journey as well as at the sanctuary. That context helps explain Q 9:2’s promise to “travel freely in the land”: the protection can cover movement to and from the pilgrimage, not only the days spent performing rites in Mecca.

### 5. The Qurʾān distinguishes the months of ḥajj from its ritual days

Q 2:197 states:

<QuranPassage verse="2:197">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The ḥajj is during known months.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Q 2:197 gives no names or number, but it describes the ḥajj as lasting across **months**. When the Qurʾān refers to the concentrated rites, it speaks of **days**: Q 22:28 says “known days” (*ayyāmin maʿlūmāt*), and Q 2:203 says “numbered days” (*ayyāmin maʿdūdāt*).

This distinction matters: the days of the central rites at ʿArafāt, Muzdalifa, and Minā do not necessarily define the full span of the “known months” in which ḥajj takes place.

Some later exegetes define the pilgrimage months by asking when the rites end. But if the Qurʾān distinguishes the **months of ḥajj** from the **days of its rites**, the last ritual day alone does not prove that the wider pilgrimage season has ended.

The Qurʾān does not say that the “known months” of ḥajj are separate from the sacred months. Later interpretation makes that distinction. Read together, Q 2:197, Q 5:2, Q 5:97, and Q 9:1–5 can instead be understood as describing two parts of one institution: pilgrimage and the protection of its season.

### 6. The lunar calendar itself is tied to pilgrimage

Q 2:189 adds another piece:

<QuranPassage verse="2:189">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“They ask you about the crescents. Say: they are measurements of time for mankind and for the ḥajj.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلْأَهِلَّةِ ۖ قُلْ هِىَ مَوَٰقِيتُ لِلنَّاسِ وَٱلْحَجِّ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Q 2:189 links the lunar calendar directly to pilgrimage. The moon’s phases are not just a backdrop to ḥajj; they help mark its time.

The argument so far is:

- the lunar crescents regulate time and the ḥajj (Q 2:189);
- the ḥajj occupies known **months** (Q 2:197);
- its concentrated rites occupy known or numbered **days** (Q 22:28; 2:203);
- the sacred month protects the rites, offerings, and those travelling toward the Sacred House (Q 5:2);
- the Sacred House and sacred time belong to one institutional system (Q 5:97);
- four months of protected movement are proclaimed at the Greater Pilgrimage and their expiration is called the expiration of “the sacred months” (Q 9:2–5);
- the annual order contains exactly four sacred months whose placement is not to be manipulated (Q 9:36–37).

Together, these passages support reading the sacred months as a **continuous pilgrimage season**. That is the article’s interpretation of the Qurʾānic evidence; the verses do not give the months’ names.

### 7. What the Qurʾān establishes—and what it does not

The Qurʾān never says, “the sacred months are Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I.” It gives neither those names nor the numbers 12–1–2–3. The proposed sequence is a reconstruction from how the passages fit together, not a list stated in the Qurʾān.

What the Qurʾān establishes is the underlying structure:

1. there are four sacred months in a divinely fixed calendar;
2. altering their placement while preserving the number is condemned;
3. the only continuous four-month period narrated in the relevant context is proclaimed at the pilgrimage;
4. its expiration is called the expiration of “the sacred months”;
5. sacred time elsewhere is repeatedly integrated with the Sacred House, offerings, pilgrims, and the journey to ḥajj;
6. the ḥajj itself occupies “known months,” broader than the ritual “days.”

On this reading, the Qurʾān describes a continuous four-month sacred season centered on pilgrimage. Since the proclamation is made during Dhū al-Ḥijja, the sequence to test against the historical evidence is:

**Dhū al-Ḥijja → al-Muḥarram → Ṣafar → Rabīʿ I**  
**12 → 1 → 2 → 3**

The historical question is whether the earliest commentary and calendar traditions preserve evidence for precisely that sequence.

## What early reports add

### Early commentary links the proclamation to the months 12–1–2–3

Early commentary provides a bridge between the Qurʾānic passage and named months. Al-Ṭabarī preserves five early authorities who connect the period ending in Q 9:5 with the four months introduced in Q 9:2.[^3] Al-Suddī gives the fullest count, starting from the Day of Sacrifice in Dhū al-Ḥijja:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Suddī" source="Jāmiʿ al-bayān, al-Ṭabarī, 14:136, no. 16477 (al-Suddī report)">

“‘When the sacred months have passed.’ These are the four I listed for you, that is, twenty [days] of Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, Rabīʿ al-Awwal, and ten of Rabīʿ al-Ākhir.”[^4]

«(<Arabic>فإذا انسلخ الأشهر الحرم</Arabic>)<Arabic>، وهي الأربعة التي عددت لك</Arabic> = <Arabic>يعني: عشرين من ذي الحجة، والمحرم، وصفر، وربيعًا الأول، وعشرًا من شهر ربيع الآخر</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

Mujāhid, ʿAmr b. Shuʿayb, Ibn Zayd, and Ibn Isḥāq also connect Q 9:5’s ending with the four-month period announced in Q 9:2.[^5] Ibn Kathīr later explains why the count begins partway through Dhū al-Ḥijja: the terms became known on the Day of Sacrifice.[^6]

The count needs a caveat. Because the announcement came partway through Dhū al-Ḥijja, counting four elapsed months from that day reaches ten days into Rabīʿ II. That is the length of the announced protection; it does not make Rabīʿ II a fifth sacred month. Nor does it mean Dhū al-Ḥijja became sacred only on the announcement date. The announcement may have taken place during a month that was already sacred. On that reading, the four full calendar months remain Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I. The partial-month count and the proposed annual sequence answer different questions: one measures the amnesty from its announcement; the other identifies the months in the calendar.

The count still places the period across Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I: calendar positions **12–1–2–3**. Al-Marzūqī later gives the same reckoning in Abū ʿUbayda’s name and contrasts it with the later list.[^7]

A separate report attributed to ʿUmar gives a similar post-pilgrimage sequence, but it is not a list of sacred months. Ibn Abī Shayba records that pilgrims and those for whom they pray are forgiven through the remainder of Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and ten days of Rabīʿ I. Musaddad preserves substantially the same wording through another chain.[^8] The report is further evidence of a religiously significant period extending from Dhū al-Ḥijja into Rabīʿ I.

Later commentators often separated the Q 9:2 amnesty from the annual sacred season. Some explained that those months were called sacred because fighting the polytheists was temporarily forbidden in them.[^9] Al-Naḥḥās preserves Qatāda explicitly distinguishing the travel period from “the sacred months.”[^10] Al-Suddī gives the 12–1–2–3 count for Q 9:2–5 but elsewhere gives the received list for Q 9:36; al-Ṭabarī also interprets Q 9:5 differently.[^11][^12]

This disagreement matters. Commentators had to account for both the four-month proclamation reaching through Ṣafar and Rabīʿ I and the received annual list of 11–12–1–7. Some treated them as two different sets of months. The article asks whether the Qurʾān itself requires that separation, or whether it arose to reconcile the passage with the received list.

### Sacred time protected travel and pilgrimage

Even the later tradition explains sacred months in terms of pilgrimage and safe travel. Ibn Kathīr describes the received list this way:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Kathīr" source="Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm, Ibn Kathīr, 4:148">

“The sacred months were four, three in a row and one alone, for the performance of the rites of ḥajj and ʿumra ... and after [Dhū al-Ḥijja] another month was made sacred, al-Muḥarram, so that they might return in it to the farthest of their lands in safety.”[^13]

«<Arabic>وإنما كانت الأشهر المحرمة أربعة، ثلاثة سَرْدٌ وواحد فرد؛ لأجل أداء مناسك الحج والعمرة... وحرم بعده شهر آخر، وهو المحرم؛ ليرجعوا فيه إلى نائي أقصى بلادهم آمنين</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

Ibn Kathīr gives the traditional list, but his explanation is about its purpose: sacred time protects pilgrimage and the journey home.[^14] A report about the ʿAbd al-Qays delegation makes the point concrete. The delegation says hostile Muḍar tribes block the route to Medina, so they can reach the Prophet only **“in the sacred month”** (*illā fī al-shahr al-ḥarām*). Whatever the exact wording’s historicity, the report shows sacred time understood as a safe window for travel through dangerous territory.[^15]

Non-Muslim sources offer comparable examples, though they do not identify Mecca’s calendar. Nonnosus, a sixth-century Byzantine envoy quoted by Photius, describes Arab groups gathering at a sacred place twice a year. During those gatherings, **“complete peace”** prevailed with both participants and their neighbors.[^16] Procopius reports that some Saracens set aside about two months for God and did not raid during that time.[^17] These accounts do not name the Qurʾān’s four months. They show only that Arabian sacred time could protect gatherings and suspend violence.

An Old South Arabian inscription offers another comparison. CIH 547 describes a religious obligation postponed after warfare disrupted its proper timing. François de Blois notes its use of the same *n-s-ʾ* word family and its links among pilgrimage, a set time, postponement, and divine disapproval.[^18] This makes postponing a calendrically fixed religious event an Arabian practice, not just an idea found in later Muslim accounts.

Inscriptions also tie pilgrimage to **named calendar periods**. At the South Arabian Awām temple, inscriptions connect Almaqah’s pilgrimage festival with Dhū-Abhī and date sacrifices to specific days; some refer to “the festival of Abhī.”[^19] A Safaitic inscription, WH 1771, describes ritual cleansing during a calendar period in preparation for *ḥg*, or pilgrimage.[^20] Neither calendar should be equated with Mecca’s. Both show that pilgrimage could take place in known calendar periods, as Q 2:197 says.

These comparisons make the Qurʾānic structure historically plausible, but they do not identify its four months. They show that sacred places, gatherings, fixed dates, safe travel, and pauses in fighting could belong to one Arabian institution.[^21]

### Reports describe month names shifting

The next question is how early reports explain *nasīʾ*. One report describes month names shifting by one place. Its chain of transmitters also lets us ask how far back that explanation can be traced.

In the surviving *Tafsīr Mujāhid*, a report passed through **Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid** connects Q 2:197’s phrase “no disputing in the ḥajj” with an end to calendar uncertainty:

<QuoteBlock author="Tafsīr Mujāhid" source="Tafsīr, Mujāhid ibn Jabr, p. 229, on Q 2:197 (Abū al-Nīl ed.)">

“It is no longer a month that is postponed; the ḥajj has been made clear in it, and there is no doubt about it ... In the Jāhiliyya they used to drop al-Muḥarram and say, ‘Ṣafar for Ṣafar,’ and they would drop Rabīʿ al-Awwal ...”[^22]

«<Arabic>لَيْسَ هُوَ شَهْرًا يُنْسَأُ، قَدْ بُيِّنَ الْحَجُّ فِيهِ، وَلَا شَكَّ فِيهِ ... كَانُوا فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ يُسْقِطُونَ الْمُحَرَّمَ، وَيَقُولُونَ: صَفَرٌ بِصَفَرٍ، وَيُسْقِطُونَ شَهْرَ رَبِيعٍ الْأَوَّلِ</Arabic>...»

</QuoteBlock>

Ibn Abī Shayba preserves a second route, **Sufyān b. ʿUyayna → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**, for the shorter claim that ḥajj had once taken place outside Dhū al-Ḥijja but was later fixed in that month.[^23] Two students of Ibn Abī Najīḥ therefore transmit this basic explanation.

The more detailed version appears in ʿAbd al-Razzāq’s commentary on Q 9:37: **Maʿmar → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**.

and gives the crucial sequence:

<QuoteBlock author="ʿAbd al-Razzāq" source="Tafsīr, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī, 2:149, no. 1085 (ʿAbduh ed.)">

“Then they would call Dhū al-Ḥijja ‘Dhū al-Qaʿda,’ and then they would call al-Muḥarram ‘Dhū al-Ḥijja.’”[^24]

«<Arabic>ثُمَّ يُسَمُّونَ ذَا الْحِجَّةِ ذَا الْقَعْدَةِ، ثُمَّ يُسَمُّونَ الْمُحَرَّمَ ذَا الْحِجَّةِ</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

The same report continues by saying that pilgrimage moved through the named months, two years at a time, until Abū Bakr's pilgrimage fell in Dhū al-Qaʿda and the Prophet's pilgrimage in the following year fell in Dhū al-Ḥijja, at which point “time had returned to its form.”[^25]

This wording does not depend on Maʿmar alone. Ibn Abī Ḥātim preserves it through another route: **Shabāba → Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**.

with the same sequence:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Abī Ḥātim" source="Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm, Ibn Abī Ḥātim, 6:1795, no. 10021">

“They would call Dhū al-Ḥijja ‘Dhū al-Qaʿda,’ then call al-Muḥarram ‘Dhū al-Ḥijja.’”[^26]

«<Arabic>يَقُولُونَ لِذِي الْحِجَّةِ: ذُو الْقَعْدَةِ ثُمَّ يَقُولُونَ لِلْمُحَرَّمِ: ذُو الْحِجَّةِ</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

In transmission analysis, a **common link** is the earliest transmitter shared by multiple surviving branches. Here, the detailed account reaches us through students of **Ibn Abī Najīḥ**, including Maʿmar and Warqāʾ; Ibn ʿUyayna preserves the shorter explanation. The relabeling is therefore securely visible in the Ibn Abī Najīḥ transmission layer. The chains do not prove that Mujāhid himself gave the full account.

Another route, **Layth b. Abī Sulaym → Mujāhid**, describes *nasīʾ* as shifting sacred status between al-Muḥarram and Ṣafar.[^27] It does not contain the full Dhū al-Ḥijja-to-Dhū al-Qaʿda relabeling, so it cannot place that exact wording before Ibn Abī Najīḥ. It does show that a month-status shift was also associated with Mujāhid through a separate route.

Other early commentaries describe month displacement without relying on Ibn Abī Najīḥ. Sufyān al-Thawrī transmits from **Manṣūr → Abū Wāʾil** that the *nāsīʾ* made al-Muḥarram Ṣafar. Muqātil says that in one year Ṣafar became sacred and al-Muḥarram open to fighting, then al-Muḥarram’s status was restored the next year.[^28] These reports do not independently confirm the full 12→11 sequence. They do show that shifting status between al-Muḥarram and Ṣafar was an early explanation of *nasīʾ*.

The proposed original contiguous triple inside the Qurʾānic four is: **12 → 1 → 2**
The traditional contiguous triple is: **11 → 12 → 1**

The Ibn Abī Najīḥ report gives the first two steps of this one-month shift:

**actual 12 → called 11**  
**actual 1 → called 12**

The report never says that this shift changed the sacred list from 12–1–2 to 11–12–1. It offers a possible mechanism, not an explicit decree or proof that this change happened.

## Why the familiar list may differ

### The Farewell Sermon preserves the received list

The familiar list appears in versions of the Farewell Sermon. Its best-known form reaches us through Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī (d. 131/748), Ibn Sīrīn (d. 110/728), and Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock source="Jāmiʿ al-bayān, al-Ṭabarī, 14:235, no. 16686">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“Time has come round to its form on the day God created the heavens and the earth. The year is twelve months, four of them sacred, three in succession, Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja and al-Muḥarram, and Rajab of Muḍar, which is between Jumādā and Shaʿbān.”[^29]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

<Arabic>إِنَّ الزَّمَانَ قَدِ اسْتَدَارَ كَهَيْئَتِهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ اللَّهُ السَّمَوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ، السَّنَةُ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا، مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ، ثَلَاثَةٌ مُتَوَالِيَاتٌ: ذُو الْقَعْدَةِ وَذُو الْحِجَّةِ وَالْمُحَرَّمُ، وَرَجَبُ مُضَرَ الَّذِي بَيْنَ جُمَادَى وَشَعْبَانَ</Arabic>

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Bukhārī, Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī preserve this passage, with a small variation in al-Nasāʾī.[^30] Their versions are not four independent witnesses: they largely follow the same **Ayyūb → Ibn Sīrīn** chain.[^31][^32]

Al-Ṭabarī preserves another branch: **Ashʿath b. ʿAbd al-Malik → Ibn Sīrīn → Abū Hurayra**. It gives the same pattern—four sacred months, three in a row, and Rajab of Muḍar—while the **Ayyūb → Ibn Sīrīn → Abū Bakra** branch names the three months.[^33][^34] Because both routes pass through Ibn Sīrīn but diverge below him and name different Companions, **Ibn Sīrīn is the common link** in this sermon family, not Ayyūb. Ayyūb remains the main transmitter of the complete list found in the canonical collections.

Not every version gives the list. Some preserve only “time has come round to its form.”[^35] In one version in Aḥmad, the Prophet recites Q 9:36 only through “four of them are sacred” and names no months.[^36] Ibn ʿAwn and Qurra also transmit Farewell-Sermon material without the calendar passage.[^37][^38] Even so, the **11–12–1+7** list appears in more than one literary setting by the middle of the second century AH.

### The evidence for Dhū al-Qaʿda and Hudaybiyya

The strongest traditional argument for including Dhū al-Qaʿda is Q 2:194:

<QuranPassage verse="2:194">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The sacred month for the sacred month.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

**<Arabic>الشَّهْرُ الْحَرَامُ بِالشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ</Arabic>**

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Early commentators connect Q 2:194 with the Hudaybiyya agreement and the later return to Mecca. In the surviving *Tafsīr Mujāhid*, **Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid** says that Quraysh boasted of turning the Prophet back at Hudaybiyya in Dhū al-Qaʿda, and that he returned to Mecca in that month the following year.[^39] Al-Ṭabarī preserves two students, **ʿĪsā** and **Shibl**, transmitting this interpretation from **Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**. That makes Ibn Abī Najīḥ the common link for this version.[^40]

Qatāda’s reports form a separate group. **Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba → Qatāda** names Dhū al-Qaʿda for both the blocking and the return; **Maʿmar → Qatāda** preserves the same logic in the more general phrase “a sacred month for a sacred month.”[^41] Al-Suddī also names Dhū al-Qaʿda. Miqsam and ʿAṭāʾ/Ibn Jurayj connect the verse with Hudaybiyya and reciprocal sacred time, though they do not always name the month.[^42] So the evidence supports a careful distinction: **many reports connect the verse with Hudaybiyya, but the explicit Dhū al-Qaʿda date is strongest in particular branches, especially Ibn Abī Najīḥ/Mujāhid and Saʿīd/Qatāda.**

But the earliest recoverable accounts do not all agree on the month. Hishām b. ʿUrwa transmits from his father, ʿUrwa:

<HadithBlock source="al-Muṣannaf, Ibn Abī Shayba, no. 37995">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“The Messenger of God went out to Hudaybiyya, and Hudaybiyya was in Shawwāl.”[^43]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

«<Arabic>خَرَجَ رَسُولُ اللهِ ﷺ إِلَى الْحُدَيْبِيَةِ، وَكَانَتِ الْحُدَيْبِيَةُ فِي شَوَّالٍ</Arabic>»

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Andreas Görke’s study of the report’s wording and transmission identifies Hishām b. ʿUrwa and al-Zuhrī as two independent branches for reconstructing ʿUrwa’s account. Görke notes that Hishām places the event in Shawwāl, while later accounts usually place it in Dhū al-Qaʿda.[^44] The two branches share many details—the departure, encounter with Khālid, detour, water shortage, delegates, treaty, Abū Jandal, sacrifice, and Abū Baṣīr—but not a month-date.[^45]

One version through **Ibn Lahīʿa → Abū l-Aswad → ʿUrwa** does give Dhū al-Qaʿda. Görke treats this version cautiously because it contains substantial material absent from the other ʿUrwa reports. He concludes that, in its present form, it probably does not go back to ʿUrwa.[^46]

This does not prove that Hudaybiyya happened in Shawwāl. It shows the narrower point: the earliest recoverable reports do not securely establish Dhū al-Qaʿda as the date. The story of the event and the assignment of its month have different transmission histories.

The well-known list of the Prophet’s ʿumras largely comes through the **Hammām → Qatāda → Anas** transmission family.[^47] Other reports say pre-Islamic Arabs considered ʿumra during the ḥajj months a grave violation. The Prophet’s ʿumra in Dhū al-Qaʿda could therefore mark the removal of that restriction; by itself, it does not prove that Dhū al-Qaʿda belonged to Q 9:36’s original four.

The earliest recoverable Hudaybiyya reports do not give a stable Dhū al-Qaʿda date, and the strongest independent evidence for that date passes through weak or unclear links. Beside the Ibn Abī Najīḥ *nasīʾ* report—in which **Dhū al-Ḥijja is called Dhū al-Qaʿda**—this leaves open the possibility that month 11 reflects the same month-displacement problem. It is not an uncontested anchor against that explanation.

### Rajab had its own pre-Islamic sacred status

Rajab needs separate treatment. Its importance in pre-Islamic Arabia is well attested. The question is whether that independent sanctity proves that Rajab was one of the four months in Q 9:36.

Rajab already had a distinct religious and social role before the familiar list became standard. Kister describes it as a pre-Islamic holy month associated with **ʿumra, sacrifice to pagan deities, and pauses in raids and warfare**. Later scholarship also connects Rajab with pilgrimage, sacrifice, seasonal gatherings, and sacred time. Mansouri notes that its many names reflect its importance and links it with fasting, sacrifice, and pilgrimage. Rajab was more than the seventh month on a calendar; it was a religious institution in its own right.[^48]

Early reports describe what that status meant in practice. Abū Rajāʾ al-ʿUṭāridī recalls people removing the iron points from their weapons when Rajab began.[^49] Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim says people called it **“the Deaf”** (*al-aṣamm*) because its sanctity silenced the sounds of conflict.[^50] These reports recall concrete practices, not just Rajab’s place in a list.

Reports about the Nakhla raid and Q 2:217 fit the same pattern. One route in Ibn Abī Ḥātim says the raiders killed Ibn al-Ḥaḍramī without knowing whether the day was still in **Jumādā** or had entered **Rajab**. Their opponents accused them of killing in a sacred month, and Q 2:217 was then revealed.[^51] Al-Suddī preserves a similar dispute: Meccans said the killing took place in Rajab, while Muslims placed it in Jumādā; another version puts it at the boundary between the two months.[^52] The episode treats the start of Rajab as significant and violence during it as scandalous. This supports Rajab’s independent inviolability in the surrounding society.

The **ʿatīra**, or **rajabiyya**, was a sacrifice associated with Rajab before Islam. In one report, people ask about the sacrifice they used to perform in Rajab, through **Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ → Abū al-Malīḥ → Nubaysha**.[^53] Other routes describe a household animal sacrificed in Rajab and use the same names.[^54] Mansouri also discusses the pre-Islamic Rajab sacrifice, including accounts of animals offered to idols. Whatever one concludes about each detail, the evidence shows that **Rajab had its own sacrificial vocabulary and ritual cycle**.[^55]

Rajab’s prestige continued after Islam began. Kister notes that later reports both promoted and restricted Rajab practices. Some authorities tried to end or limit the old sacrifice; others permitted or continued it. The disagreement suggests that the new community inherited a month with established ritual authority.[^56]

Rajab also served as a recurring administrative date. One treaty requires a payment **“in every Rajab,”** and the Najrān material names it as one of two annual delivery dates.[^57] This shows Rajab had social and calendar importance of its own, whether or not it belonged to a continuous four-month block.

This context also matters for Ibn Sīrīn, who transmits an influential version of the Farewell-Sermon list. Legal sources remember him as continuing the **ʿatīra in Rajab**.[^58] Other authorities pushed back against Rajab’s special status. A report attributed to ʿUmar says: "I saw ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb striking men's hands when they lifted them away from food in Rajab, until they put them back to it, saying: 'It is only a month that the people of the Jāhiliyya used to venerate.'"[^59] Ibn al-Qayyim writes in *al-Manār al-Munīf*: "Every hadith mentioning the fasting of Rajab, or prayer on certain nights in it, is a fabricated lie."[^60]

The sources describe Ibn Sīrīn as the only jurist who continued the old Rajab sacrifice, the ʿatīra, and did not regard it as abrogated. They call him the exception to the position of "the scholars of all the regions," saying he used to "transmit something" in support of it: "...nor the ʿatīra, which is the sacrifice of Rajab — this is the position of the scholars of the [various] regions/metropolises (ʿulamāʾ al-amṣār), except for Ibn Sīrīn: for he used to slaughter the ʿatīra in Rajab, and he would narrate something concerning it." [^61]

This creates a notable overlap: Ibn Sīrīn is a key transmitter of the hadith list that includes Rajab, and he is also remembered for keeping the older Rajab sacrifice. His student Ibn ʿAwn, who transmits the same hadith, is likewise recorded performing the ʿatīra.[^62]

Rajab’s pre-Islamic sanctity does not, by itself, prove it was one of Q 9:36’s four months. On this reconstruction, Rajab’s older status existed independently and later made it a natural fit for the received **11–12–1+7** list. The later list did not create Rajab’s sanctity; it incorporated a month that was already sacred.

### The later definition of the ḥajj months does not settle the question

The traditional interpretation identifies Q 2:197’s “known months” as Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿda, and some or all of Dhū al-Ḥijja. Al-Ṭabarī preserves both a shorter count ending ten days into Dhū al-Ḥijja and one that includes the whole month.[^63] Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to say that God set these months for ḥajj and the rest of the year for ʿumra.[^64] Al-Ṭabarī prefers the shorter count because no ḥajj rite takes place after the days of Minā.[^65] Bukhārī also begins his chapter on the verse with Ibn ʿUmar’s list.[^66]

Here the Qurʾānic distinction between **months** and **days** matters. Al-Ṭabarī uses the final ritual day to define the season. But Q 2:197 says ḥajj takes place in “known months,” while Q 22:28 and Q 2:203 refer separately to its “known” or “numbered” days. The Qurʾān’s wording can allow a wider season than the ritual timetable used in later law.

The disagreement among commentators shows that the verse’s boundaries were not self-evident. Some included all of Dhū al-Ḥijja, even though the main rites had ended.[^67][^68] Q 2:197 itself names neither Shawwāl nor Dhū al-Qaʿda, and it does not say the pilgrimage months are separate from the sacred months. The familiar list is an interpretation of the verse, not a list found in its wording.


## Conclusion

The Qurʾān names four sacred months but does not identify them. This article’s reconstruction rests on reading Q 9:1–5 as one continuous pilgrimage-linked period, then comparing that reading with early commentary and reports about shifting month names.

<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">

The Qurʾān does not name its four sacred months, so **12–1–2–3 remains a historical reconstruction**, not an explicit Qurʾānic list. The article argues that this sequence best fits the pilgrimage-linked four-month period in Q 9:1–5 and the early count attributed to al-Suddī and other commentators. Reports associated with Ibn Abī Najīḥ describe month-name shifts that could help explain the later **11–12–1+7** list, while evidence for Rajab’s separate pre-Islamic sanctity helps explain why it remained important. Together, these points make calendrical displacement and later interpretation a possible explanation for the received list; they do not preserve a direct decree announcing that change.

</VerdictBox>
