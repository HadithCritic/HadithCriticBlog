<ClaimBox title="Central thesis">

The Qurʾān states that God established twelve months and made four of them sacred. Islamic tradition identifies those four as Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, and Rajab (the 11th, 12th, 1st, and 7th months of the Hijri calendar).[^1] This article argues that this familiar enumeration displaced an earlier, pilgrimage-centered sequence: Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I, or months **12–1–2–3**.

</ClaimBox>

## Part I — The Qurʾānic Argument

### 1. Four sacred months fixed by God

The starting point is Q 9:36:

<QuranPassage verse="9:36">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The number of months with God is twelve in God's decree, since the day He created the heavens and the earth; four of them are sacred. That is the upright religion.”[^2]

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>إِنَّ عِدَّةَ الشُّهُورِ عِندَ اللَّهِ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا فِي كِتَابِ اللَّهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ ذَٰلِكَ الدِّينُ الْقَيِّمُ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The verse gives three pieces of information. (1) The year consists of twelve months; (2) four of them possess a sacred status; (3) and this arrangement belongs to the divinely established order. What it does **not** do is name those four months.

The next verse is just as important. Q 9:37 condemns *nasīʾ*, the postponement or displacement of sacred time, because people manipulated which month carried the prohibition while still attempting “to match the number that God made sacred.” The problem, then, was not that people sometimes observed fewer or more than four months. They would preserve the **number four** while corrupting the **placement** of the sanctity.

This immediately establishes an important principle. Q 9:36–37 is concerned with a God-given calendrical order, not an abstract quota of any four months. A later tradition that supplies four names therefore cannot be assumed to reproduce the original arrangement simply because it also totals four. The location of the sacred period is precisely what Q 9:37 says could be manipulated.

### 2. Q 9:1–5 reads as one continuous proclamation

The clearest Qurʾānic clue to the placement of the sacred period appears at the beginning of the same sūra. Q 9:1 opens with a declaration concerning those polytheists with whom the believers had agreements. Verse 2 then grants a defined period of movement:

<QuranPassage verse="9:2">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“So travel freely in the land for four months.” (Q 9:2)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>فَسِيحُوا فِي الْأَرْضِ أَرْبَعَةَ أَشْهُرٍ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The audience is not immediately attacked. It receives **four months** of security and freedom of movement. Verse 3 then tells us the setting in which the proclamation is publicly announced:

<QuranPassage verse="9:3">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“A proclamation from God and His messenger to the people on the Day of the Greater Pilgrimage...” (Q 9:3)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>وَأَذَانٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦٓ إِلَى ٱلنَّاسِ يَوْمَ ٱلْحَجِّ ٱلْأَكْبَرِ …</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The four-month announcement is therefore embedded directly in the pilgrimage. Its public chronological anchor falls in Dhū al-Ḥijja, the month in which the Greater Pilgrimage is occurring. Verse 4 then makes clear that the proclamation does not cancel agreements with those who remain peaceful:

<QuranPassage verse="9:4">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“Except those of the polytheists with whom you made a treaty and who thereafter have failed you in nothing and supported no one against you; fulfill their treaty with them until its term. God loves the righteous.” (Q 9:4)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ عَٰهَدتُّم مِّنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَنقُصُوكُمْ شَيْـًٔا وَلَمْ يُظَٰهِرُوا۟ عَلَيْكُمْ أَحَدًا فَأَتِمُّوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِمْ عَهْدَهُمْ إِلَىٰ مُدَّتِهِمْ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Then comes verse 5:

<QuranPassage verse="9:5">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“When the sacred months have passed...” (Q 9:5)

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>فَإِذَا انسَلَخَ الْأَشْهُرُ الْحُرُمُ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

- Verse 2 introduces **four months**. 
- Verse 3 locates their proclamation at the pilgrimage. 
- Verse 4 preserves treaties with those who honor them. 
- Verse 5 then speaks of the expiration of **“the sacred months.”** 

No second set of months has been introduced between verses 2 and 5. The most economical reading informs us the pilgrims are granted four months of protected movement; the announcement is made at the pilgrimage; valid treaties remain binding; and when **these 4 sacred months** have elapsed, the special calendrical prohibition no longer prevents hostilities against those who remain in a state of conflict.

The Qurʾān therefore gives us something Q 9:36 alone does not: a **continuous four-month period attached to the pilgrimage** and described as “the sacred months.” The Qurʾān nowhere depicts the four sacred months as three consecutive months plus an isolated month elsewhere in the year as the Islamic tradition advocates. That 3+1 geometry enters only with the later enumeration.

### 3. Why the four months are peaceful: Q 2:217

The legal logic behind Q 9:2–5 is supplied elsewhere in the Qurʾān. Q 2:217 says:

<QuranPassage verse="2:217">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“They ask you about the sacred month, about fighting in it. Say: fighting in it is a grave matter.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلشَّهْرِ ٱلْحَرَامِ قِتَالٍ فِيهِ ۖ قُلْ قِتَالٌ فِيهِ كَبِيرٌ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The point is not dependent on identifying which named month stands behind the verse. From the Qurʾānic wording alone, fighting during sacred time is a serious violation. That principle explains the structure of the proclamation in Sūrah 9. If the four months of Q 9:2 are the sacred period, then the freedom to “travel freely in the land” is not an arbitrary grace period. It follows from the sanctity of the time itself. Hostilities are restrained during the sacred period. Verse 5 then resumes the discussion at the exact point when that restraint ends: **“when the sacred months have passed.”**

The sequence therefore has an internal legal coherence:

- sacred time prohibits or gravely restricts fighting (Q 2:217);
- hostile polytheists are granted four months of free movement (Q 9:2);
- the announcement is made during the pilgrimage (Q 9:3);
- peaceful treaties remain binding (Q 9:4);
- the special prohibition associated with the sacred period expires only after “the sacred months” have passed (Q 9:5).

The four-month period is thus intelligible as **protected sacred time**, not just as a negotiated ceasefire detached from the calendar.

### 4. Sacred time belongs to the pilgrimage system

Two passages in Sūrah 5 place “the sacred month” directly inside the institution of pilgrimage.

Q 5:2 commands:

<QuranPassage verse="5:2">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“Do not violate God's rites, nor the sacred month, nor the offerings, nor the garlands, nor those heading toward the Sacred House...”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>لَا تُحِلُّوا۟ شَعَـٰٓئِرَ ٱللَّهِ وَلَا ٱلشَّهْرَ ٱلْحَرَامَ وَلَا ٱلْهَدْىَ وَلَا ٱلْقَلَـٰٓئِدَ وَلَآ ءَآمِّينَ ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْحَرَامَ …</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The sacred month appears beside God's rites, sacrificial offerings, garlands, and **people travelling toward the Sacred House**. Sacred time is not mentioned as an isolated taboo. It is part of the system that protects pilgrimage, its offerings, and the movement of those approaching the sanctuary.

Q 5:97 makes the connection even more explicit:

<QuranPassage verse="5:97">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“God has made the Kaʿba, the Sacred House, a support for mankind, and the sacred month, the offerings, and the garlands.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْكَعْبَةَ ٱلْبَيْتَ ٱلْحَرَامَ قِيَـٰمًا لِّلنَّاسِ وَٱلشَّهْرَ ٱلْحَرَامَ وَٱلْهَدْىَ وَٱلْقَلَـٰٓئِدَ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Sacred **space** and sacred **time** stand side by side. The Kaʿba and the sacred month belong to the same structure, together with the offerings and garlands of pilgrimage. Other verses describe the sanctuary itself as a zone of security. 

- Q 2:125 calls the House a place of return and security. 
- Q 3:97 says that whoever enters it is safe. 
- Q 29:67 speaks of God establishing a secure sanctuary while people are seized around it. 

Read beside Q 5:2 and 5:97, the conceptual relationship demonstrates the Sacred House provides protected **space**, while the sacred month provides protected **time**. The journey toward the House requires both. This is particularly relevant to the phrase “travel freely in the land” in Q 9:2. The protected institution is not limited to standing inside Mecca on the ritual days. Pilgrimage involves movement toward the House, participation in its rites, movement away from it, etc. Q 5:2 explicitly protects “those heading toward the Sacred House.” The sacred period therefore makes sense as a broader season of protected movement around the pilgrimage.

### 5. The Qurʾān distinguishes the months of ḥajj from its ritual days

Q 2:197 states:

<QuranPassage verse="2:197">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The ḥajj is during known months.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>الْحَجُّ أَشْهُرٌ مَعْلُومَاتٌ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The verse gives no names and no number. Yet it deliberately speaks of **months**. Elsewhere, when the Qurʾān refers to the concentrated ritual period of pilgrimage, it speaks instead of **days**. Q 22:28 tells the pilgrims to remember God “during known days” (*ayyāmin maʿlūmāt*), while Q 2:203 tells them to remember God “during numbered days” (*ayyāmin maʿdūdāt*).

The Qurʾān knows how to describe the rites in terms of days, but it describes the encompassing time of the ḥajj in terms of months. The broader “known months” of Q 2:197 therefore should not automatically be collapsed into the few days on which the central rites at ʿArafāt, Muzdalifa, and Minā are performed.

This becomes important when later exegetes define the pilgrimage months by asking when the rites cease. That reasoning reverses the Qurʾānic distinction. If the text itself differentiates the **months of ḥajj** from the **days of its rites**, then the absence of a ritual on a particular later day does not by itself prove that the larger pilgrimage season has ended.

The Qurʾān also never states that the “known months” of ḥajj are a category separate from the sacred months. That separation is supplied by later islamic exegetical interpretation. When Q 2:197 is read together with Q 5:2, Q 5:97, and Q 9:1–5, it becomes clear that the months of pilgrimage and the sacred months are two descriptions of the same larger institution. One describing its pilgrimage function, and the other its protected legal status.

### 6. The lunar calendar itself is tied to pilgrimage

Q 2:189 adds another piece:

<QuranPassage verse="2:189">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“They ask you about the crescents. Say: they are measurements of time for mankind and for the ḥajj.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

<Arabic>يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلْأَهِلَّةِ ۖ قُلْ هِىَ مَوَٰقِيتُ لِلنَّاسِ وَٱلْحَجِّ</Arabic>

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The Qurʾān singles out pilgrimage when explaining the function of the lunar crescents. This is significant beside Q 9:36. The lunar calendar is not an incidental background to pilgrimage; the regulation of ḥajj is one of the reasons the phases of the moon function as calendrical markers at all.

The relationship is therefore consistent across the Qurʾān:

- the lunar crescents regulate time and the ḥajj (Q 2:189);
- the ḥajj occupies known **months** (Q 2:197);
- its concentrated rites occupy known or numbered **days** (Q 22:28; 2:203);
- the sacred month protects the rites, offerings, and those travelling toward the Sacred House (Q 5:2);
- the Sacred House and sacred time belong to one institutional system (Q 5:97);
- four months of protected movement are proclaimed at the Greater Pilgrimage and their expiration is called the expiration of “the sacred months” (Q 9:2–5);
- the annual order contains exactly four sacred months whose placement is not to be manipulated (Q 9:36–37).

The cumulative picture shows us that the sacred months have to be a **continuous sacred pilgrimage season**.

### 7. What the Qurʾān establishes—and what it does not

The Qurʾān does not explicitly say, “the sacred months are Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I.” It supplies neither the month names nor numerical labels such as 12–1–2–3. It doesn't need to.

What the Qurʾān establishes is the underlying structure:

1. there are four sacred months in a divinely fixed calendar;
2. altering their placement while preserving the number is condemned;
3. the only continuous four-month period narrated in the relevant context is proclaimed at the pilgrimage;
4. its expiration is called the expiration of “the sacred months”;
5. sacred time elsewhere is repeatedly integrated with the Sacred House, offerings, pilgrims, and the journey to ḥajj;
6. the ḥajj itself occupies “known months,” broader than the ritual “days.”

The Qurʾānic depiction of the sacred months is therefore a continuous four-month sacred season centered on the pilgrimage. Because the proclamation is anchored in Dhū al-Ḥijja, the natural whole-month sequence to test historically is:

**Dhū al-Ḥijja → al-Muḥarram → Ṣafar → Rabīʿ I**  
**12 → 1 → 2 → 3**

The historical question is whether the earliest commentary and calendar traditions preserve evidence for precisely that sequence.

## Part II — The Historical Reconstruction

### 8. Early interpretation of Q 9:5 preserves the 12–1–2–3 sequence

The early commentary on Q 9:2–5 provides the strongest bridge from the Qurʾānic structure to named months. Five early authorities preserved by al-Ṭabarī identify the period that expires at Q 9:5 with the four months introduced in Q 9:2.[^3] Al-Suddī gives the fullest count, beginning from the Day of Sacrifice in Dhū al-Ḥijja:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Suddī" source="Jāmiʿ al-bayān, al-Ṭabarī, 14:136, no. 16477 (al-Suddī report)">

“‘When the sacred months have passed.’ These are the four I listed for you, that is, twenty [days] of Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, Rabīʿ al-Awwal, and ten of Rabīʿ al-Ākhir.”[^4]

«(<Arabic>فإذا انسلخ الأشهر الحرم</Arabic>)<Arabic>، وهي الأربعة التي عددت لك</Arabic> = <Arabic>يعني: عشرين من ذي الحجة، والمحرم، وصفر، وربيعًا الأول، وعشرًا من شهر ربيع الآخر</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

Mujāhid, ʿAmr b. Shuʿayb, Ibn Zayd, and Ibn Isḥāq also treat the four-month period of the proclamation as the period whose expiration is described at Q 9:5.[^5] Ibn Kathīr later explains the partial first month by saying that the terms became known to the audience on the Day of Sacrifice.[^6]

This count must be handled carefully. It does not mean that Rabīʿ II becomes a fifth sacred month. The four-month amnesty is being measured from a public announcement made partway through Dhū al-Ḥijja. Counting four exact elapsed months from that announcement naturally runs into the first part of Rabīʿ II. That is a treaty clock, not a definition of five annual sacred months. The fact that the proclamation was made partway through Dhū al-Ḥijja does not require the sacred status of that month itself to have begun only on the day of the announcement. Al-Suddī’s reckoning appears to synchronize the four-month period with the date on which the proclamation became operative, counting twenty days of Dhū al-Ḥijja and then continuing until ten days into Rabīʿ II. But that is not the only way to understand the relationship between the proclamation and the sacred months. The proclamation may simply have occurred during an already sacred Dhū al-Ḥijja. On that reading, the four sacred months themselves remain four complete calendrical months—Dhū al-Ḥijja, Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ al-Awwal. There is therefore no necessity to treat the first ten days of Rabīʿ II as part of the sacred-month sequence merely because an elapsed four-month amnesty beginning late in Dhū al-Ḥijja extends into that month.

The underlying sequence preserved by the count is nevertheless unmistakable: the period begins inside Dhū al-Ḥijja and then runs through al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I. The four complete calendar positions involved are therefore **12–1–2–3**. Al-Marzūqī later transmits the same reckoning in the name of Abū ʿUbayda and explicitly contrasts it with the later Islamic enumeration.[^7]

A separate report attributed to ʿUmar preserves a similar post-ḥajj temporal sequence, although it should not be confused with a sacred-month list. Ibn Abī Shayba records on the authority of 'Umar: “The pilgrim, and whoever the pilgrim seeks forgiveness for, is forgiven for the remainder of Dhū al-Ḥijja, al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and ten days of Rabīʿ I.” Musaddad preserves substantially the same wording through Ḥammād b. Zayd → Layth b. Abī Sulaym → al-Muhājir → ʿUmar.[^8] The report gives us an early pilgrimage-related tradition again extends a religiously significant post-ḥajj period from Dhū al-Ḥijja through al-Muḥarram and Ṣafar into Rabīʿ I.

Later commentators often resisted identifying the Q 9:2 amnesty with the annual sacred season. Some said those months were called ḥurum only because Muslim bloodshed against the polytheists was temporarily forbidden in them.[^9] Al-Naḥḥās preserves Qatāda explicitly distinguishing the travel months from “the sacred months.”[^10] Al-Suddī himself can count the Q 9:2–5 period through Rabīʿ I while elsewhere giving the received four at Q 9:36,[^11] and al-Ṭabarī ultimately interprets Q 9:5 differently.[^12]

That later separation is historically important, but it does not erase the earlier reading. It shows that interpreters inherited two pieces of material that did not sit comfortably together: a four-month pilgrimage proclamation reaching through Ṣafar and Rabīʿ I, and a received annual list of 11–12–1–7. The later exegetical solution was to declare the two four-month sets different. This article asks whether that separation reflects the Qurʾān itself or a harmonization demanded by the later list.

### 9. Sacred time as protection for travel, pilgrimage, and assembly

The later Islamic tradition itself preserves the functional logic behind a pilgrimage-centered sacred season. Ibn Kathīr explains the received sacred months this way:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Kathīr" source="Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm, Ibn Kathīr, 4:148">

“The sacred months were four, three in a row and one alone, for the performance of the rites of ḥajj and ʿumra ... and after [Dhū al-Ḥijja] another month was made sacred, al-Muḥarram, so that they might return in it to the farthest of their lands in safety.”[^13]

«<Arabic>وإنما كانت الأشهر المحرمة أربعة، ثلاثة سَرْدٌ وواحد فرد؛ لأجل أداء مناسك الحج والعمرة... وحرم بعده شهر آخر، وهو المحرم؛ ليرجعوا فيه إلى نائي أقصى بلادهم آمنين</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

His enumeration is the traditional one, but the rationale is more general. He states the sacred time protects pilgrimage and the movement of pilgrims, including the journey home.[^14] The ʿAbd al-Qays delegation tradition preserves the same understanding in an especially concrete form. The delegation is made to say that hostile Muḍar tribes stand between them and Medina and that they can reach the Prophet only **“in the sacred month”** (*illā fī al-shahr al-ḥarām*). Whatever one concludes about the historicity of the delegation's exact wording, the report shows that early Muslims understood sacred time as creating a corridor of safe movement through otherwise dangerous tribal territory.[^15]

This logic is not confined to Muslim literary memory. Nonnosus, a sixth-century Byzantine envoy whose account survives through Photius, describes Arab groups gathering at a sacred place twice each year, once for about a month and once for two months. During these gatherings, he says, **“complete peace”** prevailed not only among the participants, but also with the surrounding population.[^16] Procopius likewise reports that certain Saracens (arabs) observed roughly two months dedicated to God during which they undertook no raids into foreign territory.[^17] Neither author is describing Mecca's calendar, and neither identify the Qurʾān's four months. Their importance is narrower: before Islam, Arabian sacred time could be tied to religious gathering and could suspend ordinary raiding and hostility.

Old South Arabian evidence supplies another useful comparison. CIH 547 describes a cultic obligation that was postponed after warfare interfered with its proper timing; François de Blois drew attention to its use of the same *n-s-ʾ* semantic field and to its combination of pilgrimage, specified sacred time, postponement, and divine disapproval.[^18] This demonstrates that postponement of a calendrically fixed cultic obligation was an indigenous Arabian phenomenon rather than an institution that must be reconstructed solely from late Muslim prose.

Arabian epigraphy also shows pilgrimage being attached to **named calendrical periods**. In the South Arabian Awām-temple corpus, the prominent pilgrimage-festival of Almaqah is associated with the month Dhū-Abhī, with sacrifice inscriptions dated to particular days of that month; some inscriptions can refer simply to “the festival of Abhī.”[^19] In the Safaitic corpus, WH 1771 describes a person ritually cleansing during a stellar/calendrical period in order to perform *ḥg*, pilgrimage.[^20] Neither system should be mapped onto the Meccan calendar, but both provide material parallels for the Qurʾānic assumption in Q 2:197 that pilgrimage belongs to “known months” rather than floating free of calendrical time.

These external parallels matter because they establish the historical plausibility of the Qurʾānic structure without deciding the identity of the four months. Sacred place, pilgrimage or assembly, fixed sacred time, safe movement, and suspension of violence formed a recognizable Arabian institutional complex.[^21]

### 10. Nasīʾ and the Ibn Abī Najīḥ common-link tradition

The most important historical development since the initial reconstruction concerns the transmission of the *nasīʾ* material itself. An early exegetical transmission preserves an explicit **one-position relabeling of the month names**, and its isnād can be analyzed.

The extant *Tafsīr Mujāhid* recension transmits through **Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid** an explanation of Q 2:197 in which the end of calendrical uncertainty is part of the meaning of “no disputing in the ḥajj”:

<QuoteBlock author="Tafsīr Mujāhid" source="Tafsīr, Mujāhid ibn Jabr, p. 229, on Q 2:197 (Abū al-Nīl ed.)">

“It is no longer a month that is postponed; the ḥajj has been made clear in it, and there is no doubt about it ... In the Jāhiliyya they used to drop al-Muḥarram and say, ‘Ṣafar for Ṣafar,’ and they would drop Rabīʿ al-Awwal ...”[^22]

«<Arabic>لَيْسَ هُوَ شَهْرًا يُنْسَأُ، قَدْ بُيِّنَ الْحَجُّ فِيهِ، وَلَا شَكَّ فِيهِ ... كَانُوا فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ يُسْقِطُونَ الْمُحَرَّمَ، وَيَقُولُونَ: صَفَرٌ بِصَفَرٍ، وَيُسْقِطُونَ شَهْرَ رَبِيعٍ الْأَوَّلِ</Arabic>...»

</QuoteBlock>

Ibn Abī Shayba independently preserves **Sufyān b. ʿUyayna → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid** for the shorter core claim: ḥajj had formerly occurred outside Dhū al-Ḥijja but had now become fixed in Dhū al-Ḥijja.[^23] Thus the general interpretation is already multiply transmitted below Ibn Abī Najīḥ.

More decisive is the expanded version at Q 9:37. ʿAbd al-Razzāq transmits: **Maʿmar → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**

and gives the crucial sequence:

<QuoteBlock author="ʿAbd al-Razzāq" source="Tafsīr, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī, 2:149, no. 1085 (ʿAbduh ed.)">

“Then they would call Dhū al-Ḥijja ‘Dhū al-Qaʿda,’ and then they would call al-Muḥarram ‘Dhū al-Ḥijja.’”[^24]

«<Arabic>ثُمَّ يُسَمُّونَ ذَا الْحِجَّةِ ذَا الْقَعْدَةِ، ثُمَّ يُسَمُّونَ الْمُحَرَّمَ ذَا الْحِجَّةِ</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

The same report continues by saying that pilgrimage moved through the named months, two years at a time, until Abū Bakr's pilgrimage fell in Dhū al-Qaʿda and the Prophet's pilgrimage in the following year fell in Dhū al-Ḥijja, at which point “time had returned to its form.”[^25]

This exact 12→11 wording cannot be attributed merely to Maʿmar. Ibn Abī Ḥātim independently preserves: **Shabāba → Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**

with the same sequence:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Abī Ḥātim" source="Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿaẓīm, Ibn Abī Ḥātim, 6:1795, no. 10021">

“They would call Dhū al-Ḥijja ‘Dhū al-Qaʿda,’ then call al-Muḥarram ‘Dhū al-Ḥijja.’”[^26]

«<Arabic>يَقُولُونَ لِذِي الْحِجَّةِ: ذُو الْقَعْدَةِ ثُمَّ يَقُولُونَ لِلْمُحَرَّمِ: ذُو الْحِجَّةِ</Arabic>»

</QuoteBlock>

The independent branching through **Maʿmar** and **Warqāʾ**, supplemented by the shorter **Ibn ʿUyayna** branch, makes **Ibn Abī Najīḥ the common link** of the detailed Mujāhid-associated calendrical tradition. The responsible historical conclusion demonstrates the systematic relabeling is securely visible at the Ibn Abī Najīḥ transmission layer. It should not simply be projected behind him to Mujāhid as though the isnād itself proved that attribution.

There are, however, bypass traditions that make the broader attribution to Mujāhid less artificial. **Layth b. Abī Sulaym → Mujāhid** independently describes an alternating Muḥarram/Ṣafar *nasīʾ* in which the sacred designation moves between the two months.[^27] That bypass does not reproduce the full Dhū al-Ḥijja→Dhū al-Qaʿda sequence, so it cannot move the exact matn behind the common link. It does show that Mujāhid was independently associated with a theory of *nasīʾ* involving the displacement of month-status between Muḥarram and Ṣafar.

Other early exegetical environments preserve the same broader phenomenon without passing through Ibn Abī Najīḥ. Sufyān al-Thawrī transmits from **Manṣūr → Abū Wāʾil** that the *nāsīʾ* would make Muḥarram Ṣafar, while Muqātil b. Sulaymān describes one year in which Ṣafar was made sacred and Muḥarram opened to fighting, followed by a year in which Muḥarram was restored.[^28] These are not independent witnesses to the full 12→11 model; they are independent witnesses that Muḥarram/Ṣafar displacement was an early explanatory tradition. This has a direct structural consequence. 

The proposed original contiguous triple inside the Qurʾānic four is: **12 → 1 → 2**
The traditional contiguous triple is: **11 → 12 → 1**

The Ibn Abī Najīḥ common-link tradition explicitly supplies the first two steps of exactly that one-position backward relabeling:

**actual 12 → called 11**  
**actual 1 → called 12**

The tradition never says, “this is how the sacred list 12–1–2 became 11–12–1.” It supplies a mechanism, not an explicit historical decree.

## Part III — How the Received List Entered the Interpretation

### 11. The Farewell Sermon and the early received enumeration

Only after the Qurʾānic and calendrical case has been established should the received list be introduced. The familiar enumeration appears in versions of the Farewell Sermon. The best-known form comes through Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī (d. 131/748), from Ibn Sīrīn (d. 110/728), from Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock source="Jāmiʿ al-bayān, al-Ṭabarī, 14:235, no. 16686">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“Time has come round to its form on the day God created the heavens and the earth. The year is twelve months, four of them sacred, three in succession, Dhū al-Qaʿda, Dhū al-Ḥijja and al-Muḥarram, and Rajab of Muḍar, which is between Jumādā and Shaʿbān.”[^29]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

<Arabic>إِنَّ الزَّمَانَ قَدِ اسْتَدَارَ كَهَيْئَتِهِ يَوْمَ خَلَقَ اللَّهُ السَّمَوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ، السَّنَةُ اثْنَا عَشَرَ شَهْرًا، مِنْهَا أَرْبَعَةٌ حُرُمٌ، ثَلَاثَةٌ مُتَوَالِيَاتٌ: ذُو الْقَعْدَةِ وَذُو الْحِجَّةِ وَالْمُحَرَّمُ، وَرَجَبُ مُضَرَ الَّذِي بَيْنَ جُمَادَى وَشَعْبَانَ</Arabic>

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Bukhārī, Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, and al-Nasāʾī preserve this calendar passage, with a small variation in al-Nasāʾī.[^30] Those canonical appearances should not be counted as independent witnesses to the wording: they overwhelmingly reproduce the **Ayyūb → Ibn Sīrīn** branch.[^31][^32]

The broader stemma, however, places the common link one step higher. Al-Ṭabarī preserves **Ashʿath b. ʿAbd al-Malik → Ibn Sīrīn → Abū Hurayra**, where the sermon already contains “four sacred, three consecutive, and Rajab of Muḍar between Jumādā and Shaʿbān,” alongside **Ayyūb → Ibn Sīrīn → Abū Bakra**, where the three consecutive months are explicitly named.[^33][^34] Because these branches diverge below Ibn Sīrīn and attach the saying to different Companion authorities, **Ibn Sīrīn—not Ayyūb—is the common link of the surviving Ibn Sīrīn sermon family**. Ayyūb remains the major transmitter responsible for the canonical form with the complete named triple.

The transmission history is also less uniform than the later canonical presentation suggests. Several versions preserve only “time has come round to its form” without naming the four months.[^35] A version in Aḥmad has the Prophet recite Q 9:36 only as far as “four of them are sacred” and then stop without a list.[^36] Meanwhile, Ibn ʿAwn and Qurra transmit Farewell-Sermon material without the calendar passage.[^37][^38] By the middle of the second century AH the received **11–12–1+7** enumeration is established in more than one literary environment.

### 12. Why Dhū al-Qaʿda? Q 2:194 and the Hudaybiyya chronology

The strongest traditional argument for the sanctity of Dhū al-Qaʿda is Q 2:194:

<QuranPassage verse="2:194">
<Fragment slot="translation">

“The sacred month for the sacred month.”

</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="arabic">

**<Arabic>الشَّهْرُ الْحَرَامُ بِالشَّهْرِ الْحَرَامِ</Arabic>**

</Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

Early exegesis connects Q 2:194 with Hudaybiyya and the compensatory ʿumra. In the extant *Tafsīr Mujāhid*, **Warqāʾ → Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid** says that Quraysh boasted of turning the Prophet back at Hudaybiyya while he was in iḥrām in Dhū al-Qaʿda, and that God admitted him to Mecca in Dhū al-Qaʿda the following year.[^39] Al-Ṭabarī supplies an actual branching structure: **ʿĪsā** and **Shibl** independently transmit the same explicit Dhū al-Qaʿda interpretation from **Ibn Abī Najīḥ → Mujāhid**, making Ibn Abī Najīḥ the common link of that Mujāhid recension.[^40]

The Qatāda material forms a separate cluster.*Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba → Qatāda explicitly dates both the blocking and the compensatory return to Dhū al-Qaʿda, while Maʿmar → Qatāda preserves the same Hudaybiyya/Q 2:194 logic in the more generic form “a sacred month for a sacred month.”[^41] Al-Suddī explicitly gives Dhū al-Qaʿda; Miqsam and ʿAṭāʾ/Ibn Jurayj likewise connect the verse with Hudaybiyya and reciprocal sacred time, though the surviving wording does not always name the month.[^42] The result is more precise than saying simply that “many exegetes agree”: **Hudaybiyya is broadly distributed, while the explicit Dhū al-Qaʿda label is strongest in particular branches, especially Ibn Abī Najīḥ/Mujāhid and Saʿīd/Qatāda.**

The month-date itself, however, is less secure in the earliest reconstructable Hudaybiyya narrative than the later consensus suggests. Hishām b. ʿUrwa transmits from his father ʿUrwa:

<HadithBlock source="al-Muṣannaf, Ibn Abī Shayba, no. 37995">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“The Messenger of God went out to Hudaybiyya, and Hudaybiyya was in Shawwāl.”[^43]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

«<Arabic>خَرَجَ رَسُولُ اللهِ ﷺ إِلَى الْحُدَيْبِيَةِ، وَكَانَتِ الْحُدَيْبِيَةُ فِي شَوَّالٍ</Arabic>»

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Andreas Görke's isnād-cum-matn study of the Hudaybiyya tradition identifies Hishām b. ʿUrwa and al-Zuhrī as the two independent branches usable for reconstructing ʿUrwa's account, and he explicitly notes that Hishām dates the event to Shawwāl whereas later historical tradition generally places it in Dhū al-Qaʿda.[^44] The shared elements Görke reconstructs behind the two branches include the departure, the encounter with Khālid, the detour, water shortage, delegates, treaty, Abū Jandal, sacrifice, Abū Baṣīr, and related material—but not a shared month-date.[^45]

A Dhū al-Qaʿda date does occur in the Ibn Lahīʿa → Abū l-Aswad → ʿUrwa recension. Yet this is precisely the recension Görke treats as problematic since it contains substantial material absent from the other ʿUrwa traditions and apparently connected with later narrative development, leading him to conclude that it probably does not go back to ʿUrwa in its present form.[^46]

None of this proves that Hudaybiyya historically occurred in Shawwāl. It establishes the narrower point needed that Dhū al-Qaʿda is not an uncontested datum in the earliest recoverable Hudaybiyya transmission. The event story and the month assignment have distinguishable transmission histories.

The canonical Hudaybiyya-ʿumra enumeration is heavily represented through the **Hammām → Qatāda → Anas** family,[^47] and Ibn ʿAbbās traditions simultaneously remember pre-Islamic Arabs as regarding ʿumra during the ḥajj months as a grave transgression. Performing ʿumra in Dhū al-Qaʿda would then reflect the dismantling of a restriction on ʿumra during the pilgrimage season rather than prove that Dhū al-Qaʿda belonged to Q 9:36's original four.

The earliest reconstructable Hudaybiyya narrative does not supply a stable Dhū al-Qaʿda date, and the strongest independent Qays evidence reaches us through weak or opaque links. When placed beside the Ibn Abī Najīḥ common-link *nasīʾ* tradition—where **actual Dhū al-Ḥijja can be called Dhū al-Qaʿda**—month 11 remains historically explicable as part of the displacement problem rather than an uncontested anchor against it.

### 13. Rajab: a Jāhili sacred institution

Rajab requires separate treatment because its importance in pre-Islamic Arabian society is not seriously in doubt. The historical question is why Rajab was regarded as sacred and whether that independent sanctity proves that it belonged to the specific four months intended by Q 9:36.

Long before the received Islamic enumeration of months 11, 12, 1, and 7 became standard, Rajab (7) already possessed a distinctive religious and social identity in Arabian practice. Kister summarizes the older evidence by describing Rajab as a holy month of the Jāhiliyya associated with **ʿumra, the sacrifice of the ʿatāʾir to pagan deities, and the suspension of raids and warfare**. More recent scholarship likewise emphasizes that Rajab carried its own complex of pilgrimage, sacrifice, seasonal gathering, and sacralized time. Mansouri notes that Rajab's pre-Islamic reverence was reflected in the unusually large number of names attached to it, while also connecting the month with fasting, sacrifice, and pilgrimage. Rajab was therefore not merely “month seven” in a calendrical sequence. It was a pre-islamic religious institution in its own right.[^48]

This background gives context to the early reports about the month. Abū Rajāʾ al-ʿUṭāridī recalls that when Rajab arrived, people removed the iron points from their weapons.[^49] Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim says that they called Rajab **“the Deaf”** (*al-aṣamm*) in the Jāhiliyya because of its sanctity.[^50] The epithet reflects the social consequence of the month: the normal sounds of tribal conflict and weaponry were supposed to fall silent. Such reports preserve more than an abstract claim that Rajab belonged to a list of sacred months; they remember concrete behavior through which Rajab itself was marked off from ordinary time.

The Nakhla/Q 2:217 traditions fit the same pattern. An Ibn Abī Ḥātim route ending with **Jundub b. ʿAbd Allāh** says that the raiders killed Ibn al-Ḥaḍramī without knowing whether the day still belonged to **Jumādā** or had already entered **Rajab**. The polytheists then accused them of killing in the sacred month (Rajab), after which Q 2:217 was revealed thereafter.[^51] Al-Suddī's composite account preserves essentially the same dispute. The Meccans insisted that the killing occurred in Rajab, whereas the Muslims place it in Jumādā; another form situates the event at the very boundary between the two.[^52] What gives the episode its force is the crossing into **Rajab itself**. Once the month had begun, violence became scandalous. This is strong evidence that Rajab possessed an independent status of inviolability among the Quraysh and their surrounding society.

The cultic evidence is even clearer. The **ʿatīra**, also called the **rajabiyya**, was a sacrifice characteristically associated with Rajab in the Jāhiliyya. Through **Khālid al-Ḥadhdhāʾ → Abū al-Malīḥ → Nubaysha**, people ask about the sacrifice they used to perform in Rajab before Islam.[^53] Other routes remember a household animal slaughtered in Rajab and call it the ʿatīra or rajabiyya.[^54] Mansouri likewise describes the ʿatīra as a specifically pre-Islamic Rajab sacrifice and preserves the traditional description of animals being slaughtered for idols and their blood being poured upon them. Whatever historical judgment is made about every detail of these descriptions, the point is that **Rajab generated its own sacrificial vocabulary and ritual cycle**.[^55]

The persistence of Rajab's prestige after the rise of Islam is equally revealing. Kister observes that veneration of the month continued into the Islamic period and generated contradictory traditions either promoting or restricting practices associated with Rajab. Some authorities attempted to abolish or neutralize the old Rajab sacrifice, while others permitted or continued it. The controversy is precisely what one would expect when a new religious community inherited a month already carrying substantial pre-Islamic ritual authority.[^56]

Rajab could also function as a recurring administrative date. The Adhruḥ treaty tradition fixes one hundred dinars **“in every Rajab,”** while the Najrān material makes Rajab one of its two annual delivery dates.[^57] This reinforces the broader historical point that **Rajab possessed a social and calendrical importance of its own**, independent of membership in a continuous four-month block.

This background also gives context to Ibn Sīrīn's place in the received sacred-month tradition. Legal literature remembers Ibn Sīrīn as unusually persistent in performing the **ʿatīra in Rajab**.[^58] One of the transmitters lying near the center of an influential Farewell-Sermon enumeration belonged to an environment in which Rajab's inherited cultic prestige remained especially vivid. There were also authorities denouncing the glorification of Rajab — including a report on ʿUmar's authority: "I saw ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb striking men's hands when they lifted them away from food in Rajab, until they put them back to it, saying: 'It is only a month that the people of the Jāhiliyya used to venerate.'"[^59] Ibn al-Qayyim writes in *al-Manār al-Munīf*: "Every hadith mentioning the fasting of Rajab, or prayer on certain nights in it, is a fabricated lie."[^60]

Ibn Sīrīn is independently recorded as the only jurist in the entire tradition who kept performing the ʿatīra (the old Rajab sacrifice) and refused to accept it was abrogated. Sources describe him by name as the sole exception to the consensus of "the scholars of all the regions," and say he used to "transmit something" in support of it: "...nor the ʿatīra, which is the sacrifice of Rajab — this is the position of the scholars of the [various] regions/metropolises (ʿulamāʾ al-amṣār), except for Ibn Sīrīn: for he used to slaughter the ʿatīra in Rajab, and he would narrate something concerning it." [^61]

So the individual who's the sole source-point for the hadith that legally cements Rajab's sacred status in the hadith corpus is also the same individual still practicing the pre-Islamic Rajab rite others had dropped. And his own student, Ibn ʿAwn, who carries that same hadith forward, is also independently recorded doing the ʿatīra as well.[^62]

Rajab being highly venerated and treated as inviolable in Jāhili Arabian society does not entail Rajab was one of the specific four months intended by Q 9:36. Rajab's Jāhili sacrality was originally independent of the Qurʾānic four-month arrangement. Its exceptional pre-Islamic prestige then made it a natural candidate for preservation and eventual incorporation into the received **11–12–1 + 7** enumeration by the likes of Ibn Sirin and pre-islamic cultural memory. On this reading, the later list did not create Rajab's sanctity; rather, it absorbed an already-sacralized Arabian month into the identification of Q 9:36's four

### 14. The later “months of ḥajj” do not settle the Qurʾānic question

The traditional interpretation of Q 2:197 identifies the “known months” of ḥajj with Shawwāl, Dhū al-Qaʿda, and all or part of Dhū al-Ḥijja. Al-Ṭabarī preserves both a shorter reckoning ending with the first ten days of Dhū al-Ḥijja and a reckoning including the entire month.[^63] Authorities appear on both sides of the disagreement. Ibn ʿAbbās is made to say that God appointed those months for ḥajj and the rest of the year for ʿumra,[^64] while al-Ṭabarī prefers the shorter view because no ḥajj rite occurs after the days of Minā.[^65] Bukhārī likewise places Ibn ʿUmar's list at the head of his chapter on the verse.[^66]

But this material is precisely where the Qurʾānic distinction between **months** and **days** becomes important. Al-Ṭabarī's argument defines the larger season by the date of the final rite. Q 2:197, however, says that the ḥajj is in “known months,” while Q 22:28 and Q 2:203 separately speak of its “known” or “numbered” days. The text itself permits a broader time frame than the ritual timetable used by the later juristic interpretation.

The disagreement within the transmitted exegesis also shows that the boundaries were not self-evident from the verse. Some authorities included the whole of Dhū al-Ḥijja even though the decisive rites had already ended.[^67][^68] Q 2:197 itself supplies neither the names Shawwāl and Dhū al-Qaʿda nor a statement that the pilgrimage months form a category separate from the sacred months. The traditional list is just an interpretation of the verse, not data contained in the verse.


## Conclusion

The case for 12–1–2–3 should begin where the Qurʾān begins.

Q 9:36 establishes four sacred months in a divinely fixed twelve-month order, and Q 9:37 condemns manipulating their placement while preserving their number. Q 9:1–5 then gives the Qurʾān's only narrated four-month protected period in this context: hostile polytheists and the believers are granted four months of free movement, the proclamation is made at the Greater Pilgrimage, peaceful treaties remain binding, and the period ends when **“the sacred months”** have passed. Q 2:217 supplies the legal logic by treating fighting in sacred time as a grave matter. Q 5:2 and Q 5:97 place sacred time inside the same institutional complex as the Sacred House, offerings, garlands, and pilgrims. Q 2:197 describes the ḥajj as occupying “known months,” while Q 22:28 and Q 2:203 distinguish the narrower ritual “days.” Q 2:189 makes the lunar calendar itself a regulator of pilgrimage.

Taken together, these passages point to a **continuous pilgrimage-centered sacred season**, not a 3+1 arrangement scattered across the year. Al-Suddī's count, alongside Mujāhid, ʿAmr b. Shuʿayb, Ibn Zayd, and Ibn Isḥāq, preserves a period beginning in Dhū al-Ḥijja and continuing through al-Muḥarram, Ṣafar, and Rabīʿ I.[^69][^70]

The historical reconstruction is now stronger because *nasīʾ* no longer functions merely as an abstract possibility. The **Ibn Abī Najīḥ common-link tradition** explicitly describes the relabeling of Dhū al-Ḥijja as Dhū al-Qaʿda and al-Muḥarram as Dhū al-Ḥijja.[^71][^72] This is precisely the one-position shift required to turn the beginning of **12–1–2** into the names **11–12–1**. Independent traditions about Muḥarram/Ṣafar displacement and the “two Ṣafars” show that the general problem of month identity was not confined to that one isnād family.[^73][^74][^75] The tradition supplies the mechanism; it does not preserve a decree explicitly announcing the transformation of the sacred list.

Rajab's 'independent sanctity is substantial: traditions of abstention from violence, the Nakhla controversy, the ʿatīra, and its use as a recurring payment month all show a month with its own cultic and social prestige.[^76][^77][^78] That evidence explains why Rajab would remain important without requiring it to have belonged to the continuous four-month pilgrimage season. “Rajab was sacred” and “Rajab was one of Q 9:36's four” are historically distinct propositions.

<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">

We've analyzed the Qurʾānic discourse of Q 9:1–5 and 9:36–37, the pilgrimage-security complex, the early Q 9:5 count, the Ibn Abī Najīḥ common-link evidence for exactly the required one-position relabeling, the independent character of Rajab's sanctity in pre-Islam,  and the material chronology of month-name standardization. On that cumulative evidence, 12–1–2–3 remains the strongest reconstruction of the sacred calendar presupposed by the Qurʾān, while 11–12–1+7 is better explained as the product of calendrical displacement, later standardization, and later interpretive consolidations.

</VerdictBox>
