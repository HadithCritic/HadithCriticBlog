## The Hadith Cluster and the Problem

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">يَعُوذُ عَائِذٌ بِالْبَيْتِ، فَيُبْعَثُ إِلَيْهِ بَعْثٌ، فَإِذَا كَانُوا بِبَيْدَاءَ مِنَ الْأَرْضِ خُسِفَ بِهِمْ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“A refuge-seeker will seek refuge at the House. A force will be sent against him. When they are at a desert plain, they will be swallowed.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This article is about one layer of the Mahdī tradition. It is not about every report ever attached to the Mahdī. It is about a specific Meccan cluster: a man takes refuge at the House, an army is sent against him, and God swallows that army at al-Bayḍāʾ before it reaches him. In later versions, the story grows. A caliph dies, a man flees from Medina to Mecca, people force him into allegiance between the Rukn and the Maqām, a Syrian army marches against him, the army is swallowed, Iraqi bands and Syrian substitutes come to him, a Kalbī enemy appears, and eventually the figure is named as the Mahdī.

The claim here is not that the entire Mahdī corpus comes from Ibn al-Zubayr. The earlier Kaysānī layer belongs to a different world, centered around Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya. The black-banner material has its own Abbasid setting. The Sufyānī material has its own line of development. The point here is narrower and stronger: the Meccan sanctuary layer of the Mahdī narrative is best read as a Zubayrid memory, built around the failed Meccan caliphate of ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr and later transformed into an eschatological reversal.

<ClaimBox>
The Zubayrid contribution to the Mahdī corpus is not the title “Mahdī” itself. It is the Meccan stage: the refuge-seeker at the House, the sanctuary, the Rukn and Maqām, the Syrian army, and the swallowed-army reversal at al-Bayḍāʾ.
</ClaimBox>

For a lay reader, the method is simple. We are not starting with later theology. We are starting with the shape of the reports. What is the repeated scene? Who is the unnamed figure? Why is Mecca central? Why is an army sent from Syria? Why is the army destroyed before reaching the sanctuary? And why do early versions explicitly say that people were asking about this report **in the time of Ibn al-Zubayr** or when **al-Ḥajjāj’s army** came?

Once those clues are placed next to the historical record, the pattern becomes hard to ignore.

## The Historical Anchor: Ibn al-Zubayr Was the Refuge-Seeker

The key to this cluster is not hidden inside the hadith books. It is in the chronicles. Ibn al-Zubayr was not merely a man who happened to be in Mecca. He was publicly known as **the refuge-seeker at the House** and **the refuge-seeker in the Sanctuary**. This was not a vague title. It was a political and religious identity attached to him during his revolt against the Umayyads.

Al-Masʿūdī says that Ibn al-Zubayr took refuge at the Sacred House and had named himself **العائذ بالبيت**, “the refuge-seeker at the House,” until poets mentioned him by that name.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">وَعَاذَ ابْنُ الزُّبَيْرِ بِالْبَيْتِ الْحَرَامِ، وَكَانَ قَدْ سَمَّى نَفْسَهُ الْعَائِذَ بِالْبَيْتِ، وَشُهِرَ بِهَذَا حَتَّى ذَكَرَتْهُ الشُّعَرَاءُ فِي أَشْعَارِهَا</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“Ibn al-Zubayr took refuge at the Sacred House. He had named himself ‘the refuge-seeker at the House,’ and he became known by this until the poets mentioned him in their verses.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

Al-Ṭabarī preserves the same identification with even less ambiguity. A man calls people to **the refuge-seeker in the Sanctuary**, and al-Ṭabarī glosses it directly: “meaning ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr.”

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">أَدْعُوكُمْ إِلَى الْعَائِذِ بِالْحَرَمِ — يَعْنِي عَبْدَ اللَّهِ بْنَ الزُّبَيْرِ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“I call you to the refuge-seeker in the Sanctuary — meaning ʿAbd Allāh b. al-Zubayr.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

Al-Balādhurī preserves the same title in several registers. Ibn al-Zubayr uses it for himself. His supporters use it to rally others. His opponents use it to dismiss him. One hostile statement says, “By God, Ibn al-Zubayr is not a caliph upon whom the community has agreed. He is only a man taking refuge at the House.” That is exactly why the title matters. It was not a later scholarly guess. It was a live political label.

This is the historical anchor. Without it, the hadith reports sound like abstract prophecy. With it, the vocabulary becomes specific. The reports say “a refuge-seeker seeks refuge at the House.” The chronicles say Ibn al-Zubayr was known as “the refuge-seeker at the House.” The reports say an army comes against the sanctuary. The chronicles say Syrian armies came against Ibn al-Zubayr in Mecca. The reports say God swallows the army. History says the army was not swallowed and Ibn al-Zubayr was killed. That gap between the report and the history is where the eschatological reversal happens.

## The Earliest Kernel: A Refuge-Seeker and a Swallowed Army

The simplest form of the report is not yet a full Mahdī story. There is no caliph’s death, no forced bayʿa, no Iraqi bands, no Kalb enemy, and no explicit Mahdī. There is only the sanctuary refugee, the army, and the swallowing at al-Bayḍāʾ.

The key Umm Salama version runs through:

**The Prophet → Umm Salama → ʿUbayd Allāh b. al-Qibṭiyya → ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz b. Rufayʿ → Jarīr / Zuhayr → collectors.**

The important part is not only the prediction. It is the scene around the prediction. In Ibn Abī Shayba’s version, al-Ḥārith b. Abī Rabīʿa and ʿAbd Allāh b. Ṣafwān enter upon Umm Salama and ask her about the army that will be swallowed. The report then says this happened **in the time of Ibn al-Zubayr**.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">دَخَلَ الْحَارِثُ بْنُ أَبِي رَبِيعَةَ وَعَبْدُ اللَّهِ بْنُ صَفْوَانَ عَلَى أُمِّ سَلَمَةَ ... فَسَأَلَاهَا عَنِ الْجَيْشِ الَّذِي يُخْسَفُ بِهِ، وَذَلِكَ فِي زَمَانِ ابْنِ الزُّبَيْرِ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“al-Ḥārith b. Abī Rabīʿa and ʿAbd Allāh b. Ṣafwān entered upon Umm Salama ... and asked her about the army that would be swallowed. That was in the time of Ibn al-Zubayr.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This sentence is load-bearing. It tells us how the report was being heard. People in the Zubayrid crisis were asking about a swallowed-army prophecy because a real army was threatening a real refuge-seeker at the House. The report does not name Ibn al-Zubayr as the predicted figure, but the early interpretive frame points directly to him.

There are also small wording variations. Some versions say the refuge-seeker takes refuge **at the House**. Some say **in the Sanctuary**. Aḥmad preserves a variant saying **at the Stone**. These differences do not weaken the identification. They all point to the same Meccan sacred space. What stays stable is the core image: a protected sanctuary figure and an army destroyed before reaching him.

## The Army-at-the-House Reports

A second family removes the refuge-seeker and focuses on the army. In these reports, an army attacks the House and is swallowed. This is not a different world. It is the same memory with the camera moved. The first family focuses on the protected figure. This family focuses on the punished army.

The strongest version is the Ḥafṣa report through the family of ʿAbd Allāh b. Ṣafwān. That matters because ʿAbd Allāh b. Ṣafwān was not a random name. He was a Meccan Zubayrid loyalist and was killed defending Ibn al-Zubayr’s body. His family transmits a report about an army attacking the House, and Ibn Mājah preserves the crucial gloss: when the army of al-Ḥajjāj came, “we thought they were the ones.”

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">فَلَمَّا جَاءَ جَيْشُ الْحَجَّاجِ ظَنَنَّا أَنَّهُمْ هُمْ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“When the army of al-Ḥajjāj came, we thought they were the ones.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

That is about as clear as an interpretive activation can get. The family transmitting the report thought al-Ḥajjāj’s army — the army that actually killed Ibn al-Zubayr — might be the army that the Prophet had foretold would be swallowed.

The historical irony is the point. Al-Ḥajjāj’s army was not swallowed. It reached Mecca, defeated Ibn al-Zubayr, and killed him. So what does the hadith material do? It preserves the opposite outcome. It takes the army that succeeded historically and imagines the army being destroyed by God. It takes a political defeat and turns it into an eschatological expectation.

That is why this cluster should not be read as a neutral prediction floating above history. It is embedded in the memory of the Meccan sieges.

## The Full Scenario: The Anonymous Meccan Redeemer

The third family expands the kernel into a full eschatological scenario. Now the report says a caliph dies, people disagree, a man leaves Medina for Mecca, the Meccans bring him out unwillingly, they pledge allegiance to him between the Rukn and the Maqām, a Syrian army is sent against him, and that army is swallowed at al-Bayḍāʾ. Then Iraqi bands and Syrian substitutes come to him, a Quraysh man with Kalbī maternal uncles rises against him, the enemy is defeated, wealth is distributed, the sunna is restored, and the figure reigns seven or nine years.

This is the familiar Mahdī template, but in the earlier versions the figure is usually still anonymous. He is “a man from Medina,” “a man from my community,” or, in one later route, “a man from Banū Hāshim.” The story is Mahdī-shaped before it is consistently Mahdī-named.

The main route runs through Qatāda:

**The Prophet → Umm Salama → unstable upper link → Ṣāliḥ Abū al-Khalīl → Qatāda → Hishām / Maʿmar / ʿImrān → collectors.**

The ʿAbd al-Razzāq version is especially important because it is transmitted from Qatāda in mursal form. Qatāda raises it to the Prophet, but without a named Companion in the chain. That matters because it may preserve an earlier stage before the prophetic attribution was fully stabilized through a named Companion route.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">يَكُونُ اخْتِلَافٌ عِنْدَ مَوْتِ خَلِيفَةٍ، فَيَخْرُجُ رَجُلٌ مِنَ الْمَدِينَةِ فَيَأْتِي مَكَّةَ، فَيَسْتَخْرِجُهُ النَّاسُ مِنْ بَيْتِهِ وَهُوَ كَارِهٌ، فَيُبَايِعُونَهُ بَيْنَ الرُّكْنِ وَالْمَقَامِ، فَيُبْعَثُ إِلَيْهِ جَيْشٌ مِنَ الشَّامِ، حَتَّى إِذَا كَانُوا بِالْبَيْدَاءِ خُسِفَ بِهِمْ</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“There will be disagreement at the death of a caliph. A man will leave Medina and come to Mecca. The people will bring him out from his house while he is unwilling, and they will pledge allegiance to him between the Rukn and the Maqām. An army from Syria will be sent against him, and when they are at al-Bayḍāʾ they will be swallowed.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is where the Zubayrid memory becomes a full end-times story. Ibn al-Zubayr fled to Mecca after refusing the Umayyad order. He received allegiance there. Syrian armies came against him. His caliphate lasted roughly nine years. He had Iraqi support. His great enemy was the Umayyad-Syrian order, deeply tied to Kalbī power. The hadith version keeps the structure but changes the ending. The Meccan claimant is righteous and unwilling. The Syrian army is destroyed. The supporters arrive. The Kalbī enemy is defeated. The reign succeeds.

That is not random similarity. It is narrative reversal.

## The Rukn-and-Maqām Motif Becomes Explicitly Mahdī

The fourth layer is where the Meccan bayʿa motif becomes detached from the full story and explicitly attached to the Mahdī. Earlier versions say “a man” will be pledged allegiance between the Rukn and the Maqām. Later versions say **the Mahdī** will be pledged allegiance there.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">يُبَايَعُ الْمَهْدِيُّ بَيْنَ الرُّكْنِ وَالْمَقَامِ، لَا يُوقِظُ نَائِمًا وَلَا يُهْرِيقُ دَمًا</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The Mahdī will be pledged allegiance between the Rukn and the Maqām. He will not wake a sleeping person and will not shed blood.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

That detail is important. Ibn al-Zubayr’s Meccan claim ended in blood. The later Mahdī version imagines the same sacred space but removes the blood. The bayʿa becomes peaceful, purified, and eschatological.

Fitr b. Khalīfa is important because he also appears in the earlier Kaysānī Mahdī material. He was a Kūfan Shīʿī transmitter, and some rijāl material connects him with extreme Khashabī tendencies. But that does not mean the whole Zubayrid sanctuary cluster is Kaysānī. The matn is not about Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya. The scene is not Kaysānī geography. It is Meccan: the Rukn, the Maqām, the sanctuary, the House. Fitr’s role is better understood as a later Kūfan channel through which the Meccan bayʿa motif was absorbed into the broader Mahdī corpus and explicitly named.

## The Development in One Picture

The cluster develops in stages. First, there is the historical Ibn al-Zubayr: the refuge-seeker at the House, besieged by Syrian armies, finally killed at Mecca. Then comes the simple hadith kernel: a refuge-seeker at the House and an army swallowed at al-Bayḍāʾ. Then comes the army-at-the-House family, where the focus shifts to divine punishment of the attackers. Then comes the full scenario through Qatāda, where the anonymous Meccan claimant is given a complete eschatological career. Finally, the Rukn-and-Maqām bayʿa is explicitly named as the Mahdī’s inauguration.

That is exactly how political memory often becomes eschatology. A failed claimant is not necessarily remembered directly. Instead, the shape of his claim is carried forward, purified, anonymized, and projected into the future.

This table also prevents a common mistake. The cluster does not depend on proving that one person fabricated all the reports. That is not the argument. The argument is that related Meccan sanctuary motifs circulated in several families, and the earliest interpretive frames tie those motifs to the Zubayrid crisis. Later transmitters then expanded the material until it became the familiar Mahdī scenario.

## Why the Army Being Swallowed Matters

Historically, the army was not swallowed. That is the point.

Ḥuṣayn b. Numayr’s army besieged Mecca under Yazīd. The Kaʿba was damaged and burned. Al-Ḥajjāj’s army later besieged Mecca under ʿAbd al-Malik. Ibn al-Zubayr was killed. The sanctuary did not stop the army in the way Zubayrid supporters may have hoped.

The hadith cluster rewrites that outcome. In the reports, the army never wins. It is swallowed at al-Bayḍāʾ before it can complete its mission. The sanctuary is vindicated. The refuge-seeker is protected. The Umayyad victory is transformed into divine defeat.

<ContextNote>
This is not simply “prediction.” It is reversal. The reports imagine the outcome that Zubayrid Mecca needed but did not receive in history.
</ContextNote>

That makes the reports powerful. They allow the defeated memory to survive. Ibn al-Zubayr himself is not named, so the story is not trapped in the past. It becomes reusable. Any later Meccan redeemer can step into the template.

## Why the Figure Becomes Anonymous

One of the most important features of the cluster is that the figure is often unnamed. He is a refuge-seeker, a man from Medina, a man from the community, a Meccan figure, or sometimes a man from Banū Hāshim. This anonymity is not weakness. It is how the template becomes portable.

If the reports had simply named Ibn al-Zubayr, the tradition would have died with his defeat. But by removing his name and preserving the structure of his claim, the reports turn his story into a future pattern. The failed Meccan caliph becomes the prototype of the successful eschatological claimant.

This is also how the Zubayrid layer can be absorbed into the broader Mahdī corpus. The figure is already anonymous enough to be renamed. Later transmitters can attach the Mahdī title to the Meccan bayʿa scene without needing to preserve Ibn al-Zubayr’s name.

## Why This Is Not the Same as the Kaysānī Layer

The Kaysānī Mahdī material and the Zubayrid sanctuary material should not be collapsed into one origin. They solve different historical defeats.

The Kaysānī layer centers around Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya and the hope that a failed ʿAlid-Hāshimid figure did not truly disappear. Its logic is household, occultation, and return. The Zubayrid layer centers around Mecca, the sanctuary, the House, the Rukn and Maqām, and the army sent from Syria. Its logic is sacred geography and divine reversal of a violated sanctuary.

Both layers are anti-Umayyad in broad structure. Both transform failure into future vindication. But they are not the same layer. Kaysānism contributes one kind of Mahdī material. Zubayrid Mecca contributes another.

<VerdictBox title="The Composite Mahdī">
The later Mahdī figure is not a single clean origin. He is a composite. Kaysānī material contributes one layer. Zubayrid sanctuary memory contributes another. Later Abbasid, Sufyānī, and black-banner materials add still more layers.
</VerdictBox>

## What Would Change This Analysis?

This reading would be weakened if we found an early, independent, geographically diverse form of the sanctuary-refugee scenario that clearly predates the Zubayrid crisis, does not preserve Zubayrid interpretive activation, and does not share the Meccan political vocabulary. It would also be weakened if the title “refuge-seeker at the House” were not historically attached to Ibn al-Zubayr.

But that is not what we have. We have the opposite. The historical sources explicitly identify Ibn al-Zubayr as the refuge-seeker at the House and in the Sanctuary. The simple hadith reports are asked about in his time. The Ḥafṣa strand says that when al-Ḥajjāj’s army came, they thought it was the army foretold. The full scenario then expands the same Meccan sanctuary template into a future redeemer story. Later still, the Rukn-and-Maqām scene is explicitly attached to the Mahdī.

That is a coherent developmental sequence.

## Final Verdict

The Zubayrid sanctuary cluster is best read as the Meccan layer of the Mahdī corpus. It begins with the historical memory of Ibn al-Zubayr, the man publicly known as the refuge-seeker at the House, who made Mecca the center of his anti-Umayyad claim and was ultimately killed there after the sanctuary was violated by Syrian armies. The hadith material takes the same elements and reverses them. The refuge-seeker survives. The army is swallowed. The bayʿa becomes peaceful. The Syrian enemy fails. The Meccan claimant becomes the future redeemer rather than the defeated rebel.

This does not require claiming that every transmitter in the cluster was a Zubayrid partisan. The rijāl evidence does not support that. The stronger argument is tradition-historical: the vocabulary, setting, early interpretive comments, motif structure, and later Mahdī coding all point to the same development. The political memory of Ibn al-Zubayr’s failed Meccan caliphate was anonymized, purified, and projected into the future.

That is why this cluster matters. It shows how history becomes prophecy. It shows how a failed claimant can survive as an eschatological template. And it shows how the Mahdī tradition absorbed the defeats of multiple anti-Umayyad movements until the later figure became a composite redeemer made from older political ghosts.

The Zubayrid Mahdī is Ibn al-Zubayr without the name: the refuge-seeker whose army is finally swallowed before it can reach the House.
