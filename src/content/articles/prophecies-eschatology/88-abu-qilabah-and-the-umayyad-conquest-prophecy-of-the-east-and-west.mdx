<ClaimBox title="Abstract">A hadith attributed to Muḥammad through Thawbān declares that God showed him the eastern and western extremities of the earth and that his community would rule the territory brought before him. The report appears remarkably suited to the expansion of the Umayyad caliphate under al-Walīd I, when armies entered Iberia, advanced into Sind, and seized major cities of Transoxiana between 711 and 714 CE. Its transmission history leads to the Basran scholar Abū Qilābah al-Jarmī, who lived in Umayyad Syria and whose book is explicitly named in a report about this very hadith. His connections to the Syrian administration, frontier settlements, and caliphal circles provide a plausible social context for an *ex eventu* prophecy that turned recent conquests into evidence of prophetic foreknowledge. This study examines whether Abū Qilābah was merely a transmitter or a possible originator of the account, weighing the written-book testimony, competing isnāds, related conquest traditions, and the political unrest that accompanied Umayyad expansion.</ClaimBox>

## A Prophecy of an Empire Already Taking Shape

In *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, Thawbān is said to report that Muḥammad made an extraordinary announcement:

<HadithBlock
  isnad={"Ḥammād b. Zayd → Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī → Abū Qilābah → Abū Asmāʾ al-Raḥabī → Thawbān"}

These are selections from a longer report in **Muslim 2889a**, transmitted through **Ḥammād b. Zayd → Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī → Abū Qilābah → Abū Asmāʾ al-Raḥabī → Thawbān**.[^1]

The longer narration also preserves the petition for protection from famine and an external enemy in these words:[^1]

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"وَإِنِّي سَأَلْتُ رَبِّي لأُمَّتِي أَنْ لاَ يُهْلِكَهَا بِسَنَةٍ بِعَامَّةٍ وَأَنْ لاَ يُسَلِّطَ عَلَيْهِمْ عَدُوًّا مِنْ سِوَى أَنْفُسِهِمْ"}

  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 2889a"}

The prediction is often treated as a remarkable announcement of the later geographical expansion of Muslim sovereignty. Yet there is a historical question that must precede any appeal to its fulfillment: **When is the precise wording first recoverable, and who was responsible for bringing it into circulation?** A narration preserved in a third-century collection need not have originated in the lifetime of the individual to whom it was attributed. Its history must be reconstructed from the surviving transmission networks.

An additional wording in **Ibn Abī Shaybah, *al-Muṣannaf* 32353**, supplies an explicit Persian–Roman interpretation of the treasures, with the transmission marking it as wording Ḥammād heard on one occasion:[^3]

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"قَالَ حَمَّادٌ : وَسَمِعْتُهُ مَرَّةً وَاحِدَةً يَقُولُ : فَأَوَّلْتُهَا مُلْكَ فَارِسَ وَالرُّومِ"}
  translation={"Ḥammād said: I heard him say once: ‘I interpreted them as the dominion of Persia and Rome.’"}
  highlightArabic={["فَأَوَّلْتُهَا مُلْكَ فَارِسَ وَالرُّومِ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["I interpreted them as the dominion of Persia and Rome"]}
  source={"al-Muṣannaf, Ibn Abī Shaybah, no. 32353"}
  url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/ibnabishaybah/33/1"}
/>

The language of *eastern and western regions* also occurs in the Qurʾān, in a different historical setting:[^25]

<QuranVerse
  verse="7:137"
  highlightArabic={["مَشَٰرِقَ ٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَغَٰرِبَهَا"]}
  highlightEnglish={["the land, east and west"]}

These textual parallels establish neither the date nor the direction of borrowing; they make the precise transmitted wording and its context important to the argument.

The report's earliest historically interesting transmitter, **Abū Qilābah**, lived when the Umayyad state was extending its dominion simultaneously toward both ends of the known imperial horizon. He also occupied a position within the Syrian political and military-administrative environment. An early witness claims to have read this particular hadith **in Abū Qilābah's book**. That combination makes Abū Qilābah more than an arbitrary name selected to explain a convenient prophecy.

The earth-folding report may have been formulated in Abū Qilābah's circle after the conquests, then retrospectively attributed to Muḥammad to provide artificial support for these military objectives. Such an attribution would transform victories achieved by Umayyad armies into victories the Prophet had already foreseen. It would also explain why the victorious community could continue to tear itself apart in civil war.

## The Years 711–714: The Eastern and Western Frontiers

The chronology of the conquests provides the setting for this possibility. Under **al-Walīd I (r. 86–96 AH / 705–715 CE)**, large-scale advances unfolded on opposite sides of the caliphate. In **92 AH / 711 CE**, Ṭāriq b. Ziyād entered the Iberian Peninsula; Mūsā b. Nuṣayr subsequently joined the campaign. In the east, Muḥammad b. al-Qāsim campaigned in Sind, while Qutaybah b. Muslim extended Umayyad control in Transoxiana and took Samarkand, commonly dated to **93 AH / 712 CE**.[^2]

In the account of Qutaybah's expedition under the heading *The Campaign against Samarkand*, al-Ṭabarī records:[^27]

<QuoteBlock author="al-Ṭabarī" source="Tārīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk, 5:254, Ghazwat Qutaybah b. Muslim Samarqand" sourceUrl="https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/تاريخ-الطبري/v/5/p/254">
They entered the city of Samarkand and concluded a settlement with its people.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">ودخلوا مدينة سمرقند فصالحوهم</p>
</QuoteBlock>

This is a medieval historical report about the campaign, not an independently contemporary record of the event.

| Approximate date | Campaign or development | Relevance to the prophecy |
|---|---|---|
| 698 CE | Carthage captured | Western expansion was underway before al-Walīd |
| 700–703 CE | Ibn al-Ashʿath's rebellion, arising from an eastern expedition | Military expansion provoked serious internal opposition |
| **711 CE** | Iberian conquest begins | The caliphate pushes toward the far west |
| **711–713 CE** | Sind campaigns | A major eastern advance occurs at roughly the same time |
| **712 CE** | Samarkand taken by Qutaybah | Another conspicuous eastern victory |
| **715 CE** | Qutaybah killed after the succession to Sulaymān | The army's loyalty and the expansion project were politically contested |

Approximately 712 CE, within the wider **711–714 CE** interval, offers the most striking coincidence between its *eastern and western* language and events known in Abū Qilābah's lifetime. A contemporary might have understood the victories as an imperial achievement on a scale without precedent in that generation.

## Why Might Such a Prophecy Have Been Useful?

The conquests were spectacular, but they were hardly free of opposition. The state relied upon armies whose soldiers sometimes resented the demands of distant campaigns, the conduct of governors, and the costs of continued warfare. The clearest case was the rebellion of **Ibn al-Ashʿath**. Sent east to fight the ruler of Zabulistan, Ibn al-Ashʿath came into conflict with al-Ḥajjāj over the continuation of the expedition. The dispute escalated into an Iraqi rebellion against Umayyad authority, drawing in military elites and prominent religious figures. The conflict was not simply a popular rejection of conquest as a principle, it also concerned Iraqi grievances, coercive governance, pay, military autonomy, and political legitimacy.[^4]

The problem persisted beyond the first great successes. In 715, Qutaybah b. Muslim, one of the principal architects of the eastern advances, opposed the new caliph Sulaymān. His soldiers did not sustain his challenge; he was killed in the ensuing revolt. This episode cannot be reduced to general weariness with conquest, because the immediate dispute concerned the succession and Qutaybah's political position. But it illustrates how precarious the authority of a victorious commander could become even within his own army.[^5]

A retrospective prophecy would be well suited to such a world. It could say that the empire's territorial expansion was not merely the work of controversial governors or exhausted soldiers. God had shown its future extent to Muḥammad in advance. It could also accommodate the embarrassment of Muslim-on-Muslim violence. The community was guaranteed survival against outside annihilation, **not** immunity from fighting itself.

## Abū Qilābah in the Umayyad Syrian World

Abū Qilābah, **ʿAbd Allāh b. Zayd al-Jarmī**, was an influential Basran traditionist whose death is commonly placed in **104 AH / 722–723 CE**, although later dates, including 106–107 AH, are also reported. His year of birth is not established. He was therefore alive for the conquests of Iberia, Sind, and Samarkand, and was already a recognized scholar by that time.[^6]

More important than his lifespan is the particular political environment in which he is remembered.

### His relocation to Syria and the protection of ʿAbd al-Malik

Ibn ʿAsākir preserves an account explaining why Abū Qilābah came to Syria. The governor **al-Ḥajjāj** had allegedly tried to make him a judge; Abū Qilābah fled, seeking protection. When the caliph **ʿAbd al-Malik** was informed, he reportedly wrote to al-Ḥajjāj on Abū Qilābah's behalf. Abū Qilābah is then said to have remarked that he had wanted to come to Syria and would not leave it.[^7]

The report identifies his motive for arriving at the caliphal court with the words:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn ʿAsākir" source="Tārīkh madīnat Dimashq, 28:286" sourceUrl="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/6318/286?lang=ar">
Seeking protection from al-Ḥajjāj, who had wanted to appoint him to the judiciary.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">متعوذا من الحجاج اراده على القضاء</p>
</QuoteBlock>

And it preserves Abū Qilābah's reported response:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn ʿAsākir" source="Tārīkh madīnat Dimashq, 28:286" sourceUrl="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/6318/286?lang=ar">
I had wanted to come to Syria; now I have entered it, and I will not leave it.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">قد كنت أحب ان آتي الشام وقد دخلتها ولن اخرج منها</p>
</QuoteBlock>

If reliable, this episode places his move **before 705 CE**, the end of ʿAbd al-Malik's reign. Abū Qilābah would thus have been established within the Syrian environment before al-Walīd's most conspicuous conquests. Biographical material identifies his later residence as **Dārayyā**, near Damascus, the administrative center of the Umayyad caliphate.[^6]

Al-Dhahabī's notice gives the geographical detail directly:

<QuoteBlock author="al-Dhahabī" source="Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ, biography of Abū Qilābah" sourceUrl="https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/سير_أعلام_النبلاء/أبو_قلابة">
He came to Syria and settled in Dārayyā.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">قدم الشام وانقطع بداريا</p>
</QuoteBlock>

### His *dīwān* and the frontier stations

Ibn Saʿd's biographical judgment, repeated by al-Dhahabī, includes the statement:[^6]

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Saʿd, quoted by al-Dhahabī" source="Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ, biography of Abū Qilābah" sourceUrl="https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/سير_أعلام_النبلاء/أبو_قلابة">
His *dīwān* was in Syria.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">وَكَانَ دِيوَانُهُ بِالشَّامِ</p>
</QuoteBlock>

The *dīwān* in early Islamic government was an administrative register associated in important contexts with military stipends and state payments. Abū Qilābah's registration therefore suggests a connection to Syria's official administrative order.

Ibn Ḥibbān supplies a further detail:[^8]

<QuoteBlock author="Ibn Ḥibbān" source="Mashāhīr ʿulamāʾ al-amṣār, no. 649, p. 145" sourceUrl="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/4364/145">
He entered Syria, taking refuge at the frontier stations and staying in the border regions.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">فَدَخَلَ الشَّامَ يَأْوِي الرِّبَاطَاتِ وَيَكُونُ فِي الثُّغُورِ</p>
</QuoteBlock>

If the account preserves a genuine itinerary, Abū Qilābah spent time in settings connected to the caliphate's military frontier. It does not place him in the armies of Ṭāriq, Qutaybah, or Muḥammad b. al-Qāsim. His reported frontier residence and his proximity to Damascus are instead evidence of opportunity to encounter military news, travelers, and the ideological language of expansion.

A related illustration of how news from the eastern campaigns could arrive in Syria appears in al-Ṭabarī's account of Samarkand. A messenger says:[^27]

<QuoteBlock author="al-Ṭabarī" source="Tārīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk, 5:254" sourceUrl="https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/تاريخ-الطبري/v/5/p/254">
I came to al-Ḥajjāj, who sent me to Syria. I arrived there and entered its mosque.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">قدمت على الحجاج فوجهني إلى الشأم فقدمتها فدخلت مسجدها</p>
</QuoteBlock>

The passage attests to a *reported route by which conquest news reached Syria*; it does not assert that this messenger met Abū Qilābah.

### In the presence of ʿUmar II

Two reports place Abū Qilābah in direct contact with **ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz (ʿUmar II, r. 717–720)**. One, preserved in al-Bukhārī, describes a consultation over *qasāma*, a procedure involving oaths in homicide cases, with Abū Qilābah positioned behind the caliph's seat. Another, in ʿAbd al-Razzāq, describes the caliph visiting him during illness and discussing legal proof with leading Syrian figures.[^9]

The wording in **al-Bukhārī 4193** is unusually specific about his location:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"وَأَبُو قِلاَبَةَ خَلْفَ سَرِيرِهِ"}
  translation={"And Abū Qilābah was behind his seat."}
  source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, no. 4193"}
  url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/64/37"}
/>

In **ʿAbd al-Razzāq 18356**, the reported encounter begins:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"دَخَلَ عُمَرُ بْنُ عَبْدِ الْعَزِيزِ عَلَى أَبِي قِلَابَةَ وَهُوَ مَرِيضٌ"}
  translation={"ʿUmar b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz visited Abū Qilābah while he was ill."}
  source={"al-Muṣannaf, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī, no. 18356"}
  url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/abdalrazzaq/28/136"}
/>

These accounts suggest access to the Syrian governing elite **after** al-Walīd's campaigns. Taken together with the earlier story involving ʿAbd al-Malik, they place Abū Qilābah's remembered career across precisely the decades in which the Umayyad empire reached its exceptional geographical extent.

But the same biographical tradition also preserves his warning to Ayyūb:[^10]

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"إِيَّاكَ وَأَبْوَابَ السُّلْطَانِ"}
  translation={"Beware of the doors of rulers."}
  source={"Jāmiʿ bayān al-ʿilm wa-faḍlihi, Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr, no. 1094"}
  url={"https://www.islamweb.net/ar/library/content/1040/957/"}
/>

A scholar might oppose government appointment while benefiting from a regime's protection or preserving accounts that reinforced its wider political world. Nonetheless, these contrary testimonies prevent a simple characterization of Abū Qilābah as a loyal court spokesman.

## The Rebellion He Actually Discussed

An especially revealing report places Abū Qilābah in a discussion of the rebellion of Ibn al-Ashʿath, one of the great episodes of resistance to Umayyad government in Iraq.

In **ʿAbd al-Razzāq, *al-Muṣannaf* 20745**, the chain runs **Maʿmar → Ayyūb → Abū Qilābah**. The scholar **Muslim b. Yasār** says that he went out with Ibn al-Ashʿath but did not unsheathe a sword, shoot an arrow, or thrust a spear. Muslim's own reported words in this account are:[^11]

<HadithBlock
  isnad={"Maʿmar → Ayyūb → Abū Qilābah"}
  arabic={"قد خرجت معه فوالله ما سللت سيفا ولا رميت بسهم ولا طعنت برمح"}
  translation={"I went out with him, but by God I neither drew a sword, shot an arrow, nor thrust a spear."}
  source={"al-Muṣannaf, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī, no. 20745"}
  url={"https://www.islamicbook.ws/hadeth/msnf-abd-alrzaq-017.html"}
/>

Abū Qilābah answers:

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"لَكِنْ قَدْ رَآكَ رَجُلٌ وَاقِفًا، فَقَالَ: هَذَا مُسْلِمُ بْنُ يَسَارٍ وَاقِفٌ لِلْقِتَالِ، فَرَمَى بِسَهْمِهِ، وَطَعَنَ بِرُمْحِهِ، وَضَرَبَ بِسَيْفِهِ"}
  translation={"But a man may have seen you standing there and said, ‘There is Muslim b. Yasār standing ready for battle,’ and so he shot his arrow, thrust his spear, and struck with his sword."}
  source={"al-Muṣannaf, ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Ṣanʿānī, no. 20745"}
  url={"https://www.islamicbook.ws/hadeth/msnf-abd-alrzaq-017.html"}
/>

The account says Muslim wept. Abū Qilābah adds that he himself wished he had not spoken so harshly.[^11]

What makes the passage important is not that Abū Qilābah demonstrably endorsed the Umayyad war effort. The report does not say that. Rather, he is made to assess participation in a rebellion against Umayyad authority as morally dangerous, because the public presence of a respected scholar can encourage others to fight. A related biographical account says Muslim had been brought along unwillingly because his prestige would rally fighters.[^12]

That last detail appears explicitly in a parallel biographical report:

<QuoteBlock author="al-Dhahabī" source="Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ, biography of Muslim b. Yasār" sourceUrl="https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/60/653/%D9%85%D8%B3%D9%84%D9%85-%D8%A8%D9%86-%D9%8A%D8%B3%D8%A7%D8%B1">
So he brought him out unwillingly.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">فأخرجه مكرها</p>
</QuoteBlock>

Abū Qilābah's reported reasoning resembles an anti-*fitna* ethic where a scholar's apparent endorsement could help produce the very bloodshed he personally avoided. The hadith celebrates the extraordinary extent of Muslim dominion, but makes internal killing and captivity the great exception to divine protection. That is a message with obvious relevance in the political world of a man who was remembered for criticizing a scholar's participation in rebellion.

## The Most Revealing Clue: “I Read It in Abū Qilābah's Book”

The case for Abū Qilābah's possible authorship ultimately rests more on transmission evidence than on political biography.

Al-Ḥarbī's *Gharīb al-ḥadīth* preserves a statement attributed to **ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr**, in its discussion of the earth-folding report:[^13]

<HadithBlock
  label={"ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr’s testimony"}
  arabic={"قَرَأْتُ فِي كِتَابِ أَبِي قِلَابَةَ، فَعَرَضْتُهُ عَلَى أَيُّوبَ، فَزَعَمَ أَنَّهُ سَمِعَهُ مِنْ أَبِي قِلَابَةَ، عَنْ أَبِي أَسْمَاءَ، عَنْ ثَوْبَانَ"}
  translation={"I read it in Abū Qilābah's book and presented it to Ayyūb. He stated that he had heard it from Abū Qilābah, from Abū Asmāʾ, from Thawbān."}
  highlightArabic={["قَرَأْتُ فِي كِتَابِ أَبِي قِلَابَةَ"]}
  highlightEnglish={["I read it in Abū Qilābah's book"]}
  source={"Gharīb al-ḥadīth, al-Ḥarbī, 3:55"}
  url={"https://najafdesertlibrary.com/book/%D8%BA%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%A8-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AD%D9%8E%D8%AF%D9%90%D9%8A%D8%AB/v/3/p/55"}
/>

The same testimony appears in **al-Rūyānī, *Musnad* 635**, where the quoted narration explicitly concerns the earth being drawn together, its eastern and western extremities, the two treasures, and the community's future dominion.[^14] This is no generic claim that Abū Qilābah owned books. The witness identifies **this particular prophecy** as part of material ascribed to his written collection.

In that **ʿAbbād → Ayyūb** testimony, al-Rūyānī actually continues from the book-reading statement into the prophecy's opening:[^14]

<HadithBlock
  arabic={"إن الله زوى لي الأرض حتى رأيت مشارقها ومغاربها"}
  translation={"God drew the earth together for me until I saw its eastern and western extremities."}
  source={"Musnad, al-Rūyānī, 1:413, no. 635"}
  url={"https://www.addyaiya.com/content.php?page-id=512&v=01f7743c"}
/>

This is the text-level connection between the book claim and the report being investigated, rather than merely a statement that Abū Qilābah owned a collection.

First, al-Ḥarbī and al-Rūyānī are **two later records of the same early testimony through ʿAbbād**, not two independently observed copies of Abū Qilābah's book. Second, ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr was criticized by early narrator specialists, including for unreliable transmissions and *tadlīs*. His detailed report therefore requires scrutiny rather than automatic acceptance.[^15] Third, even an authentic copy in a person's book could have been inherited from a teacher or informant. **Ownership or possession of a written narration is not identical to composition.**

For the criticism of this particular transmitter, Ibn ʿAdī quotes a judgment attributed to Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn:[^28]

<HadithBlock
  label={"Ibn Maʿīn on ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr"}
  arabic={"عباد بن منصور ضعيف الحديث"}
  translation={"ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr is weak in hadith."}
  source={"al-Kāmil fī ḍuʿafāʾ al-rijāl, Ibn ʿAdī, entry ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr"}
  url={"https://sunna.alifta.gov.sa/BookToc/ViewServicePage?BookID=54&mainId=576721"}
/>

This is a judgment preserved in narrator criticism, not proof by itself that the book-reading statement is fabricated.

Yet there is independent evidence that **Abū Qilābah's books were real objects within the remembered history of transmission**. Al-Dhahabī records the tradition that his books were sent to Ayyūb, and al-Bukhārī preserves an episode in which material from those books was read to Ayyūb. An additional notice attributed to Ayyūb is unusually frank: after a book of Abū Qilābah was read to him, he reportedly acknowledged that he had heard its contents from Abū Qilābah, **but could recall some items and not others**. The report does not refer specifically to the earth-folding prophecy, yet it makes the interaction between written exemplars and remembered oral instruction concrete.[^6][^16][^26]

Al-Dhahabī reports the transfer of books to Ayyūb in this form:

<QuoteBlock author="al-Dhahabī" source="Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ, biography of Abū Qilābah" sourceUrl="https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/سير_أعلام_النبلاء/أبو_قلابة">
He bequeathed his books to Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī, and they were brought to him.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">فأوصى بكتبه لأيوب السختياني فحملت إليه</p>
</QuoteBlock>

The more important qualification comes from **al-Fasawī**, who records Ayyūb's response when Jarīr b. Ḥāzim read a book of Abū Qilābah to him:

<QuoteBlock author="Ayyūb al-Sakhtiyānī, in al-Fasawī" source="al-Maʿrifa wa-l-tārīkh, 2:88" sourceUrl="https://scribetools.com/en/library/sira-history/%D8%B7%D8%B2-%D8%A7%D9%95%D8%B1%D8%B4%D8%A7%D8%AF-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%82%D8%AF%D9%8A%D9%85%D8%A9/03_44618-a2dcf37e/text/0002">

The earliest recognizable source of the prophecy is not simply a person who happened to live through the conquests. **A specific testimony places the prophecy in a book associated with that person, whose books were then transmitted through the very pupil from whom the surviving hadith branches.**

## Was Abū Qilābah the Originator?

An Abū Qilābah authorship hypothesis rests upon **converging circumstances** that are way too precise to be considered coincidental:

1. Chronological opportunity: He lived through the dramatic east–west campaigns of 711–714 CE and may already have been settled in Syria when they occurred.
2. Political proximity: Biographical reports connect him with ʿAbd al-Malik, the Syrian *dīwān*, frontier stations, Dārayyā, and ʿUmar II.
3. Political engagement: A report through Ayyūb has him criticize a scholar's visible participation in Ibn al-Ashʿath's uprising, fitting a milieu concerned with communal bloodshed.
4. Specific written attribution: ʿAbbād b. Manṣūr claims to have read **this particular narration** in Abū Qilābah's book and shown it to Ayyūb.
5. Narrow early transmission: The strongest demonstrable branching occurs among Ayyūb's pupils; Qatādah's alleged oral access to Abū Qilābah is disputed, and the Shaddād recension does not bypass Ayyūb.
6. Plausible social function: The wording turns an already expanding empire into an anticipated divine outcome while explaining why military victory could coexist with internal catastrophe.

On that reconstruction, a scholar resident in the Umayyad Syrian world could have written down or reshaped the report in response to the empire's increasingly visible geographical reach. Its prophetic attribution would convert political events into retrospective confirmation of Muḥammad's foreknowledge. Ayyūb and his pupils would then account for the much more secure later dissemination. If Abū Qilābah himself was responsible for the wording, he would be a possible originator, while Ayyūb would remain the demonstrable PCL (partial common link) proliferator alongside Qatada.

## Conclusion

The earth-folding prophecy is particularly intelligible against the background of the Umayyad empire's expansion under al-Walīd I. Around **711–714 CE**, Muslim armies advanced in Iberia, Sind, and Transoxiana, making the image of a community ruling toward the eastern and western extremities politically resonant. The wars were accompanied by military dissent and episodes of internal violence; the hadith declares both imperial success and Muslim civil strife to have been foreknown.

<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">Abū Qilābah is a plausible candidate for an early formative role in this account. He lived in the relevant period, was connected by biographical reports to the Syrian Umayyad environment, and is the named owner of a book in which a transmitter says he encountered the very prophecy under investigation. The transmitted lines do not establish an independent pre-Abū-Qilābah circulation, while Ayyūb's multiple pupils show that the report was subsequently disseminated through his circle.</VerdictBox>
