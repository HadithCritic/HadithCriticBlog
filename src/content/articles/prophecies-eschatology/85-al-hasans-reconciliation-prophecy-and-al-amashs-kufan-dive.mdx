*How a disputed surrender in 41 AH became a predicted triumph—and what the surviving isnāds reveal about the story's origins.*

## A prophecy for a disputed surrender

According to a well-known hadith, Muḥammad once stood before his followers with his grandson al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī and said:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/53/9">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“This son of mine is a *sayyid* [chief or leader], and perhaps God will reconcile through him two great factions of the Muslims.”[^1]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِنَّ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ، وَلَعَلَّ اللَّهَ أَنْ يُصْلِحَ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ عَظِيمَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The alleged prediction is not difficult to interpret. After his father ʿAlī's death, al-Ḥasan briefly held the caliphal claim in Iraq. Muʿāwiya, who controlled Syria, challenged him; al-Ḥasan eventually gave way. This agreement, conventionally dated to 41 AH (661 CE), became known as the reconciliation or settlement (*ṣulḥ*) of al-Ḥasan. The saying seems to anticipate precisely that decision. A contested political act becomes evidence that Muḥammad had foreseen al-Ḥasan's true achievement.

This is the historical question we should begin with. Rather than presuming that the hadith is authentic because it appears in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, we need to ask when the wording can first be recovered, who circulated it, and how its details vary in different chains. A hadith that names a specific historical outcome and survives only through later collections invites an investigation of **its original occasion of formulation**. This is not the same as assuming every account of Muḥammad's affection for his grandson is fictitious. The prediction of a later political reconciliation is the element that must be dated.[^2]

## The historical problem in 41 AH

Al-Ḥasan inherited a dangerous confrontation between Iraqi and Syrian forces, a divided Iraqi following, and serious problems of military cohesion. His movement toward accommodation with Muʿāwiya was contested even among men who had supported his father. Wilferd Madelung's historical reconstruction, based on al-Balādhurī and other early Arabic histories, discusses the mutiny among al-Ḥasan's troops, negotiations with Muʿāwiya, the disputed conditions of the settlement, and his subsequent withdrawal to Medina. Madelung dates his relinquishment of Iraqi authority to Rabīʿ II 41 AH / August 661 CE.[^3]

A later historical compilation preserves the objections. One critic allegedly confronted al-Ḥasan after the agreement with the accusation:

<QuoteBlock source="`history:2091`" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/history/3/4/12">

“You have blackened the faces of the believers.”

**<Arabic>سَوَّدْتَ وُجُوهَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

Another reportedly greeted him:

<QuoteBlock source="`history:2091`" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/history/3/4/12">

“Peace upon you, O humiliator of the believers.”

**<Arabic>السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكَ يَا مُذِلَّ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

Al-Ḥasan's reported response is equally instructive:

<QuoteBlock source="`history:2091`" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/history/3/4/12">

“I am not the humiliator of the believers; rather, I disliked killing them over political rule.”[^4]

**<Arabic>لَسْتُ بِمُذِلِّ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ، وَلَكِنِّي كَرِهْتُ أَنْ أَقْتُلَهُمْ عَلَى الْمُلْكِ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

These reports cannot be treated as transcripts of conversations taken down in 41 AH. Their value lies in their preservation of competing historical interpretations. Was al-Ḥasan weak for relinquishing his father's struggle? Or was he praiseworthy for preventing further Muslim bloodshed? The reconciliation prophecy is an extraordinarily efficient answer to this debate. The Prophet allegedly declared that al-Ḥasan's greatness would consist in the very act some followers later condemned.

Notice the title *sayyid*. The accusation *mudhill al-muʾminīn* (“humiliator of the believers”) is answered with prophetic recognition of al-Ḥasan as a *sayyid*, a figure of honor and leadership. The hadith does more than say that two armies would stop fighting. It supplies a **divine and Prophetic evaluation of the settlement**. It also calls both sides *Muslims*, a meaningful description in an era in which civil-war opponents could accuse each other of disbelief.

## Why al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī matters

The crucial figure in the transmitted corpus is not al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī, the political protagonist, but **al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī**, the renowned Basran preacher and traditionist (21–110 AH / 642–728 CE). The two men share the same name and must be distinguished carefully. The Basran scholar was approximately twenty years old in 41 AH: the settlement occurred during his lifetime. He subsequently became one of the most influential voices in a city profoundly affected by the First Fitna and debates over political conduct.[^5]

In the main hadith family, different transmitters all return to him:

Some of these branches have multiple later students, whereas others survive through narrow, occasionally questionable routes. The broad agreement on **al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī as the transmitting source**, across different recensions and narrative settings, is a substantial datum. He is the effective **common link (CL)** of this Basran transmission complex; the earliest figure around whom the recoverable lines branch.[^6]

The name attached *above* him is much less stable:
- The canonical line adds the Basran Companion **Abū Bakra**.
- Another recension adds **Anas b. Mālik** instead.
- Several others simply present al-Ḥasan saying that the Prophet spoke, without naming any Companion.
- One expressly states that the saying had merely **reached** him.

Abū Bakra is not an arbitrary authority in this setting. Elizabeth Urban's study of him describes the politically detached, anti-*fitna* figure constructed in early Basran memory. His name is associated with avoiding fighting between Muslims, including in the separate woman-ruler tradition deployed against participation in the Battle of the Camel. Urban characterizes the present reconciliation account in the context of Abū Bakra's anti-*fitna* and pro-reconciliation outlook. That association helps explain why the hadith's Basran transmitters might have attached it to **this particular Companion**.[^7]

<HadithCriticBlog id="transmission-narrators/84-the-woman-ruler-hadith-abu-bakra-and-the-battle-of-the-camel" />

The historically meaningful point is that the report's **setting, message, and most extensive transmission network all intersect in the same post-41 AH Iraqi world**.

## The canonical version and its political setting

The most revealing witness may be *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* **2704**, precisely because it does not give the prophecy on its own. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī first recounts the military standoff between al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī and Muʿāwiya, the fear of massive losses, and the eventual negotiations. Only after relating the agreement does he append his citation from Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/53/9">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“He then made peace with him. Al-Ḥasan [al-Baṣrī] said: ‘I heard Abū Bakra say ... This son of mine is a leader, and perhaps God will reconcile through him two great factions of the Muslims.’”[^8]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>فَصَالَحَهُ فَقَالَ الْحَسَنُ: وَلَقَدْ سَمِعْتُ أَبَا بَكْرَةَ يَقُولُ ... إِنَّ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ، وَلَعَلَّ اللَّهَ أَنْ يُصْلِحَ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ عَظِيمَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The juxtaposition is integral to the report's literary effect. The audience hears the conclusion of the settlement and then receives the alleged prophecy that supplies its meaning. In the same historical narration Muʿāwiya is made to ask who would care for the community if both armies destroyed one another; his magnanimity and the negotiated solution are foregrounded. The hadith therefore also participates in a larger tradition of presenting the settlement as a preferable alternative to escalating conflict.

The wording through Abū Mūsā Isrāʾīl, from al-Ḥasan, from Abū Bakra, is found in several other collections, including al-Bukhārī **3629**, **3746**, and **7109**, al-Nasāʾī **1410**, and Aḥmad **20392**. In some versions Muḥammad is on the pulpit and looks alternately at the crowd and al-Ḥasan; elsewhere the child enters during a sermon.[^9]

## The mursal hadiths and the changing Companion

The most revealing comparison comes from *al-Sunan al-Kubrā* of al-Nasāʾī, **10009–10014**. Al-Nasāʾī records the Abū Bakra chain through Abū Mūsā, then remarks:

<QuoteBlock source="10010–10011" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai-kubra/53/100">

“Ashʿath contradicted him.”

**<Arabic>خَالَفَهُ أَشْعَثُ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

The next report, through **Ashʿath b. ʿAbd al-Malik → al-Ḥasan**, no longer names Abū Bakra. Instead it states:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai-kubra/53/100">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“From one of the Prophet's Companions—meaning Anas.”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>عَنْ بَعْضِ أَصْحَابِ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ يَعْنِي أَنَسًا</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

It also changes the scene: the Prophet is now preaching while al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī sits on his thigh. Even the phrase *sayyid* is embedded in a hopeful rather than categorical prediction:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai-kubra/53/100">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“I hope that this son of mine will be a leader, and I hope that God will reconcile through him two factions of my community.”[^10]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِنِّي لَأَرْجُو أَنْ يَكُونَ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدًا، وَإِنِّي لَأَرْجُو أَنْ يُصْلِحَ اللَّهُ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ مِنْ أُمَّتِي</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The clarification **“meaning Anas”** may itself be an explanatory identification rather than Ashʿath’s verbatim wording. In either case, this Anas attribution should not be mistaken for an independently branching Anas transmission family: **it still passes through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī**. Nor does Ashʿath consistently cite Anas. Other witnesses through Ashʿath, including al-Tirmidhī **3773**, Abū Dāwūd **4662**, and al-Ḥākim **4809**, instead give the familiar **Abū Bakra** attribution. The instability is located *inside the same transmitting environment*, not between two demonstrably independent Companions.[^11]

The next three reports in al-Nasāʾī's sequence make the issue sharper. He comments:

<QuoteBlock source="10011–10014" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai-kubra/53/100">

“ʿAwf, Dāwūd, and Hishām transmitted it *mursal*.”

**<Arabic>أَرْسَلَهُ عَوْفٌ وَدَاوُدُ وَهِشَامٌ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

In the first of these, **ʿAwf → al-Ḥasan**, al-Ḥasan is quoted saying:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai-kubra/53/100">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“It reached me that the Messenger of God said something similar to al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī.”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>بَلَغَنِي أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ قَالَ لِلْحَسَنِ بْنِ عَلِيٍّ نَحْوَهُ</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The other two, through Dāwūd and Hishām, simply have al-Ḥasan say that the Prophet spoke; no intervening Companion is mentioned. A further transmission from **Ismāʿīl b. Abī al-Ṣalt → al-Ḥasan**, preserved in *al-Maṭālib al-ʿĀliya* **4743**, likewise attributes the saying directly to the Prophet without a Companion intermediary.[^12]

<IsnadDiagram width={950} height={345} orientation="bottom-up" legend={false} ariaLabel="Competing upper attributions in the al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī family. These are variant claims of authority within one network." caption="Competing upper attributions in the al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī family. These are variant claims of authority within one network." nodes={[{"id":"a","label":"Abū Bakra","x":145,"y":60,"labelPos":"below","kind":"normal","sublabel":"[many branches]"},{"id":"b","label":"Anas?","x":450,"y":60,"labelPos":"below","kind":"normal","sublabel":"[Ashʿath]"},{"id":"c","label":"No Companion / “balaghanī”","x":750,"y":60,"labelPos":"below","kind":"normal","sublabel":"[“It reached me”]"},{"id":"d","label":"al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī","x":450,"y":245,"labelPos":"below","kind":"normal"}]} edges={[{"from":"a","to":"d","kind":"normal"},{"from":"b","to":"d","kind":"normal"},{"from":"c","to":"d","kind":"normal"}]} />

ʿAwf, Dāwūd, Hishām, and Ismāʿīl b. Abī al-Ṣalt preserve the omitted-Companion forms.

It would be too mechanical to pronounce the *mursal* form original merely because it omits a name. Transmitters sometimes abridge a report, and al-Ḥasan could have taught it both with and without his authority. But when a single transmitting node gives rise to **several omitted-Companion versions**, **one explicitly anonymous report**, and **a different named Companion**, the idea that the record transparently preserves a single, fixed Companion-to-Prophet transmission becomes considerably less convincing. **The secure convergence is at al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī; the upper attribution remains contested by the surviving texts themselves.**

## An earlier-looking formulation: “If he lives”

The wording is no more fixed than the isnād. An especially interesting recension occurs in the *Muṣannaf* of ʿAbd al-Razzāq, **21058**:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/abdalrazzaq/32/269">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“ʿAbd al-Razzāq, from Maʿmar, who said: ‘Someone who heard al-Ḥasan transmitting from Abū Bakra informed me ... This son of mine is a leader; **if he lives**, he will reconcile two groups of the Muslims.’”[^13]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>أَخْبَرَنَا عَبْدُ الرَّزَّاقِ عَنْ مَعْمَرٍ قَالَ: أَخْبَرَنِي مَنْ سَمِعَ الْحَسَنَ يُحَدِّثُ عَنْ أَبِي بَكْرَةَ ... ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ، إِنْ يَعِشْ يُصْلِحْ بَيْنَ طَائِفَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Two features distinguish this version. First, **Maʿmar's immediate informant is unnamed**, so the route cannot independently confirm the purported hearing from al-Ḥasan. Second, the central phrase is conditional: **<Arabic>إِنْ يَعِشْ</Arabic>** (*in yaʿish*), “if he lives.” It also uses **<Arabic>طائفتين</Arabic>** (“two groups”) (*ṭāʾifatayn*) rather than the common **<Arabic>فئتين</Arabic>** (“two factions”) (*fiʾatayn*).

This is compatible with a movement from a cautiously phrased statement to an assured prediction. Yet **chronology cannot be inferred solely from modal strength**. The emphatic form occurs in one Jābir branch, but other versions of that same family are less emphatic; the conditional form occurs in an early collection, but through an unnamed intermediary. A shorter or less certain wording does not automatically qualify as the earliest. What the differences do demonstrate is that the alleged remembered prophecy **had no single immutable wording**.[^14]


## From a pulpit to the Prophet's back: narrative expansion

The prophecy moves among settings with noticeably different dramatic details. In the Abū Mūsā version Muḥammad stands on the **pulpit**, looking alternately at the audience and his grandson. In ʿAlī b. Zayd's recension the child climbs onto the pulpit and is embraced or kissed. In the Mubārak b. Faḍāla family, the Prophet is **praying**, and the young al-Ḥasan repeatedly climbs onto his back or neck during prostration. The Prophet handles the child gently, then announces his status as a *sayyid*. In some versions he additionally calls him his **rayḥāna**—his fragrant flower or basil.[^16]

These may be variants of traditions about Muḥammad's affection for his grandsons that later became joined to the reconciliation saying. Such affectionate stories are independently familiar within the broader biographical literature; the appearance of different scenes does not mean every scene must have been invented for this prophecy. The relevant textual fact is that the **same predictive kernel** is repeatedly placed within **different narrative occasions**. It appears to have been more portable than its supposed original occasion.

Aḥmad **20448**, from Mubārak → al-Ḥasan → Abū Bakra, gives a particularly revealing addition after the Prophetic saying:

<QuoteBlock source="20448" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/10/42">

“Al-Ḥasan [al-Baṣrī] said: ‘By God, by God, after he assumed authority, not even a cupping-glass full of blood was shed during his caliphate.’”[^17]

**<Arabic>فَقَالَ الْحَسَنُ: فَوَاللَّهِ وَاللَّهِ، بَعْدَ أَنْ وَلِيَ لَمْ يُهْرَاقْ فِي خِلَافَتِهِ مِلْءُ مِحْجَمَةٍ مِنْ دَمٍ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

This is explicit **retrospective fulfillment commentary**. The story's narrator does not leave future readers to work out which event is meant; he places the prediction next to his own assessment of al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī's political career. Whatever one thinks of the historical precision of the bloodshed claim, the addition tells us how this transmitter intended the story to be understood. The account legitimates al-Ḥasan's decision by defining restraint as an achievement.

## The Jābir bypass: a Kufan dive through al-Aʿmash

The strongest objection to dating the reconciliation prophecy to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī’s teaching world is that a report attributed to **Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh**, rather than to Abū Bakra, has also survived. If independent, it might suggest two Companions separately heard Muḥammad forecast precisely the same event. An isnād-cum-matn analysis, however, gives us something different: **a secondary Kufan recension radiating from al-Aʿmash**, long after the settlement it purports to predict.

Al-Ṭabarānī’s *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* **2597** reports:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani/7">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“ʿAbd al-Salām b. ʿĀṣim al-Rāzī narrated to us; ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Maghrāʾ narrated to us, from al-Aʿmash, from Abū Sufyān, from Jābir ... ‘My son—meaning al-Ḥasan—is a leader, and God will surely reconcile through him two factions of Muslims.’”[^18]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>حَدَّثَنَا عَبْدُ السَّلَامِ بْنُ عَاصِمٍ الرَّازِيُّ، ثنا عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنُ مَغْرَاءَ، عَنِ الْأَعْمَشِ، عَنْ أَبِي سُفْيَانَ، عَنْ جَابِرٍ ... إِنَّ ابْنِي يَعْنِي الْحَسَنَ سَيِّدٌ وَلَيُصْلِحَنَّ اللَّهُ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

A superficial reading could blame the bypass on **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Maghrāʾ** (d. c. 190 AH), who transmits this variant and whose reports from al-Aʿmash sometimes attracted criticism. But al-Ṭabarānī’s own note prevents that simple conclusion:

<QuoteBlock source="1813" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/1/57">

“No one transmitted this hadith from al-Aʿmash except ʿAbd al-Raḥmān and Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Umawī.”[^20]

**<Arabic>لَمْ يَرْوِ هَذَا الْحَدِيثَ عَنِ الْأَعْمَشِ إِلَّا عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ وَيَحْيَى بْنُ سَعِيدٍ الْأُمَوِيُّ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

The independent *lower* testimony matters. **Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Umawī** (d. 194 AH) appears through Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn in al-Bayhaqī’s *Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa*, 6:443–44, preserving the same alleged **al-Aʿmash → Abū Sufyān → Jābir** backbone. Another later-preserved route runs through **Abū ʿAwāna al-Waḍḍāḥ** (d. 175 AH), recorded by al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī and in *al-Ṭuyūriyyāt*. Al-Ṭabarānī counted two pupils; these later witnesses supply a third claimed pupil. That third attestation deserves less weight than the two expressly named by al-Ṭabarānī, but the first two alone suffice to make Ibn Maghrāʾ an inadequate explanation of the entire Kufan branch.[^21]

There is a difference between **a real common link and a genuine Companion-level source**. Several pupils may accurately preserve what al-Aʿmash said, while al-Aʿmash himself may have changed the authority he cited. Multiplication *below* an isnād-diving narrator does not repair a false attribution *above* him. The most economical explanation of this fan-out, in light of the Basran corpus and the independent evidence below, is that **al-Aʿmash circulated a secondary version of the reconciliation saying under the name of Jābir**.

The early critics noticed the oddity, even if they did not formulate this exact historical explanation. Al-Bazzār distinguished the two Companion attributions:

<QuoteBlock source="3647" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/bazzar/64">

“The hadith of Abū Bakra is better known and has a better isnād, whereas the hadith of Jābir is stranger [rarer].”[^19]

**<Arabic>وَحَدِيثُ أَبِي بَكْرَةَ أَشْهَرُ وَأَحْسَنُ إِسْنَادًا وَحَدِيثُ جَابِرٍ أَغْرَبُ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

That judgment is consistent with the transmission evidence. A geographically and textually broader **Basran al-Ḥasan family**, and a later-circulating Kufan **al-Aʿmash family** retaining the same distinctive proposition but reporting it through a different Companion.

## Finding al-Aʿmash’s possible source: Ismāʿīl b. Muslim

A transmitter who demonstrably circulated the reconciliation tradition from al-Ḥasan also belongs to al-Aʿmash’s recorded teaching network. In al-Ṭabarānī’s *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* **2594**, the prophecy travels through:

**Abū Muʿāwiya → Ismāʿīl b. Muslim → al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī → Abū Bakra.**

The report describes the young al-Ḥasan climbing onto the Prophet during prayer, being embraced, and receiving the designation *sayyid*:

<HadithBlock url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani/7">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“This son of mine is a leader, and he is my fragrant flower in this world, and I hope God will reconcile through him two great factions of Muslims.”[^26]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِنَّ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ، وَإِنَّهُ رَيْحَانَتِي فِي الدُّنْيَا، وَأَرْجُو أَنْ يُصْلِحَ اللَّهُ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ عَظِيمَتَيْنِ</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Who was this Ismāʿīl b. Muslim? The name is shared by several different transmitters, so it would be unsafe to assume an identification from the patronymic alone. Here, however, **Ibn ʿAdī supplies unusually strong comparative evidence pointing to Ismāʿīl b. Muslim al-Makkī**, a Basran-origin transmitter who settled in Mecca. In *al-Kāmil fī Ḍuʿafāʾ al-Rijāl*, Ibn ʿAdī transmits a closely related childhood/prayer account through the **same lower pair, Abū Muʿāwiya → Ismāʿīl b. Muslim → al-Ḥasan → Abū Bakra**. This form depicts **al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn** climbing onto Muḥammad’s back and has the Prophet call his grandsons his *rayḥānatān*, but it lacks the reconciliation prediction. Its proximity to al-Ṭabarānī 2594 is an important warning that **the affectionate prayer narrative and the political prophecy could circulate together or apart**.[^27]

Ibn ʿAdī goes on to identify the pupils of **Ismāʿīl al-Makkī**:

<QuoteBlock source="Arabic biographical text" sourceUrl="https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-54/h/574453">

“Ismāʿīl al-Makkī, Ismāʿīl b. Muslim, was narrated from by Kufans: al-Aʿmash, Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid, Ḥafṣ b. Ghiyāth, Abū Muʿāwiya, Sharīk, and others.”[^27]

**<Arabic>إِسْمَاعِيلُ الْمَكِّيُّ إِسْمَاعِيلُ بْنُ مُسْلِمٍ يُحَدِّثُ عَنْهُ أَهْلُ الْكُوفَةِ: الْأَعْمَشُ، وَإِسْمَاعِيلُ بْنُ أَبِي خَالِدٍ، وَحَفْصُ بْنُ غِيَاثٍ، وَأَبُو مُعَاوِيَةَ، وَشَرِيكٌ، وَجَمَاعَةٌ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

This supplies a specific candidate pathway for acquiring the **content** of the prophecy:

The strongest evidence is **not** a surviving chain explicitly reading “al-Aʿmash from Ismāʿīl, transmitting this exact prophecy.” No such direct transcript has been recovered. It is instead the conjunction of (i) Ismāʿīl’s attested participation in the precise reconciliation/childhood textual complex, (ii) a parallel from the same Ismāʿīl–Abū Muʿāwiya pair without the prophecy, and (iii) Ibn ʿAdī’s explicit identification of **al-Aʿmash as Ismāʿīl al-Makkī’s pupil**. **Ismāʿīl is therefore the best identifiable intermediary**. His reputation for errors and rearranged isnāds also makes the instability of the narrative understandable.[^28]

## Abū Ḥātim’s control case: the same intermediaries exchanged

In *al-ʿIlal*, no. **379**, Ibn Abī Ḥātim asks his father, the critic Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī, about a report concerning how a woman prayed in a cloak. Qays b. al-Rabīʿ transmitted it as:

<HadithBlock url="https://dorar.net/h/aAKbvcfM?osoul=1">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“From al-Aʿmash, who said: Abū Sufyān—meaning Ṭalḥa b. Nāfiʿ—related to me, from al-Ḥasan, from his mother ...”[^29]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>عَنِ الْأَعْمَشِ، قَالَ: حَدَّثَنِي أَبُو سُفْيَانَ — يَعْنِي طَلْحَةَ بْنَ نَافِعٍ — عَنِ الْحَسَنِ، عَنْ أُمِّهِ</Arabic> ...**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Abū Ḥātim rejects this form. He identifies a different version attributed to al-Aʿmash **through Ismāʿīl b. Muslim al-ʿAbdī → al-Ḥasan → his mother**, with Umm Salama rather than Umm Ḥabība at the end. He then says:

<QuoteBlock source="Arabic text and critic's response" sourceUrl="https://dorar.net/h/aAKbvcfM?osoul=1">

“Al-Aʿmash from Ismāʿīl b. Muslim al-Baṣrī al-ʿAbdī is more likely, because we do not know Abū Sufyān to have narrated anything from al-Ḥasan ... Perhaps the mistake came from al-Aʿmash.”[^29]

**<Arabic>وَالْأَعْمَشُ عَنْ إِسْمَاعِيلَ بْنِ مُسْلِمٍ الْبَصْرِيِّ الْعَبْدِيِّ أَشْبَهُ؛ لِأَنَّا لَا نَعْلَمُ أَبَا سُفْيَانَ رَوَى عَنِ الْحَسَنِ شَيْئًا ... وَالْغَلَطُ لَعَلَّهُ مِنَ الْأَعْمَشِ</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

This does **not** concern the reconciliation prophecy; it concerns a different account and an explicitly named **al-ʿAbdī** intermediary. Nor does Abū Ḥātim assert deliberate falsification. Nevertheless, the structural resemblance is remarkable. A critic of the early hadith tradition already identified a case within **al-Aʿmash’s own repertory** where **Ismāʿīl ↔ Abū Sufyān** became competing intermediaries leading back toward **al-Ḥasan**. In the same context, Abū Ḥātim doubted that Abū Sufyān had narrated from al-Ḥasan at all and considered al-Aʿmash a possible source of the mistake. Abū Ḥātim’s negative assessment shows that it was not beyond dispute. The known Jābir version is still mediated through al-Aʿmash.[^30]

The proposed process is therefore not a conjecture based only on a narrator’s abstract reputation for *tadlīs*. We have **a directly comparable isnād disagreement involving al-Aʿmash, Abū Sufyān, an Ismāʿīl intermediary, and al-Ḥasan**, as well as a separate *al-Ḥasan → Ismāʿīl* source for the actual prophecy. That combination is the strongest case for treating al-Aʿmash—not Ibn Maghrāʾ—as the author of the *false-independence maneuver*.

## Matn comparison: what al-Aʿmash retained and what changed

The Jābir family does not preserve the elaborate **pulpit and negotiations** recension of al-Bukhārī 2704, nor the **prayer and child-climbing** story common in the Mubārak and Ismāʿīl branches. It isolates the portable Prophetic core: **“my son is a *sayyid*” + “God will reconcile two Muslim factions through him.”** The same core appears without a Companion in al-Ḥasan’s *mursal* transmission from Ismāʿīl b. Abī al-Ṣalt (*al-Maṭālib* 4743) and within several Abū Bakra-attributed Basran recensions.[^12][^26]

What matters is the **shared predicate** and the **disappearance of the narrative frame**. The proposed reconstruction does not require al-Aʿmash to adopt the entire Basran scene. He could borrow the short maxim—whether directly from Ismāʿīl b. Muslim, from Ismāʿīl’s associates—and give it the replacement authority of his known transmitter **Abū Sufyān**, followed by **Jābir**. Abū Sufyān really was a transmitter of Jābir material generally; that is precisely what makes the substitute chain plausible to subsequent readers.[^31]

## Al-Aʿmash’s wider transmission profile and the prior HadithCritic studies

Three earlier HadithCritic articles supply context for this reading, although they have different evidentiary weight.

In <ArticleLink id="transmission-narrators/78-the-scroll-and-the-sword-the-polemical-use-of-alis-authority">“The Scroll and the Sword: The Polemical Use of ʿAlī’s Authority”</ArticleLink>, the al-Aʿmash cluster of the *Ṣaḥīfat ʿAlī* complex is interpreted as a distinctive Kufan adaptation of a shared documentary frame: it uses ʿAlī’s supposed document to articulate rules about sanctuary, clientage and authority in a specific Iraqi political setting. That is a **previous historical-critical reconstruction of textual repurposing** by the same narrator, not an independently verified record of his confessing fabrication.[^33]

<HadithCriticBlog id="transmission-narrators/78-the-scroll-and-the-sword-the-polemical-use-of-alis-authority" />

The <ArticleLink id="transmission-narrators/41-isnad-politics-the-case-of-al-amash-and-hisham">“Isnād Politics: al-Aʿmash, Hishām b. ʿUrwa, and the Bewitchment Report”</ArticleLink> study identifies a thin Zayd b. Arqam collateral route through al-Aʿmash that shares the underlying bewitchment story with the more heavily attested ʿĀʾisha/Hishām family, while changing the action and foregrounding ʿAlī. It also assembles classical biographical statements that al-Aʿmash was trustworthy yet engaged in **tadlīs**. The article itself cautions that direct borrowing is plausible rather than proven. Its value here is methodological: **a known mudallis, a shared narrative nucleus, and a different Companion chain do not automatically create a genuinely independent witness**.[^34]

For the present report, we have something more specific than those analogies: **an al-Aʿmash control case from Abū Ḥātim involving the same possible replacement intermediaries, and an actual Ismāʿīl transmission family containing the very prophecy**. Accordingly, the reconstruction advanced here is:

1. The reconciliation maxim developed and spread in al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī’s Basran milieu after 41 AH.
2. One Basran carrier was Ismāʿīl b. Muslim, most plausibly identifiable here as al-Makkī, who was known to al-Aʿmash and transmitted a related child-and-prayer version of the prophecy.
3. Al-Aʿmash’s Kufan redaction retained the distinctive *sayyid* and reconciliation kernel but issued it with an apparent independent authority, **Abū Sufyān → Jābir**, rather than acknowledging its Basran literary ancestry.

## What the prophecy accomplished

The report's wording makes more sense as a political evaluation than as a neutral forecast. Its defining element is that the Prophet is made to call al-Ḥasan a **sayyid** specifically because God would bring about reconciliation through him.

If the opponents of the settlement could call al-Ḥasan the “humiliator of the believers,” the hadith supplies a perfectly balanced rejoinder: the Prophet had already recognized his nobility. If militant factions viewed compromise with Muʿāwiya as an abandonment of ʿAlī's claim, the prophecy transforms restraint into a distinguishing virtue. If later Muslim writers needed an exemplary precedent for avoiding civil war, the Prophet's own grandson becomes the model, and both armies are identified as **factions of Muslims** rather than as a party of faithful believers opposing unbelievers.[^25]

The report need not be straightforward pro-Umayyad propaganda. Its most obvious function is **pro-reconciliation** and **pro-al-Ḥasan**. It defends a descendant of Muḥammad against a specific criticism. That framing could subsequently be welcomed by groups emphasizing community unity, including writers who preferred to read the First Fitna without condemning one entire side as outside Islam. The political usefulness of the hadith was therefore broad and durable.

This ideological setting also explains why Abū Bakra is such an apt named authority in the Basran lines. His established literary persona as a *fitna*-averse Companion would lend the prophecy a recognizable moral ancestry. Yet, the report's survival through his name, the alternative Anas attribution, and multiple *mursal* versions cautions against tracing its precise wording automatically back to him.

## Reconstructing the development of the report

The combined historical and transmission evidence permits a reasonably concrete reconstruction.

**First**, the settlement of 41 AH produced competing judgments about al-Ḥasan's character and political decision. The later historical sources preserve harsh criticism from disappointed supporters. This is the event to which the prophecy supplies an answer.

**Second**, within the first/seventh-century and early second/eighth-century Basran world, the idea circulated that al-Ḥasan's refusal to continue the civil war was noble rather than humiliating. **Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī** is the best recoverable point at which the Prophetic reconciliation saying spread through multiple pupils. The event occurred within his lifetime, and its anti-*fitna* meaning fits the political concerns of his Iraqi milieu.

**Third**, the upper attribution was not uniform. Some pupils report the saying through Abū Bakra, one named transmission substitutes Anas, and several preserve it *mursalan*. The strongest version in al-Bukhārī explicitly claims al-Ḥasan heard Abū Bakra, but the divergent reports prevent the upper chain from being treated as a universally fixed element of the tradition.

**Fourth**, different branches elaborated the scene and the prediction. The Prophet appears with al-Ḥasan on the pulpit, or while preaching, or during prayer with the child climbing onto him. The wording ranges from “if he lives” to “perhaps” or “I hope,” and sometimes to “God certainly will.” Some versions add an unmistakable retrospective gloss about al-Ḥasan's tenure or recite the detailed account of the settlement immediately before the alleged prediction.

**Fifth**, the most defensible candidate for creating the **false-independent Jābir dive is al-Aʿmash**. Several alleged pupils retain al-Aʿmash’s distinct Companion attribution, whereas no independent route attests this political maxim from Jābir or Abū Sufyān without al-Aʿmash. The direct personal contact needed for borrowing is concrete: al-Aʿmash is named as a student of **Ismāʿīl b. Muslim al-Makkī**, whose transmissions from al-Ḥasan include the reconciliation prediction and closely related childhood accounts. Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s *ʿIlal* also preserves a comparison in an unrelated report where **Ismāʿīl and Abū Sufyān compete as the immediate source of al-Aʿmash’s information from the al-Ḥasan network**, with Abū Ḥātim suspecting a mistake from al-Aʿmash. These facts make al-Aʿmash’s replacement of the Basran source with **Abū Sufyān → Jābir** a positive historical reconstruction, not simply a suspicion based on his reputation.[^26][^27][^29]

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">

The reconciliation hadith fits the aftermath of **41 AH**. The leader whom disappointed followers could denounce as having humiliated the believers becomes, in the hadith, the grandson whose distinguishing nobility consists in reconciling believers. The strongest surviving Basran transmission network radiates from **al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī**, who was alive when the treaty occurred and whose pupils preserve the statement through **competing upper attributions** and different narrative settings.

On this evidence, the most persuasive explanation is that the prophecy was **formulated or given its recognizable Prophetic form after the settlement**, and that al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's Basran circle was central to its circulation. The subsequent attachment to Abū Bakra furnished an apt authority from the same anti-*fitna* milieu. The separate Jābir family is now best explained as an **al-Aʿmash-generated false-independent isnād**. Its multiple reported pupils demonstrate circulation from al-Aʿmash, not independent survival from Jābir. The identifiable bridge through **Ismāʿīl b. Muslim**, the striking *ʿIlal* comparison where **Abū Sufyān and Ismāʿīl alternate beneath al-Aʿmash**, the Jābir matn’s extraction of the Basran kernel, and the questionable written/limited hearing behind Abū Sufyān’s Jābir material support this specific historical model. Its mechanism is inferred, not admitted by al-Aʿmash in any surviving testimony.

In short, what the hadith can be seen doing most clearly is **not predicting an unknown future**. It instead is **turning a disputed past into divinely anticipated history**. Its transmission variations preserve the traces of that transformation.

</VerdictBox>
