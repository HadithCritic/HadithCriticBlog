*In 122 AH, the people of Kūfa were ordered into the Great Mosque to prevent an uprising. A famous Kūfan transmitter would later be credited with a warning that people would gather in mosques while not one of them was a believer. Could the prediction have been a verdict on his own world, subsequently projected into the distant past?*

## I. The mosque that became a prison

In 122 AH (739–740 CE), an Alid named **Zayd b. ʿAlī** prepared a rebellion in Kūfa against Umayyad rule. The governor of Iraq, Yūsuf b. ʿUmar al-Thaqafī, learned that Zayd's supporters were planning to rise. Rather than simply meeting them in battle, he instructed his subordinate in Kūfa, al-Ḥakam b. al-Ṣalt, to gather the city's inhabitants inside its Great Mosque. Al-Ṭabarī's *Tārīkh* gives us a striking account of the order:[^1]

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Ṭabarī" source="Tārīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk, events of 122 AH, Dār al-Maʿārif ed., vol. 7, p. 181" sourceUrl="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/18157/181">

"He ordered him to gather the people of Kūfa in the Great Mosque and confine them there."

**<Arabic>فَأَمَرَهُ أَنْ يَجْمَعَ أَهْلَ الْكُوفَةِ فِي الْمَسْجِدِ الْأَعْظَمِ يَحْصُرَهُمْ فِيهِ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

Al-Ṭabarī continues that the city's officials and armed men compelled the people to assemble. A proclamation threatened anyone found at home: *"Whoever we find in his dwelling will no longer enjoy our protection."* On Tuesday, the day before Zayd's intended uprising, the people went into the mosque. When Zayd emerged with a small force and asked where his supporters were, he was told that the people were confined in the mosque.[^1] Ibn Kathīr repeats this episode and preserves Zayd's supporters calling out to the Kūfans to come to religion and honor, declaring that the people possessed neither. These are partisan accounts, composed and transmitted long after the event; they are not a verified transcript of every act and conversation in 122 AH.[^2]

That historical picture is remarkable beside the report which concerns us:

<HadithBlock source="Ibn Abī Shayba, al-Muṣannaf, 30992 and 38742; al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak, 8365" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/ibnabishaybah/28/6">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"A time will come upon the people when they gather and pray in mosques, yet **there will not be a believer among them**."

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>يَأْتِي عَلَى النَّاسِ زَمَانٌ يَجْتَمِعُونَ وَيُصَلُّونَ فِي الْمَسَاجِدِ وَلَيْسَ فِيهِمْ مُؤْمِنٌ</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The juxtaposition raises an obvious historical possibility: **a warning about ostensibly Muslim congregations without genuine believers could have been formulated in a community where the mosque had become an instrument of political control and where religious commitments were violently tested**. But notice the limits immediately. Al-Ṭabarī's account describes confinement **in a mosque**; it does not say the captives gathered there **to pray**, and no early source explicitly connects this incident with the #63 saying. The match supplies a possible **historical occasion or polemical resonance**, not a demonstrated origin date. We must therefore bring the chains and wording into the investigation rather than declaring the case solved by a vivid parallel.[^1][^3]

## II. The common link was alive in this Kūfa

The figure who matters is **Sulaymān b. Mihrān al-Aʿmash**, born around 61 AH and deceased around 147–148 AH. He lived through the government of al-Ḥajjāj, the suppression of Zayd's revolt in 122 AH, and the Abbasid seizure of power in 132 AH. As a teacher of hadith and Qurʾānic recitation, he occupied a prominent position in Kūfa's scholarly community. His reported pupils included **Shuʿba b. al-Ḥajjāj**, **Sufyān al-Thawrī**, and **Fuḍayl b. ʿIyāḍ**—precisely the three men through whom our short saying can be independently recovered.[^4]

That last fact is crucial. We are not dealing with a report found in one late book attributed to an obscure transmitter. The wording recurs through separate pupils of a named Kūfan teacher. This makes it historically credible that al-Aʿmash **circulated** the short form. Whether he first **composed** it is a further proposition.

The biographical tradition actually connects al-Aʿmash to Zayd's uprising. In an anecdote reported by Ḥafṣ b. Ghiyāth and preserved by al-Dhahabī, someone asked him, during Zayd's days, why he did not join the rising. His response was not a promise of unconditional revolutionary loyalty:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Aʿmash, as transmitted through Ḥafṣ b. Ghiyāth" source="Al-Dhahabī, Siyar Aʿlām al-Nubalāʾ, biography of al-Aʿmash" sourceUrl="https://islamweb.net/ar/library/content/60/1040/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D8%B9%D9%85%D8%B4">

"Woe to you! By God, I do not know anyone for whose sake I would risk my honor—so how could I risk my religion for his sake?"

**<Arabic>وَيْلَكُمْ! وَاللَّهِ مَا أَعْرِفُ أَحَدًا أَجْعَلُ عِرْضِي دُونَهُ، فَكَيْفَ أَجْعَلُ دِينِي دُونَهُ؟</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

A retrospective biographical report, of course, cannot tell us his every political conviction. It does tell us that later remembered al-Aʿmash as a scholar making **judgments about religious fidelity in the midst of Kūfan upheaval**, but cautious about taking up arms. *Encyclopaedia Iranica* also documents competing Alid and Shiʿi portrayals of him, including support for the Prophet's family and material associated with the subsequent rebellions of 145 AH. Those portrayals vary in their sectarian commitments and cannot all be accepted at face value.[^5]

There is another report of equal interpretive value. According to a tradition cited by al-Dhahabī through ʿĪsā b. Yūnus, al-Aʿmash claimed to have seen the Companion Anas b. Mālik but declined to take hadith directly from him because of Anas's association with al-Ḥajjāj. He is made to say, in effect: "You served the Messenger of God, then you came to al-Ḥajjāj and he appointed you."[^4] We should not turn a late preserved anecdote into direct proof of an actual encounter. Its *reported attitude*, however, is entirely relevant: an individual could possess impressive religious standing and yet be condemned for political association.

This tension is much closer to our hadith than a generalized complaint about society being wicked. **Attendance, recitation, companionship, and outward religious credentials were not necessarily the same thing as true faith.**

## III. Faith was itself a contested word

A second context overlapped the political conflicts. Early Kūfa was one of the major centers of dispute over **īmān**, its relation to actions, and whether serious wrongdoing affected one's status as a believer. Historians describe the emergence of *irjāʾ* first within disputes over judgment of early Muslim combatants and political loyalties, and its transformation in Iraq into an articulated theological teaching. Joseph Givony's historical study of the Murjiʾa and the early Abū Ḥanīfa movement places these disputes in Kūfa's unstable political, legal, and religious milieu.[^6]

It is important **not** to caricature the Murjiʾa as teaching that everyone who physically enters a mosque must be a sincere believer. Early *irjāʾ* was not monolithic, and its adherents did not all deny the obligation of religious actions. The basic issue was how confession, interior assent, action, and judgment of other Muslims were related. The language of #63 nevertheless has an unmistakable polemical capacity: even a congregation performing recognizable Muslim rites may possess **no true īmān**.

One named figure makes the context considerably less abstract. **ʿUmar b. Dharr** (d. circa 153–157 AH), a preacher and traditionist active in Kūfa, was associated with *irjāʾ*. A later biographical report even puts him and al-Aʿmash in direct disagreement. When ʿUmar was preaching and invited al-Aʿmash to approach, the latter is reported to have said:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Aʿmash, in a biographical report of ʿUmar b. Dharr" source="Cited from Tārīkh al-Muntajālī in Ikmāl Tahdhīb al-Kamāl; narrator dossier" sourceUrl="https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/rawi/p-4680">

"I am here following the Sunna; you are engaged in an innovation."

**<Arabic>أَنَا هُنَا فِي سُنَّةٍ وَأَنْتَ فِي بِدْعَةٍ</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

That exchange is striking because **ʿUmar b. Dharr appears later in the alternative prophetic isnād of our very tradition**. However, the anecdote's immediate context concerns **storytelling or preaching (*qaṣaṣ*)**, not a demonstrable debate over this hadith or even necessarily over the doctrine of *irjāʾ*. It cannot be cited as proof that the two men were disputing #63. Its significance is more limited but important: these were not isolated names in disconnected chains; they belonged to the same disputed religious world.[^7]

The younger Sufyān al-Thawrī—one of the actual pupils who preserved #63 from al-Aʿmash—is independently depicted as distancing himself from ʿUmar b. Dharr's circle. Ibn Saʿd reports that after ʿUmar died, Sufyān and al-Ḥasan b. Ṣāliḥ did not attend his funeral because of his *irjāʾ*. This is a later retrospective testimony about the pupil's position, **not a cause we can prove for al-Aʿmash's initial wording**.[^8]

### How did later scholars actually use the saying?

Here the evidence is much firmer than attempts to reconstruct private motives. Al-Ājurrī places the report among proof-texts about the **increase and decrease of faith** in *al-Sharīʿa*, and al-Khallāl reproduces a version where the worshippers are **reciting the Qurʾān** inside a long sequence of material about actions, faith and hypocrisy.[^9] Al-Khallāl's context includes sayings that distinguish outward profession from true faith. One adjacent statement attributed to Ḥudhayfa defines a hypocrite as a person who describes Islam yet does not practice it. A separate al-Aʿmash → Abū Wāʾil → Ḥudhayfa report declares the hypocrites of its speaker's day worse than those of Muhammad's because earlier hypocrites concealed their hypocrisy while later ones displayed it.[^9]

<HadithBlock source="al-Khallāl, al-Sunna, no. 1643, via Wakīʿ → al-Aʿmash → Abū Wāʾil → Ḥudhayfa" url="https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A7%D8%A8_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%B3%D9%86%D8%A9_%D9%84%D9%84%D8%AE%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%84/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AC%D8%B2%D8%A1_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AE%D8%A7%D9%85%D8%B3">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"The hypocrites among you are worse than the hypocrites in the time of the Messenger of God." When asked why, the response is that those earlier hypocrites hid their hypocrisy, while these openly display it.

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>الْمُنَافِقُونَ الَّذِينَ فِيكُمْ شَرٌّ مِنَ الْمُنَافِقِينَ الَّذِينَ كَانُوا عَلَى عَهْدِ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

This second text is a better **historical-literary comparator** than an arbitrary parallel about doom in the final age: it circulates through **al-Aʿmash himself**, conveys a similar judgment about the present community, and attributes that judgment to an earlier Companion. It does **not** prove that al-Aʿmash made up either text. It does show a *recognizable kind of religious rhetoric* in his documented transmission repertoire.

## IV. A specific hypothesis of formulation

We can now construct a motive hypothesis that accounts for more than the language of prophecy.

**Setting:** Kūfa after years of Umayyad coercion, amid sectarian disputes over who counted as a believer. In 122 AH an Umayyad governor actually confined the people in the Great Mosque as part of suppressing an Alid rebellion. Kūfan religious figures were remembered as debating the legitimacy of action versus caution. Within that same intellectual community, *irjāʾ* remained controversial.

**Potential audience:** scholars and their pupils who already regarded public forms of piety as insufficient proof of authentic religion, and who questioned the sincerity of those willing to submit to a coercive government or excuse the conduct of powerful Muslims.

**Potential message:** a communal assembly in the mosque, even accompanied by prayer or recitation, does not establish genuine faith. Religious labels can remain after religious substance has disappeared.

**Possible method:** instead of giving a direct political sermon naming the governor or Zayd, the speaker assigns this judgment to the safely distanced voice of an early Companion—ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr—under the futuristic formula "a time will come." That form can condemn contemporary realities without naming a living opponent and can circulate as moral admonition after the immediate political crisis passes.

The reconstruction is historically economical **if** one assumes the short saying arose during al-Aʿmash's career. But none of the extant #63 isnāds explicitly mentions **122 AH**, Zayd, al-Ḥajjāj, the Murjiʾa, or a particular political incident. Moreover, al-Aʿmash's early teacher Khaythama is a plausible conduit to ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr; this upper connection is not chronologically impossible. The exact initial context must therefore remain a hypothesis, not an established event.[^4][^10]

<ClaimBox title="Historical thesis">

**The recoverable short saying fits an argument over religious sincerity in al-Aʿmash's Kūfa and could plausibly be an ex-eventu moralization of experiences such as the 122 AH mosque confinement.** Its secure circulation begins at al-Aʿmash's transmission level. Its authorship before that point remains unverified.

</ClaimBox>

## V. The isnāds: where the actual evidence begins

The standard form is **not** originally a prophetic quotation in the independently corroborated transmission family. It stops with the Companion ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr b. al-ʿĀṣ, through his alleged pupil Khaythama b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, then al-Aʿmash. It is repeated by **three distinguishable pupils** of al-Aʿmash: Sufyān al-Thawrī, Shuʿba b. al-Ḥajjāj, and Fuḍayl b. ʿIyāḍ.[^11]

The source-critical value of that agreement is substantial. Three separate pupils point back to al-Aʿmash with the *same basic non-prophetic formulation*. They establish his role as a historical distributor. They do not prove that Khaythama never said it; they only leave that early claim without the same independent control.[^11]

One textual caveat matters: **al-Khallāl 1609 prints “ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar”** rather than the usual **ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr** and describes worshippers **reciting the Qurʾān**, not just praying. Since other copies of the same Sufyān/Wakīʿ cluster use Ibn ʿAmr, this is best treated as an unresolved transmitter/copyist variation, not instantly as a completely separate Companion tradition.[^9]

## VI. First variation: a Companion's warning becomes a prophecy of Muhammad

A narrow Ibn ʿAdī version retains the standard backbone—**al-Aʿmash → Khaythama → Ibn ʿAmr**—but attributes the saying explicitly to the Prophet. It passes through **Sufyān al-Thawrī → Rawwād b. al-Jarrāḥ**, a student whose recensions of Sufyān were criticized.[^12]

<HadithBlock source="Ibn ʿAdī, al-Kāmil, Rawwād recension; see Ibn al-Qaysarānī's criticism" url="https://dorar.net/h/2U37lt28?osoul=1">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“… Rawwād b. al-Jarrāḥ, from Sufyān, from al-Aʿmash, from Khaythama … from the Prophet.”

In this branch the short sentence is introduced as something Muhammad said, rather than as a statement of ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr.

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>... رَوَّادُ بْنُ الْجَرَّاحِ، عَنْ سُفْيَانَ، عَنِ الْأَعْمَشِ، عَنْ خَيْثَمَةَ ... عَنِ النَّبِيِّ</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The change is exceptionally informative because other students of **the very same Sufyān** preserve the non-prophetic form. Ibn al-Qaysarānī explicitly counted the raised version among the reports criticized in Rawwād's narrations from al-Thawrī.[^12] If secondary attribution arose at an identifiable level, **Rawwād or someone below him** is a stronger candidate than al-Aʿmash, whose broadly transmitted versions do not make the statement prophetic.

It is technically more precise to call this **rafʿ**, an elevation of attribution, than an *isnād dive*: the purported intermediate authorities are substantially preserved while the endpoint is changed.

## VII. The Mujāhid bypass: did Abū Muṭīʿ obtain a Kūfan saying and replace the isnād?

A second version recorded by Ibn ʿAdī preserves an entirely different upper transmission route:

**Prophet → ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr → Mujāhid → ʿUmar b. Dharr → Abū Muṭīʿ al-Balkhī → Muḥammad b. al-Qāsim → ʿUbayd b. Muḥammad b. Mūsā → Ibn ʿAdī.**[^13]

This recension does more than attach the sentence to Muhammad. The listeners reportedly ask *when* such a time will arrive, and the Prophet is said to explain:

<HadithBlock source="Ibn ʿAdī, al-Kāmil, 2:214; full chain and text" url="https://dorar.net/h/OgvOlHAH?osoul=1">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"When they consume usury and elevate their buildings ... They will say: 'There is no god but God,' and God will say: 'You lie; you are not truthful in saying it.'"

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِذَا أَكَلُوا الرِّبَا وَشَرَّفُوا الْبِنَاءَ ... فَإِذَا قَالُوا لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ قَالَ اللَّهُ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ: كَذَبْتُمْ لَسْتُمْ بِهَا بِصَادِقِينَ</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The social location is interesting. **ʿUmar b. Dharr** was a Kūfan preacher linked to *irjāʾ*, and al-Aʿmash was remembered as reproaching him. **Abū Muṭīʿ al-Ḥakam b. ʿAbd Allāh al-Balkhī** was closely connected to the eastern Ḥanafī milieu and is biographically associated with receiving hadith from **Sufyān al-Thawrī**. We have a genuine source-family connection:

- al-Aʿmash transmitted the *short non-prophetic saying* to Sufyān;
- Abū Muṭīʿ is named among those who reportedly transmitted from Sufyān;
- the longer Mujāhid line is first recovered in the examined corpus through Abū Muṭīʿ's narrow descent.

That creates a possible **donor path**: `al-Aʿmash → Sufyān → Abū Muṭīʿ`, followed by the claim of an alternative upper route through `ʿUmar → Mujāhid → Ibn ʿAmr → Prophet`. If this is a dive, **Abū Muṭīʿ** is the most convenient currently visible place to investigate its formation, although **Muḥammad b. al-Qāsim** could instead have introduced it. Ibn ʿAdī's own assessment of Abū Muṭīʿ says that much of his hadith goes uncorroborated; this weighs against treating the lone form as securely independent, without identifying its author by itself.[^13][^14]

Equally, **ʿUmar b. Dharr** could genuinely have uttered or transmitted an analogous version: the reported relationship between him and Mujāhid is not automatically chronologically impossible, and ʿUmar himself had other pupils. We lack a second independent pupil of ʿUmar carrying *this particular wording*. That absence is a limit to confidence, not evidence of an impossibility.

## VIII. The other apparent diversions

The remaining variants help identify *how* the motif evolved, but they are not all equivalent to the Mujāhid route.

Al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr* no. 14413 presents an unusual line through **Abū al-Faḍl al-Qurashī** which says people will respond to the call to prayer but are not believers. Abū Nuʿaym transmits that very chain through al-Ṭabarānī, so two **books** do not mean two **independent pupils** of al-Aʿmash.[^15]

The alternative **Ḥudhayfa → Abū Wāʾil** route is still an **al-Aʿmash** route because it descends through **al-Aʿmash → Sufyān**, then **Ismāʿīl b. ʿAyyāsh**. Later hadith critics specifically regarded Ismāʿīl's material from non-Syrian teachers as problematic; since Sufyān and al-Aʿmash were Kūfan, this is a more pointed concern than simply saying a narrator was weak.[^16]

The **Mālik → Nāfiʿ → Ibn ʿUmar** variant survives with the striking detail of **more than one thousand worshippers**. Its comparatively late transmission through al-Qāsim al-Farghānī is poorly controlled, and its different wording reduces our ability to prove that it copies the exact al-Aʿmash formula. It is a collateral tradition, not a decisive early refutation of the main CL or a proven dive.[^17]

## IX. What the case does—and does not—show about al-Aʿmash

Previous HadithCritic studies have already examined the way al-Aʿmash appears in politically or theologically charged transmission dossiers. In *Isnād Politics: al-Aʿmash, Hishām b. ʿUrwa, and the Bewitchment Report*, the issue was a narrow Kūfan collateral route alongside a broader Medinan dossier. In *The Scroll and the Sword*, the issue was the use of ʿAlī's authority in competing legal and political formulations. In *Al-Ḥasan's Reconciliation Prophecy and al-Aʿmash's Kufan Dive*, the argument concerned a plausible Kūfan reuse of a widely transmitted Basran prophetic saying.[^18]

Those investigations provide a **comparative hypothesis about transmission behavior**, not admissible proof that a person was "caught" manufacturing *every* other report that happens to pass through him. The historical method requires us to re-establish the relevant link for #63. The case has unusually strong material for doing so:

1. A short **Companion-level saying** transmitted from al-Aʿmash by three separate pupils.
2. A **politically precise contemporary event** in which Kūfans were confined in the Great Mosque before Zayd's revolt.
3. A **theologically contested environment** where outward Muslim identity, worship, and genuine faith were disputed.
4. An **al-Aʿmash-transmitted parallel** declaring the hypocrites of the present worse than those in the Prophet's time.
5. Several **thin later prophetic versions**, some with visible signs of change at identifiable lower transmitters.

This combination makes **authorship or substantial reformulation by al-Aʿmash a serious historical possibility**, not a gratuitous accusation. The two strongest alternatives remain: **Khaythama really inherited the statement from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr**, or al-Aʿmash inherited a shorter circulating maxim and adapted it to his own milieu. Nothing in the surviving #63 text decisively excludes either. The historical motive can elevate a proposal from speculation to a plausible reconstruction; it cannot replace missing evidence of the earlier chain.

The **diversions** are a distinct issue. They can be secondary **even if al-Aʿmash did not fabricate the initial maxim**. The later expansion of a terse Companion warning into an elaborate prophetic sermon is independently visible as a textual phenomenon. In fact, the evidence for such **prophetic elevation after al-Aʿmash** is stronger than the evidence that al-Aʿmash deliberately forged the initial upper isnād.

## X. The likely sequence of development

Our best working model separates three moments rather than calling everything a single fabrication:

**Stage 1 — Kūfan warning, potentially in the 120s–140s AH.** A critique of religious emptiness enters scholarly circulation, perhaps sharpened by the 122 AH mosque confinement and contemporaneous disputes about *īmān*. Al-Aʿmash is its first securely recoverable distributor. A Companion attribution to Ibn ʿAmr is attached in all of the strongest surviving pupil branches.

**Stage 2 — Stable transmission below al-Aʿmash.** Sufyān, Shuʿba, and Fuḍayl carry variants of the nonprophetic warning to their students. Small details change, but the shared core survives, including the crucial refusal to attribute it directly to Muhammad.

**Stage 3 — Propheticization and multiplication of sources.** Narrower later recensions elevate the short warning to the Prophet; another branch elaborates it with usury, architecture, and false confession; still others replace Ibn ʿAmr with Ḥudhayfa or Ibn ʿUmar, or change the setting to the call to prayer. A possible **Abū Muṭīʿ → ʿUmar b. Dharr → Mujāhid** dive is worth investigating, but the direction of transmission and individual responsibility have not been proved.

<VerdictBox title="Historical verdict">

The #63 saying is **securely traceable to al-Aʿmash's transmission circle**, where it was normally a Companion's statement—not a Prophetic prediction. Kūfa's forced Great Mosque assembly in 122 AH, its disputes over political allegiance, and arguments about *īmān* provide an unusually concrete historical setting in which a warning of this sort could have been formulated or repurposed. **Al-Aʿmash's personal authorship is plausible, but not proven.** The strongest transmission-critical conclusion is that the **later prophetic ascriptions and elaborate versions are secondary candidates**, with Rawwād's raised Sufyān recension and Abū Muṭīʿ's Mujāhid bypass deserving particular scrutiny.

</VerdictBox>
