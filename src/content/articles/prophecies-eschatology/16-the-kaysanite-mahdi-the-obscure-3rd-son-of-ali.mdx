## The Report

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">لَوْ لَمْ يَبْقَ مِنَ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا يَوْمٌ، لَبَعَثَ اللهُ عَزَّ وَجَلَّ رَجُلًا مِنَّا يَمْلَؤُهَا عَدْلًا كَمَا مُلِئَتْ جَوْرًا.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“If only one day remained of the world, God would send a man from us who would fill it with justice as it had been filled with oppression.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

The ʿAlī Mahdī reports are usually read backward from later Sunnī doctrine. A reader already knows the later Mahdī and therefore assumes that the report is simply about a future end-time redeemer from the Prophet’s household. That is not how the report looks when it is placed in its earliest political setting.

The wording is strikingly broad. It does not say “from the descendants of Fāṭima.” It does not name al-Ḥasan or al-Ḥusayn. It does not give the familiar later name formula, “his name matches my name and his father’s name matches my father’s name.” It says only that a man **from us**, or **from my family**, will arise and fill the world with justice after oppression.

That is exactly the kind of wording that could support **Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya**, the third famous son of ʿAlī, whose followers made him a messianic figure after the trauma of Karbalāʾ and the revolt of al-Mukhtār in Kūfa.

<ClaimBox>
The strongest historical reading is that this report began as Kaysanite or proto-Kaysanite Alid propaganda. The Abū al-Ṭufayl route supplies the ideal Companion-level witness: a late surviving Companion, a partisan of ʿAlī, and a figure remembered as connected to al-Mukhtār’s movement. If the report did not arise below him, Abū al-Ṭufayl is not a neutral transmitter. He is the motivated origin point.
</ClaimBox>

The article does not need to claim that every later collector knew the original polemic. Later Sunnī reception could preserve the report as a generic Mahdī text after its first political target had faded. The task is to explain why this particular wording, through this particular network, appears where it does.

## Two ʿAlī Reports, One Kaysanite Problem

The surviving material forms two related families.

The first is the **Abū al-Ṭufayl family**, preserved in Abū Dāwūd and Aḥmad:

> ʿAlī → Abū al-Ṭufayl → al-Qāsim b. Abī Bazzah → Fiṭr b. Khalīfa → later collectors

Its main wording is the justice-filling report: if only one day remained, God would send a man from the Prophet’s family to fill the earth with justice.

The second is the **Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya family**, preserved in Ibn Mājah and Aḥmad:

> ʿAlī → Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya → Ibrāhīm b. Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya → Yāsīn al-ʿIjlī → later collectors

Its wording is shorter and even more revealing:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">الْمَهْدِيُّ مِنَّا أَهْلَ الْبَيْتِ، يُصْلِحُهُ اللهُ فِي لَيْلَةٍ.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The Mahdī is from us, the people of the Household. God will rectify him in one night.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This second route does not pass through Abū al-Ṭufayl, but it names the political world that makes the first route intelligible. A report about the Mahdī from the Household is transmitted through the family of Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya itself. That does not prove the Abū al-Ṭufayl report by independent attestation. It shows that the same ʿAlid-Hanafid memory world preserved Mahdī material in more than one channel.

<ContextNote>
In strict isnād-cum-matn terms, Fiṭr b. Khalīfa is the visible lower partial common link for the Abū al-Ṭufayl family in the extant books. Abū al-Ṭufayl is the claimed Companion-level bottleneck. The historical argument is that this is exactly where the motive lies: not in a random later transmitter inventing an abstract Mahdī, but in an Alid partisan whose political world already centered on ʿAlī’s surviving son, Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya.
</ContextNote>

## Why Abū al-Ṭufayl Matters

Abū al-Ṭufayl ʿĀmir b. Wāthila is not just a name in a chain. He is one of the few Companion figures whose political profile matters directly for the report. He was remembered as the last surviving Companion to have seen the Prophet, a supporter of ʿAlī, and a participant in ʿAlid politics. The historical and biographical reports also remember him as connected with al-Mukhtār b. Abī ʿUbayd, the Kūfan rebel who built his movement around Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya.

The point is not merely that Abū al-Ṭufayl “liked ʿAlī.” Many transmitters did. The sharper point is that the report’s wording is precisely the kind of wording needed by the Kaysanite cause. It allows the promised figure to be from ʿAlī’s house without restricting him to the Fāṭimid Ḥasanid or Ḥusaynid line. That is what makes Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya possible.

Later Sunnī Mahdī doctrine often solved the problem by placing the Mahdī through Fāṭima, usually through Ḥasan or Ḥusayn. This report does not. It preserves an earlier, less specified Alid expectation. That lack of specification is not a weakness in the Kaysanite reading. It is the clue.

## The Kaysanite Setting

The Kaysanite movement grew out of the political and theological aftermath of Karbalāʾ. Al-Mukhtār’s revolt in Kūfa, 66–67 AH, claimed to avenge al-Ḥusayn’s blood and mobilized support in the name of Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya. Muḥammad himself may have remained cautious or ambiguous, but the movement surrounding his name was not cautious. It presented him as the rightful leader from the House of ʿAlī.

After Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya died, some of his followers refused to treat his death as final. In later heresiographical memory, a Kaysanite strand claimed that he was hidden at Mount Raḍwā and would return. This is one of the earliest Islamic settings where **Mahdī**, hidden return, and the restoration of justice converge around a named Alid figure.

That matters for the hadith because the report does not need to say “Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya” explicitly. It only needs to say enough to make him fit:

This is why the report should not be treated as an isolated pious saying. It is a theological solution to a factional problem. The Kaysanite movement needed a way to turn Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s ambiguous public role into a divinely timed mission. “God will rectify him in one night” does exactly that.

## The Matn Is Not Fāṭimid Enough for Later Sunnism

A useful way to test the report is to compare what it does **not** say. It does not contain the later Sunnī clause found in Abū Dāwūd 4284, where the Mahdī is said to be from the descendants of Fāṭima. It does not contain the Ibn Masʿūd name formula, “his name matches my name.” It does not tie the Mahdī to al-Ḥasan. It does not tie him to al-Ḥusayn. It does not specify a father named ʿAbdallāh. It is open-textured.

That openness makes sense before later Mahdī doctrine hardened. In the earliest Alid messianic environment, several claimants could be imagined. The report’s vagueness is therefore politically functional. It lets the hearer complete the referent from the factional setting.

For a Kaysanite audience, the completion is obvious: the man from ʿAlī’s house who will bring justice is Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya.

## Fiṭr b. Khalīfa: The Lower Proliferator

Fiṭr b. Khalīfa is the key lower transmitter in the Abū al-Ṭufayl family. Classical critics do not present him as a simple liar. Some call him trustworthy or acceptable. But his sectarian profile is exactly what matters here. He is a Kūfan transmitter, repeatedly described with Shīʿī leanings, and in some reports more sharply as a **khashabī** or extreme Shīʿī. Abū Bakr b. ʿAyyāsh is reported to have avoided him because of his bad doctrine. Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal is reported to have regarded him as reliable in hadith while still noting his Shīʿism.

This is the same pattern we meet in other politically useful reports. The problem is not that the lower transmitter is automatically worthless. The problem is that a politically ideal matn is carried by a politically fitted network. Fiṭr is the sort of transmitter who could preserve, polish, or promote an Alid Mahdī report without needing to invent the whole thing himself.

The Abū al-Ṭufayl layer gives the motive. Fiṭr gives the later Kūfan channel.

## The Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya Family Route

The Ibn Mājah and Aḥmad route is often treated as a separate corroboration. It is better read as a collateral witness to the same ideological environment.

The chain runs through Ibrāhīm b. Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya from his father, Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya, from ʿAlī. Its wording says that the Mahdī is from the Household and that God will rectify him in one night. The family line is the entire point. The Mahdī saying is being carried through the son of the very figure whose followers made him a Mahdī.

This is not a neutral accident. If a tradition about the Mahdī from the Household travels through Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s own family, then the report’s first political resonance is hard to miss. Later hadith grading may ask whether the isnād is acceptable. Historical analysis asks why this family would preserve this wording and what it would have meant in the first century after Karbalāʾ.

## “God Will Rectify Him in One Night”

The phrase **“God will rectify him in one night”** is especially useful for the Kaysanite case. Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya was not a straightforward revolutionary commander like al-Mukhtār. The sources often show him as cautious, pressured, claimed by others, or reluctant to endorse the full violence of the movement done in his name.

That ambiguity created a theological problem. How can a quiet or hesitant figure be the promised leader?

The hadith solves it. God does not need a long public revolutionary career. God can rectify, prepare, or set right the Mahdī in a single night. A man who has not yet acted like the redeemer can still become the redeemer when the divine moment arrives.

That is why this wording fits Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya better than it fits a generic future saint. It explains delay. It explains ambiguity. It explains why the claimant can be present, known, and not yet openly acting.

## From Kaysanite Mahdī to Abbasid Daʿwa

The Kaysanite afterlife also explains why these ideas survived. The movement around Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya did not disappear after al-Mukhtār’s defeat. Later Hāshimite and Abbasid ideology drew on channels associated with Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s son, Abū Hāshim. The famous claim that Abū Hāshim transmitted the imamate or daʿwa to the Abbasid line gave the Abbasids a way to inherit a portion of the earlier Alid-Hanafid religious capital.

So the trajectory is not:

> A timeless prophecy appears, and only later people apply it politically.

The better reconstruction is:

> Alid messianic claims formed in the first/seventh century; some were attached to Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya; those claims survived through Kaysanite-Hāshimite channels; later Sunnī books preserved some of the reports after the original factional target had become less visible.

## Could It Have Been Created Below Abū al-Ṭufayl?

Yes, and the article should be honest about that. The extant collector layer does not show a large fan-out directly from Abū al-Ṭufayl. The Abū al-Ṭufayl report is mainly visible through al-Qāsim b. Abī Bazzah and Fiṭr b. Khalīfa, with a small variation through Ḥabīb b. Abī Thābit reported in Aḥmad. That means a strict common-link argument cannot simply say, “Abū al-Ṭufayl is proven to be the common link in the same way ʿĀṣim is for the Ibn Masʿūd report.”

The more defensible claim is layered:

1. **The report’s claimed Companion-level bottleneck is Abū al-Ṭufayl.**
2. **Abū al-Ṭufayl has the right political motive and setting.**
3. **The lower proliferation is through Kūfan Shīʿī-friendly channels.**
4. **A collateral family route through Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s own descendants preserves a compatible Mahdī claim.**

That layered case is stronger than saying “Fiṭr fabricated it” or “Ibrāhīm fabricated it.” It explains both the isnād and the political meaning. It also explains why the report could later be accepted in Sunnī compilations: the wording was broad enough to be detached from the Kaysanite referent.

## Objections

### “Abū al-Ṭufayl is a Companion, so he cannot be criticized.”

That is a theological rule inside later Sunnī hadith theory, not a historical method. The historical question is not whether a Companion is automatically upright in doctrine. The question is whether this specific report, with this specific wording, is more likely to preserve seventh-century Prophetic speech or first-century Alid factional memory. Abū al-Ṭufayl’s known pro-ʿAlid profile is directly relevant because the report functions as pro-ʿAlid messianic legitimation.

### “The report only says Ahl al-Bayt, not Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya.”

That is exactly why it works. A factional report does not always name the claimant. It often supplies a description that the intended audience can complete. “From us,” “from my family,” and “fills the earth with justice” were enough for a Kaysanite audience whose messianic focus was already Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya.

### “The later Mahdī is usually Fāṭimid.”

Yes, and that is part of the argument. Later Sunnī and Shīʿī Mahdī traditions often move toward more specific Fāṭimid, Ḥasanid, Ḥusaynid, or name-formula identifiers. The Abū al-Ṭufayl report is earlier-looking precisely because it lacks those later restrictions. Its vagueness fits the period before the doctrine was standardized.

### “Ibn Mājah 4085 is a separate report.”

It is. It should not be collapsed into the Abū al-Ṭufayl chain. But it is still important because it is a Mahdī report carried through Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s family. That makes it a contextual witness. It shows that this family and its circles were an obvious home for Mahdī language.

### “Fiṭr was considered reliable by some critics.”

That is true. The argument does not require declaring Fiṭr a liar. His value lies in his location. A trustworthy or semi-trustworthy transmitter can still carry a partisan report. Rijāl reliability and historical origin are not the same question.

## The Best Reconstruction

The most economical reconstruction is this.

A broad Alid Mahdī expectation formed in the aftermath of Karbalāʾ and the Mukhtārite revolt. In that setting, Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya could be presented as the promised figure from ʿAlī’s house who would restore justice. Abū al-Ṭufayl, remembered as a pro-ʿAlid Companion and connected to al-Mukhtār’s movement, is the ideal Companion-level voice for that claim. The report was then transmitted through al-Qāsim b. Abī Bazzah and especially Fiṭr b. Khalīfa, a Kūfan Shīʿī-leaning transmitter, before entering later Sunnī compilations.

The family route through Ibrāhīm b. Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya confirms the same ideological environment from another angle. It does not prove the Abū al-Ṭufayl route independently, but it shows that Mahdī language was preserved precisely where the Kaysanite reading would predict: in the circles of Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya’s own family.

<VerdictBox>
The ʿAlī Mahdī reports are best read as relics of early Alid messianism, not as clean Prophetic forecasts. The Abū al-Ṭufayl route gives the report a politically meaningful Companion-level bottleneck, while the Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya family route reveals the likely referent. The original force of the report was not “a generic Mahdī will come one day.” It was: ʿAlī’s house still has a divinely prepared restorer, and the most natural early candidate was Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya.
</VerdictBox>

## Sources consulted

1. **Abū al-Ṭufayl justice-filling family.** Abū Dāwūd, *Sunan*, no. 4283; Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, *Musnad*, no. 773 and related ʿAlī reports; compare the variant note in Aḥmad where the report is also heard through Ḥabīb b. Abī Thābit from Abū al-Ṭufayl.

2. **Ibn al-Ḥanafiyya family route.** Ibn Mājah, *Sunan*, no. 4085; Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, *Musnad*, no. 645: Yāsīn al-ʿIjlī from Ibrāhīm b. Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya from his father from ʿAlī.

3. **Abū al-Ṭufayl’s rijāl and political profile.** Ibn Saʿd, *al-Ṭabaqāt al-kubrā*; al-Dhahabī, *Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ*; Ibn Ḥajar, *Tahdhīb al-tahdhīb*; Ibn Kathīr, *al-Bidāya wa-l-nihāya*, biography of Abū al-Ṭufayl, where he is remembered as a partisan of ʿAlī and connected with al-Mukhtār.

4. **Fiṭr b. Khalīfa.** al-Dhahabī, *Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ*; al-ʿUqaylī, *al-Ḍuʿafāʾ*; Ibn ʿAdī, *al-Kāmil*; Ibn Ḥajar, *Tahdhīb al-tahdhīb* and *Taqrīb al-tahdhīb*. The key notices are that he was treated as usable by some critics while also being marked as Shīʿī, even sharply as *khashabī* in some reports.

5. **al-Mukhtār, Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya, and Kaysanite ideology.** al-Ṭabarī, *Tārīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk*; al-Balādhurī, *Ansāb al-ashrāf*; Abū al-Faraj al-Iṣfahānī, *Maqātil al-Ṭālibiyyīn*; al-Nawbakhtī, *Firaq al-Shīʿa*; al-Ashʿarī, *Maqālāt al-Islāmiyyīn*; al-Shahrastānī, *al-Milal wa-l-niḥal*.

6. **Modern scholarship.** Moshe Sharon, *Black Banners from the East*; Patricia Crone, “On the Meaning of the ʿAbbāsid Call to al-Riḍā”; Wilferd Madelung on early Shīʿī factions and the Zaydiyya; Farhad Daftary on the Kaysaniyya and early Shīʿism; S. A. Arjomand, “The Crisis of the Imamate and the Institution of Occultation”; David Cook, *Studies in Muslim Apocalyptic*.
