According to a well-known hadith[^1][^2], Muḥammad once stood before his followers with his grandson al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī and said:

<HadithBlock translation={"This son of mine is a sayyid [chief or leader], and perhaps God will reconcile through him two great factions of the Muslims."} source={"Ṣaḥīḥ, no. 2704"}  arabic={"إِنَّ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ وَلَعَلَّ اللَّهَ أَنْ يُصْلِحَ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ عَظِيمَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/53/9"} />

The alleged prophecy is not difficult to interpret. After his father ʿAlī's death, al-Ḥasan briefly held the caliphal claim in Iraq. Muʿāwiya, who controlled Syria, challenged him; al-Ḥasan eventually gave way. This agreement, conventionally dated to 41 AH (661 CE), became known as the reconciliation or settlement (ṣulḥ) of al-Ḥasan. The hadith seems to anticipate precisely that decision. This contested political act then became evidence that Muḥammad had foreseen al-Ḥasan's achievement.

<ClaimBox title="Transmission hypothesis">The reconciliation saying becomes multiply visible through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī’s Basran network; the Jābir attribution through al-Aʿmash is investigated as a secondary Kufan route.</ClaimBox>

## The Historical Problem of 41 AH



Al-Ḥasan inherited a dangerous confrontation between Iraqi and Syrian forces, a divided Iraqi following, and serious problems of military cohesion. His movement toward accommodation with Muʿāwiya was contested even among men who had supported his father, Ali. Wilferd Madelung's historical reconstruction, based on al-Balādhurī and other early Arabic histories, discusses the mutiny among al-Ḥasan's troops, negotiations with Muʿāwiya, the disputed conditions of the settlement, and his subsequent withdrawal to Medina. Madelung dates his relinquishment of Iraqi authority to Rabīʿ II 41 AH / August 661 CE.[^3] One critic allegedly confronted al-Ḥasan after the agreement with the accusation:[^4]

<QuoteBlock source={"Ibn Kathīr, al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya, events of 41 AH, 8:20"} sourceUrl={"https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1897/20?lang=ar"}>
You have blackened the faces of the believers—or, “O blackener of the faces of the believers.”
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">سودت وجوه المؤمنين - أو يا مسود وجوه المؤمنين</p>
</QuoteBlock>


Another reportedly greeted him:

<QuoteBlock source={"Ibn Kathīr, al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya, events of 41 AH, 8:21"} sourceUrl={"https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1897/21?lang=ar"}>
Peace upon you, O humiliator of the believers.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">السلام عليك يا مذل المؤمنين</p>
</QuoteBlock>


Al-Ḥasan's reported response:

<QuoteBlock source={"Ibn Kathīr, al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya, events of 41 AH, 8:21"} sourceUrl={"https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/1897/21?lang=ar"}>
Do not say that, ʿĀmir. I am not the humiliator of the believers; rather, I disliked killing them over political rule.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">لا تقل هذا يا عامر لست بمذل المؤمنين ولكني كرهت أن أقتلهم على الملك</p>
</QuoteBlock>


These reports cannot be treated as transcripts of conversations taken down in 41 AH. Their value lies in their preservation of competing historical interpretations. Was al-Ḥasan weak for relinquishing his father's struggle? Or was he praiseworthy for preventing further Muslim bloodshed? The reconciliation prophecy we looked at is an extraordinarily efficient answer to this debate. The Prophet allegedly declared that al-Ḥasan's greatness would consist in the very act some followers later condemned.

Notice the title sayyid. The accusation *mudhill al-muʾminīn* (humiliator of the believers) is answered with prophetic recognition of al-Ḥasan as a “sayyid,” a figure of honor and leadership. The hadith does more than say that two armies would stop fighting. It supplies a borderline Prophetic evaluation of the settlement. It also calls both sides Muslims, which would be a meaningful description in an era in which civil-war opponents could accuse each other of disbelief.

## Why al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī matters



The crucial figure in the transmitted material is not al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī, the political protagonist, but  al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, the renowned Basran preacher[^5] and traditionist (21–110 AH / 642–728 CE). The two men share the same name and must be distinguished carefully. The Basran scholar was approximately twenty years old in 41 AH, which means the settlement occurred within his own lifetime. He subsequently became one of the most influential voices in a city profoundly affected by the First Fitna and debates over political conduct.[^5] In the main hadith family, different transmitters all return to him:

Some of these branches have multiple later students, whereas others survive through narrow, occasionally questionable routes. The broad agreement on al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī as the transmitting source, across different recensions and narrative settings, is a substantial datum. He is the effective common link (CL) of this Basran transmission complex; the earliest figure around whom the recoverable lines branch.[^6]

His alleged source, Abū Bakra, is not an arbitrary authority in this setting. Elizabeth Urban’s study of him[^7] describes him as politically detached and “anti-fitna” within early Basran memory. His name is associated with avoiding fighting between Muslims, including in the separate woman-ruler tradition deployed against participation in the Battle of the Camel that was analyzed in a previous article. Urban characterizes the present reconciliation account in the context of Abū Bakra's anti-fitna and pro-reconciliation outlook. That association helps explain why the hadith's Basran transmitters might have attached it to this particular Companion.[^7] The historically meaningful point is that the report's setting, message, and most extensive transmission network all intersect in the same post-41 AH Iraqi world.

## The canonical version and its political setting



The most revealing witness may be Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2704, precisely because it does not give the prophecy on its own. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī first recounts the military standoff between al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī and Muʿāwiya, the fear of massive losses, and the eventual negotiations. Only after relating the agreement does he append his citation from Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock translation={"He then made peace with him. Al-Ḥasan [al-Baṣrī] said: ‘I heard Abū Bakra say ... This son of mine is a leader, and perhaps God will reconcile through him two great factions of the Muslims.’"} source={"Ṣaḥīḥ, no. 2704"}  arabic={"إِنَّ ابْنِي هَذَا سَيِّدٌ وَلَعَلَّ اللَّهَ أَنْ يُصْلِحَ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ عَظِيمَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/53/9"} />

The juxtaposition is integral to the report's literary effect. The audience hears the conclusion of the settlement and then receives the alleged prophecy that supplies its meaning. In the same historical narration, Muʿāwiya is made to ask who would care for the community if both armies destroyed one another. His magnanimity and the negotiated solution are foregrounded within the narrative. The hadith therefore also participates in a larger tradition of presenting the settlement as a preferable alternative to escalating conflict.

The wording through Abū Mūsā Isrāʾīl, from al-Ḥasan, from Abū Bakra, is found in several other collections, including al-Bukhārī 3629, 3746, and 7109[^8][^9], al-Nasāʾī 1410, and Aḥmad 20392. In some versions Muḥammad is on the pulpit and looks alternately at the crowd and al-Ḥasan; elsewhere the child enters during a sermon.[^8] A good comparison comes from al-Sunan al-Kubrā of al-Nasāʾī, 10009–10014. Al-Nasāʾī records the Abū Bakra chain through Abū Mūsā, then remarks:[^10]

<HadithBlock translation={"Ashʿath contradicted him."} source={"al-Sunan al-Kubrā, al-Nasāʾī, no. 10010 (authorial note before no. 10011)"}  arabic={"خالفه أشعث"} url={"https://test.dorar.net/h/6SXW7H5c?osoul=1"} />

The next report, through Ashʿath b. ʿAbd al-Malik → al-Ḥasan, no longer names Abū Bakra. Instead it states:

<HadithBlock translation={"From one of the Prophet's Companions—meaning Anas."} source={"al-Sunan al-Kubrā, al-Nasāʾī, no. 10011 (chain excerpt)"}  arabic={"عن بعض أصحاب النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم يعني أنسا"} url={"https://test.dorar.net/h/6SXW7H5c?osoul=1"} />

It also changes the scene by having the Prophet preaching while al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī sits on his thigh. Even the phrase sayyid is embedded in a “hopeful” rather than categorical prediction:

<HadithBlock translation={"I hope that this son of mine will be a leader, and I hope that God will reconcile through him two factions of my community."} source={"al-Sunan al-Kubrā, al-Nasāʾī, no. 10011"}  arabic={"إني لأرجو أن يكون ابني هذا سيدا وإني لأرجو أن يصلح الله به بين فئتين من أمتي"} url={"https://test.dorar.net/h/6SXW7H5c?osoul=1"} />

The clarification “meaning Anas” may itself be an explanatory identification rather than Ashʿath’s verbatim wording. In either case, this Anas attribution should not be mistaken for an independently branching Anas transmission family considering it still passes through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī. Nor does Ashʿath consistently cite Anas. Other witnesses through Ashʿath[^10], including al-Tirmidhī  3773, Abū Dāwūd 4662, and al-Ḥākim 4809, instead give the familiar Abū Bakra attribution instead of Anas. The next three reports in al-Nasāʾī's sequence gives ys a few mursal traditions, when it is said, “*ʿAwf, Dāwūd, and Hishām transmitted it mursal,*” (al-Sunan al-Kubrā, al-Nasāʾī, no. 10011). In the first of these, ʿAwf → al-Ḥasan, al-Ḥasan is quoted saying, “*It reached me that the Messenger of God said something similar to al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī.*” (al-Sunan al-Kubrā, al-Nasāʾī, no. 10012

The other two, through Dāwūd and Hishām, simply have al-Ḥasan say that the Prophet spoke; no intervening Companion is mentioned. A further transmission from Ismāʿīl b. Abī al-Ṣalt[^11] → al-Ḥasan, preserved in al-Maṭālib al-ʿĀliya 4743, likewise attributes the saying directly to the Prophet without a Companion intermediary.[^11]

## From a pulpit to the Prophet’s back: narrative expansion



The prophecy moves among settings with noticeably different dramatic details. In the Abū Mūsā version Muḥammad stands on the pulpit, looking alternately at the audience and his grandson. In ʿAlī b. Zayd's recension the child climbs onto the pulpit and is embraced or kissed. In the Mubārak b. Faḍāla family, the Prophet is praying, and the young al-Ḥasan repeatedly climbs onto his back or neck during prostration. The Prophet handles the child gently, then announces his status as a sayyid. In some versions he additionally calls him his rayḥāna—his fragrant flower or basil.[^14]

These may be variants of traditions about Muḥammad's affection for his grandsons that later became joined to the reconciliation saying. Such affectionate stories are independently familiar[^15][^15] within the broader biographical literature; the appearance of different scenes does not mean every scene must have been invented for this prophecy. The relevant textual fact is that the same predictive kernel is repeatedly placed within different narrative occasions. It appears to have been more portable than its supposed original occasion.

A variant from Musnad Aḥmad gives an addition after the Prophetic saying.[^16]

<HadithBlock translation={"Al-Ḥasan [al-Baṣrī] said: ‘By God, by God, after he assumed authority, not even a cupping-glass full of blood was shed during his caliphate.’"} source={"al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, no. 20448"}  arabic={"فَوَاللهِ وَاللهِ ، بَعْدَ أَنْ وَلِيَ لَمْ يُهَرَاقْ فِي خِلَافَتِهِ مِلْءُ مِحْجَمَةٍ مِنْ دَمٍ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/10/42"} />

This is explicit retrospective fulfillment commentary.[^16] The story's narrator does not leave future readers to work out which event is meant; he places the prediction next to his own assessment of al-Ḥasan b. ʿAlī's political career. Whatever one thinks of the historical precision of the bloodshed claim, the addition tells us how this transmitter intended the story to be understood. The account legitimates al-Ḥasan's decision by defining restraint as an achievement.

## The Jābir bypass: a Kufan dive through al-Aʿmash



The strongest objection to dating the reconciliation prophecy to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī’s teaching world is that a report attributed to Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh. If independent, it might suggest two Companions separately heard Muḥammad forecast precisely the same event. An isnād-cum-matn analysis, however, demonstrates this version being a secondary Kufan recension radiating from al-Aʿmash, long after the settlement it purports to predict. Al-Ṭabarānī reports:[^17]

<HadithBlock translation={"ʿAbd al-Salām b. ʿĀṣim al-Rāzī narrated to us; ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Maghrāʾ narrated to us, from al-Aʿmash, from Abū Sufyān, from Jābir ... ‘My son—meaning al-Ḥasan—is a leader, and God will surely reconcile through him two factions of Muslims.’"} source={"al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr, no. 2597"}  arabic={"إِنَّ ابْنِي يَعْنِي الْحَسَنَ سَيِّدٌ ولَيُصْلِحَنَّ اللهُ بِهِ بَيْنَ فِئَتَيْنِ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani/7"} />

A superficial reading could blame the bypass on ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Maghrāʾ (d. c. 190 AH), who transmits this variant and whose reports from al-Aʿmash sometimes attracted criticism. But al-Ṭabarānī’s own note prevents that simple conclusion:[^18]

<HadithBlock translation={"No one transmitted this hadith from al-Aʿmash except ʿAbd al-Raḥmān and Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Umawī."} source={"al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, no. 1813"}  arabic={"لم يرو هذا الحديث عن الأعمش إلا عبد الرحمن ويحيى بن سعيد الأموي"} url={"https://test.dorar.net/h/PCgp1UB4?osoul=1"} />

The independent lower testimony matters here because Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Umawī (d. 194 AH) appears through Yaḥyā b. Maʿīn in al-Bayhaqī’s *Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa*, preserving the same alleged al-Aʿmash → Abū Sufyān → Jābir tradition. Another later-preserved route runs through Abū ʿAwāna[^19] al-Waḍḍāḥ (d. 175 AH), recorded by al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī and in al-Ṭuyūriyyāt. Al Ṭabarānī counted two pupils; these later witnesses supply a third claimed pupil.[^20]

## Finding al-Aʿmash’s possible source: Ismāʿīl b. Muslim



A transmitter who demonstrably circulated the reconciliation tradition from al-Ḥasan also belongs to al-Aʿmash’s recorded teaching network. In al-Ṭabarānī’s *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*, the prophecy travels through: Abū Muʿāwiya → Ismāʿīl b. Muslim → al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī → Abū Bakra. The report describes the young al-Ḥasan climbing onto the Prophet during prayer, being embraced, and receiving the designation sayyid:[^21]

This does not concern the reconciliation prophecy[^24]; it concerns a different account and an explicitly named al-ʿAbdī intermediary. We have a critic of the early hadith tradition who already identified a case within al-Aʿmash’s own repertory where Ismāʿīl ↔ Abū Sufyān became competing intermediaries leading back toward al-Ḥasan. In the same context, Abū Ḥātim doubted that Abū Sufyān had narrated[^25] from al-Ḥasan at all and considered al-Aʿmash a possible source of the mistake. Abū Ḥātim’s negative assessment shows that it was not beyond dispute. The known Jābir version is still mediated through al-Aʿmash.[^24]

The proposed process is therefore not a conjecture[^26][^27][^28] based only on a narrator’s reputation for tadlīs. We have a directly comparable isnād disagreement involving al Aʿmash, Abū Sufyān, an Ismāʿīl intermediary, and al-Ḥasan, as well as a separate al-Ḥasan → Ismāʿīl source for the actual prophecy. That combination is the strongest case for treating al Aʿmash as the author of the false-independence maneuver.

What matters is the shared predicate and the disappearance of the narrative frame. The proposed reconstruction does not require al-Aʿmash to adopt the entire Basran scene. He could borrow the short maxim—whether directly from Ismāʿīl b. Muslim, from Ismāʿīl’s associates—and give it the replacement authority of his known transmitter Abū Sufyān, followed by Jābir.

## What the prophecy accomplished


The report's wording makes more sense as a political evaluation than as a neutral forecast. Its defining element is that the Prophet is made to call al-Ḥasan a sayyid specifically because God would bring about reconciliation through him. If the opponents of the settlement could call al-Ḥasan the “*humiliator of the believers,*” the hadith supplies a perfectly balanced response with the Prophet having already recognized his nobility. If militant factions viewed compromise with Muʿāwiya as an abandonment of ʿAlī's claim (like the Kharajites did), the prophecy transforms restraint into a distinguishing virtue. If later Muslim writers needed an exemplary precedent for avoiding civil war, the Prophet's own grandson becomes the model, and both armies are identified as factions of Muslims rather than as a party of faithful believers opposing unbelievers.[^28]

The report need not be straightforward pro-Umayyad propaganda. Its most obvious function is  pro-reconciliation and pro-al-Ḥasan. It defends a descendant of Muḥammad against a specific criticism. That framing could subsequently be welcomed by groups emphasizing community unity, including writers who preferred to read the First Fitna without condemning one entire side as outside Islam. The political usefulness of the hadith was therefore broad and durable.

This ideological setting also explains why Abū Bakra[^29] is such an apt named authority in the Basran lines. His established literary persona as a fitna-averse Companion would lend the prophecy a recognizable moral ancestry. Yet, the report's survival through his name, the alternative Anas attribution, and multiple mursal versions cautions against tracing its precise wording automatically back to him.

## Reconstructing the development of the report


The combined historical and transmission evidence permits a reasonably concrete reconstruction. First, the settlement of 41 AH produced competing judgments about al-Ḥasan's character and political decision. The later historical sources preserve harsh criticism from disappointed supporters. This is the event to which the prophecy supplies an answer. Second, within the first/seventh-century and early second/eighth-century Basran world, the idea circulated that al-Ḥasan's refusal to continue the civil war was noble rather than humiliating. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is the best recoverable point at which the Prophetic reconciliation saying spread through multiple pupils. The event occurred within his lifetime, and its anti-fitna meaning fits the political concerns of his Iraqi milieu.

Third, the upper attribution was not uniform. Some pupils report the saying through Abū Bakra, one named transmission substitutes Anas, and several preserve it mursal. A version in al-Bukhārī explicitly claims al-Ḥasan heard Abū Bakra, but the divergent reports prevent the upper chain from being treated as a universally fixed element of the tradition. Fourth, different branches elaborated the scene and the prediction. The Prophet appears with al-Ḥasan on the pulpit, or while preaching, or during prayer with the child climbing onto him. The wording ranges from “if he lives” to “perhaps” or “I hope,” and sometimes to “God certainly will.” Some versions add an unmistakable retrospective gloss about al-Ḥasan's tenure or recite the detailed account of the settlement immediately before the alleged prediction.

Fifth, the most defensible candidate for creating the false-independent Jābir dive is al Aʿmash. Several alleged pupils retain al-Aʿmash’s distinct Companion attribution, whereas no independent route attests this political maxim from Jābir or Abū Sufyān without al-Aʿmash. The direct personal contact needed for borrowing is established with al-Aʿmash having a student, Ismāʿīl b. Muslim al-Makkī, whose transmissions from al-Ḥasan include the reconciliation prophecy and closely related childhood accounts. Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s *ʿIlal* also preserves a comparison in an unrelated report where Ismāʿīl and Abū Sufyān compete as the immediate source of al Aʿmash’s information from the al-Ḥasan network, with Abū Ḥātim suspecting a mistake from al-Aʿmash. These facts make al-Aʿmash’s replacement of the Basran source with Abū Sufyān → Jābir a positive historical reconstruction, not simply a suspicion based on prior reputation.[^21][^22][^24] The model is therefore:
