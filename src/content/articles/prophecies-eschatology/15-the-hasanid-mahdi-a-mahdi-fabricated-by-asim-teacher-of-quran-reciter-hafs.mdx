## The Report and the Name Problem

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">لَوْ لَمْ يَبْقَ مِنَ الدُّنْيَا إِلَّا لَيْلَةٌ لَطَوَّلَ اللهُ تِلْكَ اللَّيْلَةَ حَتَّى يَمْلِكَ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ بَيْتِي يُوَاطِئُ اسْمُهُ اسْمِي وَاسْمُ أَبِيهِ اسْمَ أَبِي، يَمْلَأُهَا قِسْطًا وَعَدْلًا كَمَا مُلِئَتْ ظُلْمًا وَجَوْرًا.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“If only a night remained of the world, God would prolong that night until a man from my household ruled. His name would match my name and his father’s name would match my father’s name. He would fill it with equity and justice as it had been filled with oppression and injustice.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

The report looks simple only when it is read as timeless eschatology. A man from the Prophet’s household will appear near the end of time. His name will match the Prophet’s name. His father’s name will match the Prophet’s father’s name. He will restore justice. In later Sunnī reception, this became one of the most familiar Mahdī proof-texts.

The historical problem is that the report does not merely describe a future redeemer. It identifies him by a highly specific name formula. A bare “his name matches my name” points to any **Muḥammad** from the Prophet’s family. The fuller formula, **“and his father’s name matches my father’s name,”** narrows the referent to **Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh**. That was not an abstract possibility in the second/eighth century. It was the exact name of the most famous Ḥasanid messianic claimant of the early Abbasid period: **Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ḥasan**, known as **al-Nafs al-Zakiyya**.

The question is therefore not whether later Sunnī books contain the report. They do. The question is when this formula became attached to a Prophetic isnād and why the report’s most charged wording points so neatly to a live political claimant who rebelled against al-Manṣūr in 145/762.

<ClaimBox>
This article argues that the recoverable Ibn Masʿūd Mahdī report is a Kūfan second/eighth-century construction or reshaping. Its base form is a name-only Ahl al-Bayt expectation transmitted through ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd. The father-name clause is a sharper partisan accretion that made the report fit Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya, and it circulated through precisely the kind of Shīʿī and pro-ʿAlid transmission networks where that claim would make sense.
</ClaimBox>

This does not require the argument that every later transmitter in the books was lying. It also does not require a cartoon picture of ʿĀṣim as a villain. The evidence points to a more precise conclusion. The report becomes historically visible at ʿĀṣim’s circle. The clause that identifies **Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh** belongs to the partial common-link layer and is strongest in transmitters whose sectarian or political location already fits the Ḥasanid Mahdī environment.

## What the Variant Map Shows

The Ibn Masʿūd report survives in many books, but the spread is structurally misleading. The collector layer is wide. The report appears in Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Bazzār, Ibn Ḥibbān, al-Ṭabarānī, al-Ḥākim, and later Mahdī compilations. But the upper structure is narrow. The ordinary route is:

> **ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd → Zirr b. Ḥubaysh → Ibn Masʿūd → the Prophet**

The most stable early-looking wording is not the full later formula. It is the name-only form:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">لَا تَذْهَبُ الدُّنْيَا حَتَّى يَمْلِكَ الْعَرَبَ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ بَيْتِي يُوَاطِئُ اسْمُهُ اسْمِي.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The world will not pass away until a man from my household rules the Arabs. His name will match my name.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is the form found in the strongest-looking Sufyān routes. Al-Tirmidhī 2230, Aḥmad 3571, Aḥmad 3572, Aḥmad 3573, Aḥmad 4098, Aḥmad 4279, al-Bazzār’s Sufyān and al-Shaybānī routes, Ibn Ḥibbān 5954, and several Ṭabarānī routes preserve this simpler wording, or a close version of it. In this form, the report could support a general Ahl al-Bayt expectation without naming a single claimant.

The father-name clause is different. It appears in selected PCL-level forms, especially through Fiṭr b. Khalīfa, Zāʾida, and ʿAmr b. Abī Qays. It is also bundled in Abū Dāwūd 4282, where Abū Dāwūd explicitly gathers several routes from ʿĀṣim and says their meanings are one, while also noting that Fiṭr adds the justice clause and that Sufyān’s wording lacks the father-name phrase. This is exactly the kind of place where a later harmonized matn can make a secondary clause look older than it is.

## ʿĀṣim Is the Recoverable Common Link

ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd is famous as one of the transmitters of Qurʾānic recitation. The modern reader often meets him through the Ḥafṣ transmission of the Qurʾān. That can make a political hadith transmitted through him feel automatically secure. The rijāl material does not allow that simple move.

Classical critics did not treat ʿĀṣim as an outright liar. They often respected him, especially in recitation. But the same material repeatedly distinguishes between his rank as a Qurʾān transmitter and his precision as a hadith transmitter. Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal is reported to have said that ʿĀṣim was a righteous man, a reciter, and trustworthy, but that al-Aʿmash was more retentive in hadith. Yaʿqūb b. Sufyān says that in his hadith there was disturbance, while still calling him trustworthy. Ibn Saʿd calls him trustworthy but says he made many mistakes in hadith. That is exactly the profile one expects for a transmitter whose authority could carry material outward but whose solitary politically useful report should not be allowed to become early history by default.

The point is not that ʿĀṣim must personally have invented every form of the report. The more careful claim is this: the report is not historically visible before his teaching circle, and the strongest formulation of the report changes below him. That makes ʿĀṣim the common-link layer for the base report and the partial common links below him the likely layer of editorial sharpening.

This distinction matters because the father-name clause does not behave like an inherited stable phrase. It behaves like a political identifier added to a more general Ahl al-Bayt Mahdī expectation.

## The Father-Name Clause Is the Fingerprint

The phrase **“his name matches my name”** was useful but vague. Many Alids could be named Muḥammad. It created space for expectation without requiring one claimant. The phrase **“and his father’s name matches my father’s name”** closes that space.

The Prophet’s name was Muḥammad. His father was ʿAbdallāh. A Mahdī whose name and father’s name match the Prophet’s is therefore **Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh**. In the second/eighth-century political world, the obvious figure is Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ḥasan, al-Nafs al-Zakiyya. His father, ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ḥasan al-Maḥḍ, promoted him as the promised leader of the Prophet’s family.

The historical reports about the al-Abwāʾ meeting make the connection explicit. After the murder of al-Walīd b. Yazīd in 126/744 and the turmoil that followed, Hāshimites gathered at al-Abwāʾ, between Mecca and Medina. In the Ḥasanid memory of the event, ʿAbdallāh al-Maḥḍ called the family to pledge to his son:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">فَهَلُمَّ نُبَايِعْ مُحَمَّدًا، فَقَدْ عَلِمْتُمْ أَنَّهُ الْمَهْدِيُّ.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“Come, then, let us pledge allegiance to Muḥammad, for you know that he is the Mahdī.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

A second tradition names the future Abbasid rulers among the attendees. Ibrāhīm al-Imām, al-Saffāḥ, al-Manṣūr, Ṣāliḥ b. ʿAlī, ʿAbdallāh al-Maḥḍ, and his sons Muḥammad and Ibrāhīm appear in the memory of the meeting. In that version, al-Manṣūr himself is made to say that people incline to no one more readily than this young man, meaning Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh, and the group pledges to him.

These reports have to be read critically. Elad is right to treat the al-Abwāʾ cycle as a composite, post-revolt memory shaped by Ḥasanid anti-Abbasid polemic. But that caution helps the argument rather than weakening it. It shows that the memory of Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh as “the Mahdī” was part of the ideological struggle between the Ḥasanids and the Abbasids. The hadith formula does the same work in prophetic form. It turns a contested Hāshimid claim into foreknowledge.

<ClaimBox>
The father-name clause is not an accidental detail. It is the prosopographical key that turns a general Ahl al-Bayt expectation into the name of a live Ḥasanid claimant: Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh.
</ClaimBox>

## The Hāshimid Blank: “al-Riḍā from the Family of Muḥammad”

The larger political setting makes the report even clearer. The anti-Umayyad underground did not begin as a neatly separated Abbasid party on one side and a neatly separated Alid party on the other. It operated inside a broader Hāshimid revolutionary imagination. The slogan **al-Riḍā min Āl Muḥammad**, “the agreed-upon one from the family of Muḥammad,” was powerful precisely because it was vague. It allowed different factions to cooperate before the identity of the chosen leader was publicly fixed.

That vagueness created a problem as soon as success became possible. Who, exactly, was al-Riḍā? The Ḥasanid answer was Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya. The Abbasid answer was different. Their claim drew on the alleged Abū Hāshim testament and the Khurāsānī daʿwa, then later on the black-banner apocalyptic language of the revolution. The Abbasids could claim the victorious army from the East. The Ḥasanids could claim the name formula.

This is why the black-banner material matters. The report in al-Ḥākim 8434 is not merely a random weak parallel. It fuses the two revolutionary toolkits: black banners from the East and the Mahdī whose name and father’s name match the Prophet’s. That fusion is exactly what we should expect in a world where Hāshimid, ʿAlid, and Abbasid messianic symbols overlapped before being claimed by rival factions.

The Abbasids then did something especially revealing. Al-Manṣūr’s own name was ʿAbdallāh. He named his heir **Muḥammad** and gave him the regnal title **al-Mahdī**. The Abbasid al-Mahdī was therefore also **Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh**. The formula that fit al-Nafs al-Zakiyya could be co-opted by the dynasty that crushed him.

This is not a coincidence to pass over quickly. It shows why the father-name report was so useful. It was a contested prophecy. The Ḥasanids could point to Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ḥasan. The Abbasids could point to Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh b. Muḥammad, al-Manṣūr’s son. The wording did not merely predict a future saint. It supplied a name-template usable in a succession struggle.

## The Bypass Routes Do Not Rescue the Report

A defender of the report might say that the ʿĀṣim common-link argument is too narrow because there are routes that seem to bypass him. Two routes matter most: the Ṭabarānī 10208 route through ʿAmr b. Murra and the al-Ḥākim 8434 route through Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī and ʿAlqama. Neither route gives the report an early independent foundation.

### Ṭabarānī 10208: a weak side-route, not an early control

Ṭabarānī 10208 runs:

> Yūsuf b. Ḥawshab al-Shaybānī → Abū Yazīd al-Aʿwar → ʿAmr b. Murra → Zirr b. Ḥubaysh → Ibn Masʿūd

Its wording is also not the dangerous wording. It says only:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">لَا يَذْهَبُ الدُّنْيَا حَتَّى يَمْلِكَ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ بَيْتِي يُوَافِقُ اسْمُهُ اسْمِي.</div>
<div class="hadith-block__translation">“The world will not pass away until a man from my household rules. His name will correspond to my name.”</div>
</HadithBlock>

This route should not be rejected on chronological impossibility. The death dates do not make ʿAmr b. Murra hearing from Zirr impossible. The problem is more defensible: the route is thin, late in attestation, and does not carry the father-name clause. It may be an independent paraphrase of the name-only expectation, or it may be a side-route shaped by the ʿĀṣim tradition. Either way, it cannot establish the full Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh formula.

### al-Ḥākim 8434: the “bypass” that proves the context

The al-Ḥākim 8434 route looks more dramatic because it bypasses Zirr and ʿĀṣim entirely. It runs through:

> Ibn Abī Dārim → Ḥanān b. Sudayr → ʿAmr b. Qays → al-Ḥakam → Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī → ʿAlqama and ʿUbayda → Ibn Masʿūd

Its matn combines the Mahdī name formula with black banners from the East. Al-Dhahabī’s judgement is blunt: **“This is fabricated.”** That should already prevent it from functioning as clean independent support. But the rijāl context makes it more important than a normal weak route. It is a documentary link to the very kind of partisan environment this thesis predicts.

Al-Ḥākim’s own shaykh for the report, Ibn Abī Dārim, was a Kūfan Shīʿī/Rāfiḍī figure whom al-Dhahabī treats with suspicion. Al-Ḥākim himself is reported to have called him a Rāfiḍī and not trustworthy. Al-Dhahabī’s entry says that Ibn Abī Dārim composed material attacking some Companions and was not reliable in transmission. The same polemical profile is visible in the reports associated with him: the Fāṭima miscarriage accusation against ʿUmar and a sectarian tafsīr gloss making Pharaoh stand for ʿUmar.

The bridge through Ḥanān b. Sudayr is just as revealing. Ḥanān belongs to a Kūfan Shīʿī Ṣayrafī family. Al-ʿUqaylī’s entry preserves material linking Ḥanān’s transmission environment to Sudayf al-Makkī, a poet and activist who attached himself to Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya when he rose in Medina. The material says Sudayf inclined to Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh, pledged to him, became one of his inner circle, attacked Abū Jaʿfar al-Manṣūr, exalted the Banū ʿAlī, and recited verses from Muḥammad’s own minbar calling him the Mahdī. In the same report-cycle, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq is made to disown their material as having no foundation.

That makes al-Ḥākim 8434 the opposite of what a defender needs. A true independent bypass would have to be earlier, cleaner, and less ideologically fitted. This one is later, judged fabricated, and runs straight through the pro-ʿAlid agitation network around the Ḥasanid Mahdī claim. Its black-banner language also belongs to the shared Hāshimid revolutionary apocalyptic vocabulary of the 120s–130s, later monopolized by the Abbasid Khurāsānī daʿwa.

<ClaimBox>
The bypass routes do not undercut the ʿĀṣim thesis. Ṭabarānī 10208 does not carry the father-name clause. Al-Ḥākim 8434 carries the clause in the most partisan form and is tied to the very pro-Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya network that explains why the clause was useful.
</ClaimBox>

## Why Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya Fits the Hadith

The historical fit is not limited to the name. Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya occupied the exact political role that the report creates.

He was a Ḥasanid, from the Prophet’s family through ʿAlī and Fāṭima. His father was ʿAbdallāh b. al-Ḥasan al-Maḥḍ, a figure of extraordinary Alid pedigree because he was connected to both Ḥasanid and Ḥusaynid descent. The Ḥasanid claim did not present Muḥammad merely as a rebel. It presented him as the chosen one from the Prophet’s family. The al-Abwāʾ reports remember his father saying, “you know he is the Mahdī.”

Under the Umayyads, the claim could remain within the broader anti-Umayyad Hāshimid movement. Under the Abbasids, it became a direct challenge. Al-Manṣūr hunted the Ḥasanids, imprisoned ʿAbdallāh al-Maḥḍ and his relatives, forced the family into chains, and eventually crushed Muḥammad’s revolt in Medina in 145/762. Elad’s study emphasizes that this revolt was not a minor family quarrel. It was a politico-religious challenge to Abbasid legitimacy, supported by a wide range of non-Ṭālibī actors as well as Alid factions.

The supporter map also matters for hadith criticism. The revolt attracted scholars, jurists, and transmitters. Mālik b. Anas is famously connected in the sources to legal reasoning that undercut coerced allegiance to the Abbasids. Sufyān al-Thawrī, Abū Ḥanīfa, Hishām b. ʿUrwa, and others appear in the broader support or sympathy lists with varying degrees of certainty. In Kūfa and Baṣra, the Zaydī and pro-ʿAlid networks around Muḥammad and his brother Ibrāhīm were especially important. That is precisely the kind of world where a Prophetic report naming a Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh from the Prophet’s family would have political value.

## Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq and the Rival Alid Memory

The Ḥasanid claim was not accepted by all Alids. The reports about Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq at al-Abwāʾ are important because they show a rival Alid response to the same claim. In these traditions, Jaʿfar refuses to pledge to Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh and predicts Abbasid victory. Some versions have him point to al-Saffāḥ or al-Manṣūr; others have him foretell that Muḥammad and his brother Ibrāhīm will be killed.

These reports are not neutral historical recordings either. Elad reads much of this material as Ḥusaynid/Imāmī memory, shaped after the failure of the Ḥasanid revolt, in order to justify Jaʿfar’s quietism and to distinguish the true Imāmī line from the activist Ḥasanid line. This is useful because it shows how crowded the field was. Different Alid groups fought over the meaning of the same events. The Ḥasanids had a Mahdī claim. The Ḥusaynid/Imāmī memory denied it. The Abbasids crushed it and later appropriated parts of it.

The hadith formula belongs in that contested space. It is not floating above history. It is a scriptural weapon in a world where the identity of the rightful leader from the Prophet’s family was being fought over.

## How the Matn Developed

The report’s development can be summarized as a sequence of increasingly specific political meaning.

This sequence explains why the same hadith complex can serve more than one party. A general Ahl al-Bayt Muḥammad expectation could serve many. The father-name clause served the Ḥasanid Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh. The black-banner form served the Abbasid revolutionary imagination, or at least the Hāshimid apocalyptic vocabulary that the Abbasids made dynastic. The later Abbasid title al-Mahdī then gave the dynasty its own Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh.

## Objections

### “But Abū Dāwūd preserves the father-name clause.”

He does, but Abū Dāwūd 4282 is a composite report. It brings together several PCLs from ʿĀṣim: ʿUmar b. ʿUbayd, Abū Bakr b. ʿAyyāsh, Sufyān, Zāʾida, and Fiṭr. The collector explicitly says the wording is by meaning, and the report itself notes differences between the PCLs. Sufyān’s wording is the name-only “a man from my household whose name matches my name.” Fiṭr adds the justice clause. The composite form is not proof that every branch carried the father clause from the beginning.

### “But Ibn Ḥibbān 6824 has Sufyān with the father clause.”

That report should be registered, but it should not control the whole family against the broader Sufyān pattern. The Sufyān material in al-Tirmidhī, Aḥmad, al-Bazzār, and Ṭabarānī is overwhelmingly name-only. A single later or harmonized Sufyān form with the father clause is better explained as contamination from the expanded wording than as the original form from which the name-only Sufyān family somehow descended.

### “But there are routes through other Companions.”

There are broader Mahdī traditions from ʿAlī, Abū Saʿīd, Umm Salama, Abū Hurayra, and others. That does not solve the Ibn Masʿūd name-and-father-name report. The question here is not whether later Muslims circulated many Mahdī reports. The question is whether the specific formula **name = my name, father = my father** is early and independent. The most important parallel that carries the same identifier, the Qurra al-Muzanī route, runs through the discredited Baṣran family of Dāwūd b. al-Muḥabbar / al-Muḥabbar b. Qaḥdham, and al-Bazzār himself points away from it toward the better-known Abū Saʿīd line. That looks like a migrating formula, not an inherited Prophetic core.

### “But ʿAmr b. Murra could have heard from Zirr.”

Yes. The chronology does not make that impossible. The rejection should not rest on a lifespan argument. It rests on attestation and content. Ṭabarānī 10208 is a lone late side-route with a thin lower chain, and it does not carry the father-name clause. Even if one accepts it as an independent name-only expectation, it does not prove the Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh formula.

### “But ʿĀṣim died before the 145 revolt.”

That objection misunderstands the argument. The Ḥasanid Mahdī claim did not begin only when Muḥammad rose in 145. The al-Abwāʾ memory places the claim in the late Umayyad crisis after 126. ʿĀṣim died around 127/128, exactly on the edge of that world. The father-name clause also need not be ʿĀṣim’s original wording. The evidence suggests a base ʿĀṣim report that was sharpened at the PCL layer by transmitters active in the Muḥammad al-Nafs al-Zakiyya window.

## The Most Likely Reconstruction

The most economical reconstruction is as follows.

First, a Kūfan Ahl al-Bayt expectation circulated in ʿĀṣim’s circle: a man from the Prophet’s household, named like the Prophet, would rule. This was vague enough to belong to the wider Hāshimid revolutionary environment.

Second, as the Ḥasanid claim of Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya sharpened, some transmitters expanded the formula by adding the father-name clause. This turned a general Muḥammad expectation into a Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh identifier.

Third, the report absorbed justice-restoration language and, in a fabricated bypass route, black-banner language from the East. These expansions moved the report from a dynastic name notice into full apocalyptic propaganda.

Fourth, after the Abbasid victory, the same formula became contestable. The Abbasids could crush the Ḥasanid Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh while naming their own heir Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh and calling him al-Mahdī. The formula survived because it could be reinterpreted after its first political target failed.

<VerdictBox>
The Ibn Masʿūd Mahdī report is not a clean prophecy preserved from the Prophet. The recoverable transmission converges on ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd, and the father-name clause behaves like a second/eighth-century political identifier. It points into the Ḥasanid campaign for Muḥammad b. ʿAbdallāh al-Nafs al-Zakiyya, then enters the larger Hāshimid and Abbasid struggle over who counted as the promised leader from the Prophet’s family. The report’s later spread is real, but its historical origin is best explained as partisan crystallization, not foreknowledge.
</VerdictBox>
