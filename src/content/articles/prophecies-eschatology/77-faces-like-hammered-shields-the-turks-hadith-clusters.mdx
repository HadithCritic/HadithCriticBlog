The Sunni canon preserves roughly two dozen reports in which the Prophet warns of a people with small eyes, red faces, and faces "like hammered shields," wearing sandals of hair, who will one day fight the Arabs and tie their horses somewhere they should not be. Every modern reader of these reports reaches for the same referent: the Mongols. That reading is chronologically impossible — Bukhārī and Muslim had already canonized this material some five centuries before Chinggis Khan was born — and it also misses what the isnāds themselves are trying to tell us.

This corpus is not one hadith. It is five, attributed to five different Companions, transmitted through five different bottlenecks, none of whom can be checked against each other. Read as isnad-cum-matn evidence rather than as a single undifferentiated "Turks hadith," each cluster resolves into a specific transmitter with a specific regional stake, reporting on (or reformulating) a specific, datable seventh- or eighth-century frontier war. This piece walks through all five.

<ClaimBox>
The apocalyptic Turks corpus is not a single tradition with multiple chains. It is five independent companion-attributions — Abū Hurayra, Buraydah, ʿAmr b. Thaghlab, Abū Bakr, and Muʿāwiyah — each collapsing at its own bottleneck, each bottleneck situated in a real war on the eastern or northern frontier between 650 and 750 CE. The physiognomic vocabulary is shared because it is a floating ethnographic formula, not because the five clusters share an origin.
</ClaimBox>

<ContextNote title="Why Five Clusters, Not One">
Classical takhrīj apparatus groups these reports under a single chapter heading — al-Tirmidhī closes his own chapter on fighting the Turks by noting five further companions transmit "on this subject." That grouping is thematic, not genealogical. None of the five isnāds shares a transmitter with any other below the Companion level. Treating them as one hadith with six witnesses, rather than six hadiths sharing a stock vocabulary, is the single most consequential interpretive choice a reader of this corpus makes.
</ContextNote>

## Cluster 1 — Abū Hurayra: The Physiognomic Core

<HadithBlock
  label="The Ur-Form, Attested Independently in the Bazzār Route"

  source="al-Bazzār, via al-Haythamī, Majmaʿ al-Zawāʾid; parallel wording in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī nos. 2928–2929, 3587, 3590; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2912a–e"

Four elements recur across virtually every redaction of this report regardless of which stream carries it: small eyes, red faces, flat noses, and faces like hammered shields. Under the ordinary text-critical logic of *utrum in alterum abiturum erat* — which wording more easily explains the others — this four-element core is the best candidate for the ur-form. It is short, unspecific to any single campaign, and every later elaboration (footwear, geography, the shields simile's variant phrasing) reads as an addition to it rather than a pruning of something more specific.

<ContextNote title="The Historical Anchor: 649–651 CE">
Ibn al-Athīr's *al-Kāmil* dates Yazdegerd III's flight from Fārs into Kirmān to 30 AH. The Arab force sent in pursuit was caught in a snowstorm and perished; Yazdegerd escaped through Khurāsān to Marv, where he wrote to the Khāqān beyond the Oxus for reinforcement. The Khāqān answered, mustering Soghdian and Farghanan forces, and crossed the river to meet al-Aḥnaf b. Qays near Balkh — the first sustained contact between Arab armies and Central Asian steppe cavalry, fought in a style (mounted, mobile, composite-bow archery) wholly unlike the static Sasanian and Byzantine infantry warfare the Arabs had fought until then. Abū Hurayra, established in Medina and briefly governor of Bahrayn under ʿUmar, was demonstrably positioned to hear this campaign reported as it happened.
</ContextNote>

The geographical variant — "the Ḥour will not be established until you fight the Khūdh and the Kirmān from among the non-Arabs" — survives only in al-Aʿraj's stream (Bukhārī 3590, Ibn Mājah 4096). Khūdh is the Arabic rendering of Khūzistān; Kirmān is the province Yazdegerd fled through. It is far easier to explain the loss of a specific, verifiable place-name across the more widely circulated Zuhrī and Abū Ṣāliḥ streams, as the tradition abstracted beyond the generation that had actually fought there, than its independent invention by the one stream that happens to preserve it.[^1]

A fifth apparent witness, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī (Ibn Mājah 4099), does not survive scrutiny. The isnād runs through al-Aʿmash, from Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān — the same Abū Ṣāliḥ already identified as Suhayl's own father and source for the genuine Abū Hurayra stream. Al-Aʿmash is independently documented elsewhere reformulating existing material under a substituted companion-authority for reasons internal to Kufan, pro-ʿAlid sectarian politics — a milieu with its own reasons to be reserved about Abū Hurayra specifically. This is a re-routing, not a second memory of the event.

<ContextNote title="Buraydah's Own Vantage Point">
Buraydah is remembered as the last of the Prophet's Companions to die in Khurāsān (63/682-3), and Nuʿaym ibn Ḥammād's *Kitāb al-Fitan* preserves a detail absent from the canonical versions: "Buraydah used to never be far away from two or three camels, and traveling-gear for fleeing, because of what he had heard of the Turks." He organized his own household in continuous anticipation of the threat this report describes — he did not receive it as distant abstraction.
</ContextNote>

Chronologically, the report's content fits the Umayyad–Türgesh wars of 711–723 far better than anything in Buraydah's own lifetime: escalating, repeated pressure that eventually forces a negotiated peace maps loosely onto the sequence from early raids, through the partial catastrophe of the Battle of the Defile (112/730), to the eventual settlement following Suluk Khāqān's death. That correspondence should be read as suggestive rather than a proven one-to-one match — a three-part schema is loose enough to fit more than one sequence of events.

The mosque-columns detail is less distinctive than it first appears. Nuʿaym's *Fitan* preserves the identical "tying horses" climax attached to at least five different locations across different Turk-invasion reports — the Euphrates, the aqueduct at Fusṭāṭ, the palm groves of Jūkhā, the olive groves of Syria, and the Tigris (in a separate "Banū Qanṭūrāʾ" tradition that also names Khurāsān and Sijistān by name).[^2] The image is a floating apocalyptic formula that different transmitters localized to their own geography, not a fixed memory of one specific desecration.

Responsibility for the report's specific wording is best located with **Bashīr b. al-Muhājir**, not Buraydah or even ʿAbdullāh b. Buraydah. Bashīr's spread below him is real and checkable; the link above him to ʿAbdullāh b. Buraydah is not. Aḥmad's verdict on Bashīr — *munkar al-ḥadīth* — is the technical charge leveled at a transmitter whose material is unrecognized relative to what more reliable narrators report, which is exactly the profile of someone suspected of independently shaping a tradition rather than faithfully relaying it. Ibn Ḥajar's more measured *ṣadūq līn al-ḥadīth* keeps this an open, not a closed, question.

## Cluster 3 — ʿAmr b. Thaghlab: Baṣra's Own Bottleneck

<HadithBlock
  label="Bukhārī 2927"
  arabic="إِنَّ مِنْ أَشْرَاطِ السَّاعَةِ أَنْ تُقَاتِلُوا قَوْمًا نِعَالُهُمُ الشَّعَرُ، وَإِنَّ مِنْ أَشْرَاطِ السَّاعَةِ أَنْ تُقَاتِلُوا قَوْمًا عِرَاضَ الْوُجُوهِ كَأَنَّ وُجُوهَهُمُ الْمَجَانُّ الْمُطَرَّقَةُ"
  translation='"Among the signs of the Hour is that you will fight a people whose sandals are of hair, and among the signs of the Hour is that you will fight a people with broad faces, as if their faces were hammered shields."'
  source="al-Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ, no. 2927; Aḥmad; Ibn Mājah — via al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī ← ʿAmr b. Thaghlab"
/>

Ibn Abī Ḥātim's own note on ʿAmr b. Thaghlab is blunt: *lam yarwi ʿanhu ghayru al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī* — "no one narrated from him except al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī." That is not a common link in the technical sense at all. A common link requires multiple independent lines converging on a single figure; this is a bare single strand, and nothing exists to check al-Ḥasan's claim against.

That matters more than it might first appear, because al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is independently and repeatedly documented in the rijāl literature as a **mudallis** who transmitted with the ambiguous *ʿan* particle rather than an explicit statement of direct audition — a documented pattern, not a one-off suspicion.[^3] Between an untestable single-strand claim and an independently attested habit of loose attribution, al-Ḥasan is the more defensible candidate for responsibility, by elimination rather than by direct accusation.

<ContextNote title="What Baṣra Specifically Had to Fear">
Al-Ḥasan died in 110/728, two years after Baṣra's own catastrophe on the eastern front: the Day of Thirst (Yawm al-ʿAṭash, 106/724), in which Ashras b. ʿAbdallāh al-Sulamī's army — recruited and administered through Baṣra's own dīwān — was besieged at Baykand and lost some seven hundred men to thirst before fighting its way out. This is not a general "Umayyad-era anxiety." It is Baṣra's own military disaster, reported home through Baṣra's own soldiers, inside the final years of al-Ḥasan's life and preaching.
</ContextNote>

## Cluster 4 — Abū Bakr: The Dajjāl from Khurāsān, and a Corrected Common Link

<HadithBlock
  label="Preserved in Nuʿaym's Kitāb al-Fitan"
  arabic="سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم يَقُولُ: يَخْرُجُ الدَّجَّالُ مِنْ أَرْضٍ يُقَالُ لَهَا: خُرَاسَانُ، وَأَشَارَ بِيَدِهِ نَحْوَ الْمَشْرِقِ، وَمَعَهُ قَوْمٌ كَأَنَّ وُجُوهَهُمُ الْمَجَانُّ"
  translation='"I heard the Messenger of God say: The Dajjāl will emerge from a land called Khurāsān" — and he pointed with his hand toward the East — "and with him will be a people whose faces are like shields."'
  source="Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād, Kitāb al-Fitan, 1/117-118; parallel routes in al-Muṣannaf and Musnad Abī Bakr al-Ṣiddīq — via Abū al-Ṭayyāḥ / Qatāda ← ʿAmr b. Ḥurayth or Saʿīd b. al-Musayyab ← Abū Bakr"
/>

Two apparently independent chains reach Abū Bakr: one through ʿAmr b. Ḥurayth, al-Mughīra b. Sabīʿ, and Abū al-Ṭayyāḥ (Yazīd b. Ḥumayd al-Ḍubaʿī, d. 128/745-6, graded *thabt thiqa*); another through Saʿīd b. al-Musayyab and Qatāda b. Diʿāma (d. 117/735). Read naively, that looks like two common links corroborating each other. It is not. **Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba appears in both chains**, once citing Abū al-Ṭayyāḥ as his source and once citing Qatāda — the textbook signature of what Juynboll calls a "spider": a single lower transmitter attaching two different upper chains to the same material, rather than two genuinely separate lines of memory.

A common link, properly defined, is the single earliest figure in an isnād bundle at whom independent lines first converge — there is only one per bundle. Everything above the CL is unverifiable single-strand testimony; a later bottleneck beneath the CL is a partial common link, not a rival CL. Applying that correctly here, **Ibn Abī ʿArūba is the actual common link**, not Abū Bakr's two claimed upper informants. Ibn Shawdhab and Shuʿba each independently confirm that Ibn Abī ʿArūba really was circulating this material — that part is checkable. What is not checkable is whether Abū al-Ṭayyāḥ and Qatāda actually said what he attributes to them, since he is the only witness to either claim.

<ContextNote title="The Event Out of Khurāsān">
Tafsīr Muqātil b. Sulaymān records: "the historians mention that al-Ḥārith b. Surayj — the man of the black banner (ṣāḥib al-rāya al-sawdāʾ) — rose up against Umayyad rule in the year 116/734." Al-Ḥārith's revolt, which ran roughly 116–128/734–746, is a real movement that emerged from inside Khurāsān and allied with the Türgesh khāqān, bringing actual Turkic cavalry into the field against the established order under a black banner — the same symbol the Abbasid daʿwa would carry out of the same province a year after the revolt's end. Ibn Abī ʿArūba, an adult Baṣran scholar throughout this period and into the Abbasid revolution itself, lived through two successive, concrete versions of "a people emerging from Khurāsān" within his own working life.
</ContextNote>

## Cluster 5 — Muʿāwiyah: Genuine Multiple Attestation, Individually Weak

<HadithBlock
  label="Musnad Abī Yaʿlā, no. 7376"
  arabic="سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم يَقُولُ: لَتَظْهَرَنَّ التُّرْكُ عَلَى الْعَرَبِ، حَتَّى تُلْحِقَهَا بِمَنَابِتِ الشِّيحِ وَالْقَيْصُومِ"
  translation='"I heard the Messenger of God say: The Turks will surely prevail over the Arabs, until they drive them back to the wormwood-and-southernwood pastures."'
  source="Abū Yaʿlā, Musnad, no. 7376, via Muʿāwiyah b. Ḥudayj ← Isḥāq b. Ibrāhīm b. al-Ghamr (mawlā Sumūk) ← his father ← his grandfather ← Muʿāwiyah b. Ḥudayj; graded by Ḥusayn Salīm Asad as musalsal bi'l-majāhīl"
/>

This cluster genuinely has two independent routes — a real methodological rarity in this corpus — but neither survives isnād-critical scrutiny on its own. The Abū Yaʿlā route is, in the modern editor's own words, "a chain entirely of unknowns": Isḥāq's father and grandfather are both unnamed. The second route, preserved in Nuʿaym's *Kitāb al-Fitan* and independently in al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*, runs through an Egyptian network — Ibn Lahīʿa, Kaʿb b. ʿAlqama al-Tanūkhī, Ḥassān b. Kurayb, Ibn Dhī al-Kalāʿ — and specifies the frontier by name: a courier arrives from Armenia's governor reporting a Turkish raid, and Muʿāwiyah orders restraint, citing the Prophet's own words that "they will surely catch up with us at the wormwood-pastures."

<ContextNote title="Armenia, Not Egypt">
The Egyptian transmission network carrying Route B is misleading if read as evidence the report is really about Egypt. The content it carries is explicit: a governor of Armenia, a Turkish raid on the frontier, a caliph's order to stand down. That fits the Umayyad–Khazar war in the Caucasus far better than anything in Egypt — specifically the pattern already visible in Salmān b. Rabīʿa al-Bāhilī's own campaign at Balanjar a decade or two earlier, where, after capturing the city, his own men are recorded as suggesting a return the following year to finish the conquest, and Salmān answering that they would never conquer that land. The "we cannot press this frontier" sentiment this report attributes to Muʿāwiyah was already frontier lore before his caliphate began.
</ContextNote>

The doctrinal formula underneath both routes — "leave the Turks alone as long as they leave you alone" — has a third, independent line worth noting: a version attributed to Ibn Masʿūd, transmitted by al-Aʿmash from Zayd b. Wahb and Shaqīq b. Salama (both core members of Ibn Masʿūd's own Kufan circle), preserved in al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Kabīr* and *al-Awsaṭ*, with the added clause "for the first to strip my nation of what God granted them will be the Banū Qanṭūrāʾ" — the standard Arab genealogical label for Turkic peoples. Al-Aʿmash surfaces here for the second time in this corpus, again sitting at a bottleneck for Turk-related material funneled through his own narrow Kufan network. That does not prove this version is also a re-routing, but it repeats exactly the operating signature already documented in Cluster 1.

The "wormwood and southernwood" phrase itself deserves one further caution: it also appears, entirely independently, attached to a companion with no connection to any Turk tradition at all. Al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Awsaṭ* preserves, on the authority of ʿAbdullāh b. al-Sāʾib (graded *matrūk*), "the Arabs will reach the birthplace of their fathers, the wormwood-and-southernwood pastures" — with no mention of Turks, Khazars, or any enemy whatsoever. The phrase is a pre-existing idiom for Arabs reverting to nomadism at the end of days. Both Muʿāwiyah routes appear to graft an already-circulating formula onto their anecdote rather than preserving a phrase distinctive enough to prove common descent between them.

## What the Five Clusters Share

Three patterns cut across all five clusters and are worth naming directly.

First, **Baṣra is the dominant node outside the Medinan Zuhrī stream**. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (Cluster 3), Qatāda (Cluster 4), and Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba (also Cluster 4) are all Baṣran, all active in the same forty-year window, and Baṣra was the administrative and recruiting base for the entire Khurāsān command structure throughout this period. This is a sharper claim than "the Umayyad East was anxious about Turks in general" — it identifies a specific scholarly circle with a specific, direct institutional stake in exactly the wars this material describes.

Second, **the corpus's physiognomic and narrative vocabulary is largely a floating formula, not a fingerprint**. "Faces like hammered shields," "sandals of hair," and "tying horses to X" all recur across companion-attributions that share no transmitter, applied to different specific locations by different specific transmitters. This cuts against reading shared wording as proof of shared origin, and toward reading it as a common ethnographic and apocalyptic idiom available to any transmitter describing any steppe threat.

Third, **every recoverable bottleneck sits in the same seventy-year window**, roughly 700–750 CE — the exact span in which the Umayyad East absorbed Qutayba's Transoxianan conquests, the Day of Thirst, the Battle of the Defile, al-Ḥārith b. Surayj's Türgesh-allied revolt, and finally the Abbasid revolution itself. Whatever any individual Companion may or may not have said, the generation that made this material urgent enough to preserve, expand, and re-attribute was the generation that lived through the frontier's worst decades.

<VerdictBox>
Five companions, five bottlenecks, no shared origin. Abū Hurayra's physiognomic core plausibly reflects real news of Yazdegerd III's Central Asian allies reaching Medina in 650–651. ʿAmr b. Thaghlab's and Abū Bakr's versions cannot be traced past a single Baṣran transmitter each — al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba respectively — both of whom lived through Baṣra's own frontier disasters. Buraydah's version stops at Bashīr b. al-Muhājir, independently graded munkar al-ḥadīth. Muʿāwiyah's is the one cluster with genuine multiple attestation, and also the one where both routes are individually weakest. What survives across all five is not one authentic prophecy multiply confirmed, but a shared vocabulary repeatedly redeployed by transmitters each responding to their own city's version of the same two-generation crisis.
</VerdictBox>
