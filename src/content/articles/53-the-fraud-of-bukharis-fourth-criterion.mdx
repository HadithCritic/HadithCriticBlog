The defense of Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī often begins with a simple claim: al-Bukhārī was stricter than Muslim because he required proof that two adjacent narrators actually met. Muslim, by contrast, was said to accept a muʿanʿan chain when the two narrators were contemporaries, could plausibly have met, and the narrator was not known for tadlīs. The stricter rule is then treated as a badge of historical certainty. Bukhārī, we are told, did not merely collect plausible reports. He verified the links.

The problem is not that contact between transmitters is irrelevant. It clearly matters. If a narrator could not have met the person he cites, the chain has a serious problem. If he was a mudallis and uses an ambiguous “from,” the chain has another problem. If a report depends on an impossible chronology, it should be rejected. A meeting requirement can catch some broken links.

But the apologetic argument quietly changes the subject. It moves from **access** to **transmission**, and then from **transmission** to **Prophetic origin**. Those are three different claims. Showing that two narrators could meet, or even that they did meet once, does not show that the specific report under discussion was delivered in that meeting. It does not prove that the wording was preserved. It does not prove that the report did not grow in later teaching circles. It does not prove that the content goes back to the Prophet.

<ClaimBox title="The argument in one sentence">
Bukhārī’s alleged “fourth criterion” is useful as a chain-continuity filter, but it is fraudulent when sold as proof that a specific hadith was actually transmitted from the Prophet. At most, it shows that one possible route was open. It does not show what travelled through that route.
</ClaimBox>

This distinction is often blurred because the word **ṣaḥīḥ** sounds stronger than the method can bear. In classical hadith terminology, a report is ṣaḥīḥ when it satisfies a set of internal criteria: connected chain, upright transmitters, accurate transmitters, no contradiction, and no hidden defect. That is already a later scholarly classification. It is not a time machine. It does not take the reader back into a first/seventh-century conversation and make the alleged act of hearing visible.

## What the “Fourth Criterion” Actually Is

The issue concerns the **muʿanʿan** chain: a chain in which a narrator says “from so-and-so” rather than using an explicit audition formula such as “I heard” or “he narrated to us.” The word “from” is ambiguous. It can mean direct transmission, but it can also hide an intermediary.

Muslim’s position, stated in the introduction to his *Ṣaḥīḥ*, is that the report of a trustworthy non-mudallis narrator from a trustworthy contemporary should be treated as connected when meeting and hearing were possible, unless there is clear evidence to the contrary. He even attacks the stricter view as a late and invented claim.

<HadithBlock label="Muslim’s rule for the muʿanʿan chain" source="Muslim, Muqaddima to Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    وهذا القول، يرحمك الله، في الطعن في الأسانيد قولٌ مخترعٌ مستحدثٌ غير مسبوق صاحبه إليه... وذلك أن القول الشائع المتفق عليه بين أهل العلم بالأخبار والروايات قديماً وحديثاً أن كل رجل ثقة روى عن مثله حديثاً وجائز ممكن له لقاؤه والسماع منه لكونهما جميعاً كانا في عصر واحد، وإن لم يأت في خبر قط أنهما اجتمعا ولا تشافها بكلام، فالرواية ثابتة والحجة بها لازمة، إلا أن يكون هناك دلالة بينة أن هذا الراوي لم يلق من روى عنه أو لم يسمع منه شيئاً.
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    “This view, may God have mercy on you, about criticizing chains is an invented, newly introduced statement whose proponent was not preceded in it... The widespread view agreed upon among the scholars of reports and transmissions, early and late, is that whenever a trustworthy man narrates a hadith from someone like him, and it is possible for him to have met and heard from him because they were both in the same era, then even if no report ever states that they met or spoke face to face, the narration is established and proof by it is binding, unless there is clear evidence that this narrator did not meet the one from whom he narrated, or did not hear anything from him.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

That passage matters because the stricter rule was not self-evident even inside the hadith tradition. Muslim did not say, “Bukhārī has the obvious gold standard and I am choosing something weaker.” He argued that the stricter demand was itself an unjustified innovation in isnād criticism. Later writers then debated whether Muslim’s unnamed opponent was al-Bukhārī, ʿAlī b. al-Madīnī, Abū Zurʿa, or a broader group of early critics. Ibn Rajab later identifies the view with Ibn al-Madīnī and al-Bukhārī, while some later defenders tried to soften the attribution by saying that al-Bukhārī only followed this stricter practice in his *Ṣaḥīḥ*, not as a universal rule for all authentic reports.[^1]

So even before we critique the logic of the criterion, we should be clear about its status. “Bukhārī’s criterion” is not a rule written by al-Bukhārī in a preface to his book. It is a later reconstruction of his practice, defended, refined, and disputed by later scholars.

## A Rule Reconstructed After the Fact

Christopher Melchert’s study of al-Bukhārī’s *al-Tārīkh al-kabīr* is useful here because it cuts through the mythology surrounding “Bukhārī’s criterion.” Medieval scholars certainly spoke of **sharṭ al-Bukhārī**, but Melchert notes that this criterion was “deduced in retrospect from the Ṣaḥīḥ” and “never expounded directly by Bukhārī.”[^2] This is not a minor point. If the alleged criterion is reconstructed from the collection, then it should not be treated as though we possess al-Bukhārī’s own complete methodological manual.

Melchert also shows that *al-Tārīkh al-kabīr* cannot simply be treated as the external database that proves the *Ṣaḥīḥ*. It omits a portion of the transmitters who appear in al-Bukhārī’s hadith collections, gives personal evaluations for only a small share of its subjects, and does not consistently record birth and death data. Melchert’s conclusion is blunt: *al-Tārīkh al-kabīr* “hardly establishes even contemporaneity.”[^3]

This directly weakens the apologetic use of the criterion. The defender says that Bukhārī verified meetings. But where is that verification preserved? If the evidence is the same later biographical and isnād material whose reliability is under discussion, then the argument is not independent. It is a circle dressed as a database.

## Meeting Is Not Transmission

A meeting proves access. It does not prove delivery.

This is the most basic point, but it is often hidden behind technical language. Suppose we know that Narrator A met Narrator B in Basra. That fact can answer one question: *was direct transmission possible?* It cannot answer a different question: *did Narrator B actually deliver this exact report to Narrator A?* A meeting could involve a lecture, a legal question, a greeting, a commercial exchange, a shared prayer, a funeral, a journey, a visit, or nothing more than being present in the same circle. The existence of contact does not specify the content of contact.

Hadith criticism partially recognizes this because it distinguishes between formulas of transmission. “I heard” is stronger than “from.” “He narrated to us” is stronger than an ambiguous link. A written certificate, recitation session, or known teaching circle can add weight. But even explicit audition formulas do not solve every problem. A person can misremember what he heard. He can paraphrase. He can combine two reports. He can narrate from a notebook whose history is unclear. He can transmit a teacher’s later harmonized version. He can be honest and wrong.

That means the liqāʾ criterion does not solve the central problem of hadith authority. It does not turn reports into revelation. It only removes one kind of objection: “these two people could not have been connected.”

<ContextNote title="The fair version of the criterion">
The criterion is not useless. It is a reasonable historical filter against impossible or suspicious links. The problem begins when the filter is sold as proof. A working bridge does not prove what crossed it.
</ContextNote>

The same distinction applies to reputation. A narrator’s general reliability is not the same as the reliability of every report attributed to him. Classical critics knew this too. They sometimes accepted a narrator generally but rejected a particular report as mistaken, anomalous, or defective. That is why the five-part definition of ṣaḥīḥ includes not only uprightness and precision, but also freedom from shudhūdh and hidden ʿilla.[^4] The very existence of those last two conditions admits that a reliable narrator with a connected chain can still produce a defective report.

## The Biographical Database Is Not Neutral Ground

The apologetic version of the criterion often assumes that biographical data are stable, external facts. The hadith chain says A narrated from B; the biographical dictionary confirms A met B; therefore the chain is sound. But the biographical dictionary is part of the same tradition-world. It is built from reports, chains, memories, scholarly judgments, and later disputes.

Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s *Bayān khaṭaʾ Muḥammad b. Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī fī Tārīkhihi* is a good reminder. The work presents corrections to al-Bukhārī’s historical-biographical entries, transmitted from Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s father and Abū Zurʿa al-Rāzī. The very title says what it is doing: “Clarifying the errors of Muḥammad b. Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī in his *Tārīkh*.” The opening example corrects a lineage. Another early example says that ʿImrān b. Ẓabyān did not meet ʿAlī and only narrated from him through Abū Taḥyā.[^5]

That does not mean al-Bukhārī was incompetent. It means the rījal archive was a contested scholarly field. Names, lineages, dates, meetings, hearing claims, and teacher-student relations were all matters of investigation. They were not floating above the hadith system as an independent court of appeal.

Melchert’s point is especially damaging: it is hard to see how a traditionist could be certain that two transmitters met except by relying on accepted chains themselves.[^6] But if the chain is used to prove the meeting, and the meeting is then used to prove the chain, the logic is circular.

## Muslim’s Objection Exposes the Weakness

Muslim’s introduction is often treated as proof that he was less rigorous than Bukhārī. That is too simple. Muslim’s objection exposes something deeper: there was no universally obvious way to transform a muʿanʿan link into a historically proven act of transmission.

Muslim’s argument is pragmatic. If reliable contemporaries could plausibly meet, and there is no evidence of tadlīs or non-hearing, then the report is accepted. The stricter party says: no, we need proof of meeting or hearing. But both sides are still dealing with probability. Muslim’s probability threshold is lower. The stricter threshold is higher. Neither one produces direct access to the event itself.

That is why “Bukhārī is stricter than Muslim” does not mean “Bukhārī proves Prophetic speech.” It means Bukhārī, or the later reconstruction of his practice, demanded stronger evidence for a link. Stronger evidence for a link is still evidence for a link, not for the historical origin of the matn.

A defender may respond that hadith criticism does not rely on liqāʾ alone. It also uses transmitter criticism, variant comparison, shudhūdh, ʿilal, and corroboration. That is true. But then the defense must stop presenting the fourth criterion as though it solves the problem. It is only one part of a probabilistic apparatus. The whole apparatus still operates through later human testimony.

## The Real Burden: Specific Transmission, Not Possible Access

A serious historical method would ask a different set of questions:

  <div class="burden-row">
    <span class="burden-num">01</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Where does the report first become visible?</h3>
      <p>Is the report already spread across independent early lines, or does it first appear through one common link?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="burden-row">
    <span class="burden-num">02</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Do the variants point upward or outward?</h3>
      <p>Do multiple routes preserve a stable early core, or do they look like later branches from a single transmitter’s teaching circle?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="burden-row">
    <span class="burden-num">03</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Does the matn fit a later controversy?</h3>
      <p>Does the report answer a legal, political, sectarian, or theological problem that became urgent after the Prophet?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="burden-row">
    <span class="burden-num">04</span>
    <div>
      <h3>Can the chain be explained as prestige attachment?</h3>
      <p>Does the report gain authority by being attached to a famous Companion or teacher whose name serves the later argument?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

These are the questions that matter if the claim is historical. They move beyond “could these men have met?” and ask whether the report behaves like early memory or later attribution. Modern isnād-cum-matn analysis, whatever its limits, is useful precisely because it refuses to stop at a single chain. It looks at the family of variants, distribution, wording, common links, and historical setting.[^7]

This does not mean every hadith is false. It means the burden of proof is much higher than “Bukhārī required meeting.” A chain can be formally respectable and still carry a report whose content crystallized later.

## The False Comfort of “He Would Have Checked”

The appeal to Bukhārī’s piety and diligence is not an argument. It is a character appeal. A scholar can be brilliant and still work with limited information. He can be sincere and still inherit mistaken biographical data. He can be careful and still classify a report according to the best tools available in his time, not according to what actually happened two centuries earlier.

The issue is not whether Bukhārī was smarter than us or more devoted than us. The issue is whether his method can bear the theological weight placed on it.

When later Muslims say that Bukhārī’s stricter condition makes his collection uniquely reliable, they are making a claim about evidence. Evidence has to be examined. Piety cannot substitute for access. Discipline cannot substitute for documentation. A rigorous probability remains a probability.

<ContextNote title="What the criterion really buys">
The stricter rule can make a chain less vulnerable to one objection: impossible or unproven contact. It does not certify the contents of the report. It does not remove the need for matn criticism, variant analysis, historical context, and independent corroboration.
</ContextNote>

## The Qur’anic Standard Is Different

The Qur’an repeatedly directs the reader back to God’s revealed book as the criterion. It does not ask believers to construct religion on probabilistic chains of post-Prophetic testimony. It tells them to verify, not to follow what they do not know.

<QuranVerse verse="49:6" />

For a Qur’an-first reader, the problem with Bukhārī’s fourth criterion is therefore not merely technical. It is theological. A method that can at best produce ranked probabilities is being used to authorize religious claims as though they were revealed certainty.

<QuranVerse verse="6:114" />

<QuranVerse verse="6:38" />

<QuranVerse verse="45:6" />

The issue is not whether hadith literature can preserve useful historical material. It can. The issue is whether a later authentication label can transform a fallible report into binding religion. The fourth criterion cannot do that. It was never capable of doing that.

## The Verdict

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">
Bukhārī’s alleged fourth criterion is strongest when treated modestly: it is a test of whether a direct chain is plausible. It becomes fraudulent when apologists use it to imply that a specific report has been historically proven back to the Prophet. Meeting is not transmission. Transmission is not preservation. Preservation is not Prophetic origin. A chain can be formally possible and still fail as revelation.
</VerdictBox>

The honest conclusion is not that Bukhārī was careless. It is that the apologetic presentation is inflated. The criterion may make Bukhārī stricter than Muslim on one technical question, but it does not overcome the deeper epistemic problem. It cannot prove the event of narration. It cannot prove the matn. It cannot prove that God authorized this report as religion.

A method that only establishes possible access should not be marketed as historical certainty.
