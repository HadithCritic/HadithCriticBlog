The narrative of **early Islamic consensus (*Ijmāʿ*)** is one of the most persistent and heavily guarded myths in traditional Sunni hadith scholarship. We are told that the *Ṣaḥāba* (Companions of the Prophet) formed a unified, harmonious front, accepting and transmitting hadith with total consistency. This supposed consensus is weaponized today to silence critics: *"How can you doubt a hadith when the Companions all agreed upon it?"*

But the historical evidence—found within the Sunni corpus itself—tells a dramatically different story. 

Far from unanimous agreement, the Companions engaged in heated disputes over specific hadith, questioned each other's memory, and often rejected one another's reports entirely based on their contradiction with the Quran. Some, like the second Caliph, even enforced strict bans on hadith transmission. 

If the primary generation of Muslims, who supposedly lived through the events in question, disagreed this violently over what the Prophet did or said, then claims of a pristine, unified "consensus" become nothing more than retroactive mythology.

## Aisha vs. Abu Hurairah: The Quranic Filter

Perhaps no conflict illustrates the chaos of early hadith transmission better than the fierce tension between ʿĀʾisha (the Prophet's wife) and Abu Hurairah (the most prolific hadith transmitter in history). 

Aisha did not passively accept narrations simply because another Companion claimed "The Prophet said..." When Abu Hurairah circulated narrations that violated the ethical universals of the Quran, she aggressively shut him down.

<ClaimBox>
**Abu Hurairah's Claims (al-Ḥākim, al-Mustadrak 2910):**
Abu Hurairah circulated that the Prophet said:
1. "The child of adultery is the worst of the three."
2. "The deceased is punished due to the crying of the living."
</ClaimBox>

When news of these narrations reached Aisha, she flatly rejected them as false attributions, stating: **"May Allah have mercy on Abu Hurairah—he misheard and thus misunderstood."** She then systematically dismantled his claims using the Quran:

*   **On the child of adultery:** She rejected the idea that an innocent child bears the sin of their parents, quoting the Quran: *"No bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another"* (6:164).
*   **On the deceased being punished:** She rejected the idea that a dead person is tortured because their family is mourning, quoting the Quran: *"Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity"* (2:286).

This wasn’t a rare exception. It was Aisha’s consistent hermeneutic: **The Quran, not a Companion's memory, was the ultimate criterion for truth.**

In another famous incident recorded in the *Musnad of Aḥmad* (Hadith 25557), two men told Aisha that Abu Hurairah was narrating: *"Bad omens are in the woman, the mount, and the house."* Aisha became so infuriated that the narrator described her as metaphorically splitting between the earth and the sky. She swore an oath: 

> *"By the One who revealed the Qur’an to Abu al-Qasim, this is not what he used to say!... The people of Jahiliyyah (pre-Islamic ignorance) used to say that."*

She then recited Quran 57:22 to prove that superstitious omens violate the Islamic theology of divine decree. 

## Did Muhammad See God?

Aisha's absolute commitment to Quranic theology over hearsay is perfectly captured in her response to the question of whether the Prophet saw God during the Night Journey (*Miʿrāj*).

When asked by Masrūq, *"O my mother, did Muhammad see his Lord?"* she responded with shock:

> *"My hair stood on end because of what you said! Whoever tells you that Muhammad saw his Lord has lied."* (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4855)

She immediately cited the Quran: *"Vision perceives Him not, but He perceives all vision"* (6:103) and *"It is not for any human being that Allah should speak to him except by revelation or from behind a veil"* (42:51).

<ContextNote>
Despite Aisha's emphatic, Quran-backed denial, other Companions (like Ibn ʿAbbās) claimed the exact opposite—that the Prophet *did* see God. This created a massive theological schism. How can there be a "consensus of the Companions" on the most fundamental theological event of the Prophet's life when the Companions themselves were calling each other liars over it?
</ContextNote>

## Caliph Umar's Ban on Hadith

If you believe traditional apologetics, you would think the early Caliphs spent their time eagerly gathering and writing down hadith. The reality is the opposite. The second Caliph, ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, took extraordinary, state-backed measures to **suppress** hadith transmission.

Umar forbade the Companions from quoting the sayings of the Prophet, fearing that it would distract the masses from the Quran and lead to the fabrication of lies.

According to multiple early sources (including al-Dhahabī’s *Tadhkirat al-Ḥuffāẓ*), Umar went so far as to physically detain prominent Companions like Ibn Masʿūd, Abu al-Dardāʾ, and Abu Masʿūd al-Anṣārī in Medina because they were narrating too many hadith. 

He was especially harsh with Abu Hurairah, threatening him with exile:

> *"You shall leave alone quoting the Messenger of Allāh, or I shall send you back to the land of Daws or to the land of apes!"* (Tārīkh Abī Zurʿah al-Dimashqī)

This is a devastating historical reality: the second Caliph of Islam was so terrified of hadith fabrication that he literally imprisoned and threatened the men who would later become the foundational pillars of the Sunni hadith corpus.

## The Chaos of Mutʿah (Temporary Marriage)

To truly witness the collapse of the "Consensus" myth, we only need to look at the conflicting reports regarding *Mutʿah* (temporary marriage). Sunni jurisprudence claims the Prophet definitively banned it. But when you look at *when*, *where*, and *who* banned it, the hadith corpus descends into irreconcilable chaos.

**The Contradictions:**
1. If ʿAlī is correct that it was banned at Khaybar, then why does Sabrah claim it was permitted and then banned *years later* at the Conquest of Mecca?
2. If the Prophet definitively banned it, why did Jābir b. ʿAbdullāh swear that the Companions freely practiced it during the entire reign of Abu Bakr, only for ʿUmar to ban it later?
3. If there was a consensus, why did Ibn ʿAbbās famously issue legal rulings permitting it long after the Prophet's death, causing ʿAlī to publicly scold him?

This is not consensus. This is historical amnesia, retroactive projection, and sectarian confusion.

## The Verdict: Consensus is a Retroactive Construct

If this level of fundamental disagreement existed among the primary eyewitnesses regarding theology, ethics, and law, then later claims of "authentic consensus" become historically implausible. 

The idea that 2nd and 3rd-century compilers (like al-Bukhārī and Muslim) could somehow recover a pristine "consensus" that didn't even exist in the 1st century is a fantasy. What Sunnism calls "consensus" is actually the result of:
- Retroactive harmonization of contradictory sources.
- Selective preservation of certain political narratives.
- Institutional pressure to create unified legal frameworks for the Abbasid empire.

The historical evidence shows that disagreement, not consensus, was the norm. Early Islamic teachings were contested, debated, and often deeply contradictory.
