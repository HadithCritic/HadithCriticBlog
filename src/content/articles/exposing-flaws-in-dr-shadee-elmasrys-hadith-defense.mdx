Following the Yasir Qadhi incident, where he admitted, “Nobody in the academy affirms the Muslim Sunni science of hadith. Nobody. It is considered to be completely discredited. I’m just being factual,” Dr. Shadee Elmasry posted a response addressing the controversy.

In that response, Elmasry tried to defend traditional hadith epistemology by attacking what he called “Western epistemology.” But a closer look shows that his response depends on distortion, caricature, selective standards, and repeated straw men.

[Original clip referenced here](https://twitter.com/talkquran/status/1944887524964622694)

<ClaimBox>
Elmasry’s defense does not actually answer the academic critique of hadith. It replaces the critique with a caricature, then attacks that caricature as if it were the real argument.
</ClaimBox>

## Western Epistemology Is Inherently Biased

**Transcript Timestamp:** 00:00:32–00:02:08

**Dr. Shadee Elmasry’s Argument Summary:**

> The Western historical method is not neutral. It comes with its own theological and philosophical biases, just like any other tradition. The idea that we should adopt their critical approach to hadith is flawed because their epistemology is based on materialism, skepticism, and anti-religious presumptions.

Elmasry constructs a reductive caricature of what he calls “Western epistemology.” He portrays it as:

- inherently atheistic
- born from anti-Church trauma
- motivated by the assumption that “everyone lied to us”

This is deeply misleading. The academic critical method used in historical studies and hadith studies is not an atheistic manifesto. It is a methodological posture that withholds metaphysical assumptions. It does not deny God, prophecy, miracles, or the unseen. It simply refuses to treat supernatural claims as historically proven unless the evidence can be examined publicly.

That is not atheism. That is method.

Even Dr. Joshua Little, an Oxford-trained scholar in Islamic history and a practitioner of the method Elmasry criticizes, explicitly rejects this caricature:

> “I’m not ruling out miracles. I’m not ruling out God. I’m not ruling out prophecy. I’m not ruling out any of that. I’m happy to concede, for the sake of argument, that prophecy is possible. Miracles are possible. Other kinds of supernatural things are possible. I’ll grant it all. The argument still stands because it’s not based on an a priori rejection of those things. This is completely untrue.”
>
> — Joshua Little, in conversation with Dr. Javad T. Hashmi

Therefore, the claim that academic hadith criticism is driven by a rejection of the unseen is false. Methodological agnosticism is not the same thing as metaphysical disbelief.

<ContextNote>
The historian does not need to deny miracles in order to ask whether a specific miracle report has been transmitted reliably. Those are different questions.
</ContextNote>

## Genetic Fallacy: Dismissing a Method Because of Its Origin

**Transcript Timestamp:** 00:02:08–00:03:41

**Dr. Shadee Elmasry’s Argument Summary:**

> The Western historical method originated during the Renaissance and Enlightenment because people were reacting to lies and corruption by the Church, such as fake relics and forged documents attributed to apostles. Because of this, Western thinkers developed a critical view of religious history. Their motivation was rooted in betrayal and trauma. Therefore, their entire approach is built on the presumption that historical religious claims are suspicious, false, or invented.

Elmasry’s claim has three parts:

1. The Western historical-critical method arose as a reaction to Church corruption and forgeries.
2. The method was motivated by betrayal and psychological distrust.
3. Therefore, its entire epistemological foundation is tainted by skepticism and anti-religious assumptions.

This is a genetic fallacy. Elmasry tries to discredit a method by pointing to one alleged origin story for it.

Even if we granted his simplified history, the conclusion would not follow. A method is not invalid merely because it developed in response to forgery, corruption, or deception. If that were true, Islamic hadith science would also be invalid, because it too arose in response to widespread fabrication.

The isnād system, narrator criticism, and principles of jarḥ wa-taʿdīl were all developed because Muslims recognized that people were lying about the Prophet.

Ironically, many early defenders of hadith made the exact move Elmasry mocks. They introduced harsher scrutiny because fabrication existed. Ibn Ḥibbān, al-Khaṭīb al-Baghdādī, and others did not think that responding to deception invalidated their method. They believed it strengthened it.

So Elmasry’s logic, if applied consistently, would also dismantle his own tradition.

<VerdictBox title="The Problem">
What matters is not why a method began, but whether the method works, whether it is consistent, and whether it survives scrutiny.
</VerdictBox>

## Projection onto Other Civilizations

**Transcript Timestamp:** 00:03:41–00:04:46

**Dr. Shadee Elmasry’s Argument Summary:**

> Western scholars say Muslims are just like Europeans. Europeans made up relics, forged documents, and lied about saints, so now they assume Muslims fabricated their religious traditions too.

Elmasry presents academic hadith criticism as a form of projection. According to him, Western scholars experienced deception in their own religious tradition and then projected that experience onto Islam.

This argument combines a straw man with an appeal-to-motive fallacy.

Academic historians do not begin with the assumption that “everyone is lying.” They begin with methodological skepticism. That means claims require evidence, sources require testing, and traditions must be examined rather than merely inherited.

This is not unique to Islam. The same method is applied to Christian texts, Jewish texts, Greco-Roman texts, inscriptions, chronicles, and legal documents.

Scholars like Patricia Crone, Michael Cook, John Wansbrough, Harald Motzki, G. H. A. Juynboll, Joseph Schacht, Gregor Schoeler, and Joshua Little did not all simply project European Church trauma onto Islam. Their conclusions differ from one another, and many of them disagree sharply. But they are all engaged in historical criticism, textual analysis, source criticism, and transmission analysis.

Elmasry reduces all of that into a psychological accusation.

That is not an argument.

## Dismissing Academic Consensus as Orientalism or Imperialism

**Transcript Timestamp:** 00:04:46–00:06:15

**Dr. Shadee Elmasry’s Argument Summary:**

> Western scholarship on Islam is Orientalist or imperialist. It imposes a Western worldview on Muslims and seeks to tear down Islamic tradition because the West could not preserve its own.

This is a classic escape route. When the evidence becomes difficult, the critic is accused of bad motives.

Elmasry’s argument suggests that academic hadith criticism is not scholarship but colonialism, envy, revenge, or ideological domination. But this avoids the actual question.

Are the arguments sound?

Are the sources early?

Are the chains historically plausible?

Can the reports be traced before the common links?

Do the matns correlate with the isnāds?

Can the tradition survive comparison with external evidence?

Calling something “Orientalist” does not answer any of those questions. It may describe a real historical problem in some cases, but it cannot function as a magic word that dismisses all criticism.

<ClaimBox title="The Evasion">
Calling a method “Western,” “Orientalist,” or “imperialist” does not refute the method. It only changes the subject from evidence to identity.
</ClaimBox>

## Misrepresenting the Satanic Verses Episode

**Transcript Timestamp:** 00:06:16–00:07:12

**Dr. Shadee Elmasry’s Argument Summary:**

> If the Prophet had uttered the so-called Satanic Verses, the disbelievers would have brought it up, and the Companions would have talked about it. The story is fabricated and is refuted by the Qur’an itself, especially Sūrat al-Najm, where God says the Prophet neither strayed nor erred.

Elmasry’s claim is not merely weak. It is historically misleading.

He treats the Satanic Verses episode as if it rests on one weak narration. That erases centuries of Islamic scholarship, including mainstream Sunni scholars who reported the event, discussed it, and in many cases accepted it in some form.

Shahab Ahmed’s book-length study, *Before Orthodoxy: The Satanic Verses in Early Islam*, compiles and analyzes over fifty isnāds for the episode. These reports are not from some obscure sectarian fringe. They include material attributed through or connected to:

- Ibn ʿAbbās
- Saʿīd b. Jubayr
- ʿIkrimah
- Abū al-ʿĀliyah
- Qatādah
- al-Ḍaḥḥāk
- Muqātil
- al-Kalbī
- ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr’s maghāzī material

In other words, this is not “one fabricated narration.” It is a multi-strand tradition preserved across early exegetical, historical, and sīrah material.

Elmasry also claims that early scholars would not have accepted such a story. That is demonstrably false.

Ibn Taymiyyah, often cited as a major figure of traditional orthodoxy, affirms the historical credibility of the episode in *Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā*:

> “The early Islamic scholars collectively considered the verses of the cranes in accordance with the Qur’an. And from the later scholars who followed the opinion of the early scholars, they say that these traditions have been recorded with authentic chains of narration and it is impossible to deny them, and the Qur’an itself testifies to it.”
>
> — Ibn Taymiyyah, *Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā*, vol. 10

This refers to Qur’an 22:52:

<QuranVerse verse="22:52" />

So contrary to Elmasry’s framing:

- early scholars did not necessarily see the incident as an insult to the Prophet
- many saw it as a test, consistent with the Qur’anic pattern of divine correction
- the event was cited to reinforce the Prophet’s truthfulness, not necessarily to deny it

Major scholars such as al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Saʿd, Ibn Isḥāq, al-Zamakhsharī, and al-Bayḍāwī transmitted or discussed versions of the story. Shahab Ahmed explains:

> “The Satanic Verses was not a marginal or obscure episode; it was recorded, transmitted, and commented upon by central figures in the formation of the Islamic scholarly tradition.”
>
> — Shahab Ahmed, *Before Orthodoxy*, p. 48

Elmasry’s claim that “no one accepted it” reflects a later theological discomfort, not the actual texture of the early tradition.

Even his argument that “the disbelievers or Companions would have talked about it” fails, because the reports themselves say that the pagans did react. They allegedly praised the Prophet when they thought he had acknowledged their deities. That reaction is part of the story.

So instead of dealing with the evidence, Elmasry brushes it off as a lie. But the same hadith sciences he defends preserved this story through multiple early channels. Ignoring those channels does not protect hadith science. It exposes selective use of it.

## Hadith Science Is the Most Rational and Accurate Historical Method

**Elmasry’s Claim:**

> Hadith is based on rational inquiry. It is the most thorough history you are going to find in any subject.

Elmasry says that hadith is the most thorough historical method available. On the surface, this sounds impressive. There are chains, names, dates, narrator biographies, and detailed evaluations.

But once we look beneath the surface, the method is not nearly as solid as he suggests.

The core of hadith science is the isnād system. It traces who said what, and who allegedly heard from whom. That sounds objective, but the system depends on assumptions that cannot be independently verified.

The most important assumption is that the Companions are upright by default.

Not because every Companion can be individually verified. Not because every Companion was transparent. Not because every Companion’s motives are recoverable. But because later Sunni theory grants them collective uprightness.

That assumption runs directly into the Qur’an.

<QuranVerse verse="9:101" />

That verse is devastating for the traditional assumption. God tells the Prophet that there were hypocrites around him and among the people of Medina whom even he did not know. If the Prophet himself did not know all of the hypocrites around him, how can later scholars confidently declare the entire Companion generation upright by default?

That is not a small problem. It means the system assumes what the Qur’an complicates.

Beyond that, traditional hadith criticism focuses heavily on isnād reliability while often treating matn criticism as secondary. If the chain checks out, reports with strange or implausible contents can still pass. This is how reports about Adam being ninety feet tall, or seven ajwa dates protecting from poison and magic, can survive as ṣaḥīḥ material.

<ContextNote>
The problem is not that hadith scholars never used matn criticism. The problem is that isnād authentication often allowed bizarre or theologically loaded reports to survive once the transmitters had been judged reliable.
</ContextNote>

There is also a double standard.

If Christians presented a fully developed version of ʿilm al-rijāl, complete with chains of transmission and biographies of early Church fathers, no Muslim scholar would accept that as proof that the Gospels are divinely preserved or that Church doctrine is true. Muslims would still reject claims about Jesus, the Trinity, the crucifixion, papal authority, and Church tradition, regardless of how carefully Christian transmitters rated one another.

So why should outsiders accept the same logic when Muslims apply it to hadith?

You cannot demand epistemic charity for your own tradition while denying it to others.

## Final Assessment

Elmasry’s response fails because it does not actually answer the strongest criticisms of hadith transmission.

He caricatures academic historical method as atheistic and anti-religious. He dismisses criticism by attacking its alleged origin. He accuses scholars of projection and imperialism rather than answering their evidence. He misrepresents the Satanic Verses episode as one weak report, even though early Islamic tradition preserved it through multiple routes. And he praises hadith science as uniquely rational while ignoring its own circular assumptions.

The issue is not whether hadith scholars were intelligent. Many were brilliant.

The issue is whether the method proves what later Sunni theology needs it to prove.

It does not.

Hadith science is not neutral machinery that simply recovers the Prophet’s words. It is a tradition-bound system built on assumptions, inherited trust, retrospective biography, communal memory, and selective standards. It can preserve history. It can also preserve theology, polemic, legend, and institutional authority.

Elmasry’s defense does not solve those problems.

It exposes them.
