*How Abū Bakra’s defense of his neutrality became a saying of the Prophet—and how Ibn Jabala manufactured the appearance of a second Companion witness.*

## A Prophecy with a Convenient Ending

In 36 AH (656 CE), ʿĀʾisha, accompanied by Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, became the most visible figure in an armed political coalition that entered Baṣra and eventually fought the forces of ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib. The encounter became known as the Battle of the Camel. ʿĀʾisha’s side lost. One Basran Companion, Abū Bakra Nufayʿ b. al-Ḥārith, is remembered for refusing to join her.

That refusal was eventually explained with a hadith. According to the version in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 4425, Abū Bakra said:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī, no. 4425" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/64/83">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“God benefited me during the days of the Camel through a saying that I had heard from the Messenger of God, after I had almost joined the people of the Camel and fought alongside them … ‘A people who entrust their affair to a woman will never prosper.’”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَقَدْ نَفَعَنِي اللَّهُ بِكَلِمَةٍ سَمِعْتُهَا مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ أَيَّامَ الْجَمَلِ بَعْدَ مَا كِدْتُ أَنْ أَلْحَقَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْجَمَلِ فَأُقَاتِلَ مَعَهُمْ ... لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةً</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Muḥammad had allegedly learned that the Persians had made Kisrā’s daughter their ruler. The Prophet then pronounced the warning. Abū Bakra remembered it decades later, just when ʿĀʾisha’s movement presented him with a political choice.[^1]

Another major recension is even more explicit. In al-Tirmidhī 2262, Abū Bakra says:

<HadithBlock source="al-Jāmiʿ, al-Tirmidhī, no. 2262" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tirmidhi/33/75">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“When ʿĀʾisha arrived—that is, in Baṣra—I remembered the statement of the Messenger of God, and God protected me through it.”[^2]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>فَلَمَّا قَدِمَتْ عَائِشَةُ، يَعْنِي الْبَصْرَةَ، ذَكَرْتُ قَوْلَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ فَعَصَمَنِي اللَّهُ بِهِ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The narrative thus already contains its own interpretation. The Persian episode is not being recalled for the sake of Sasanian history. It serves as the pretext for Abū Bakra’s conduct in a much later Iraqi conflict. The prediction seems to have been made for the precise moment at which its narrator required it. A woman was leading a defeated political coalition, and Abū Bakra wanted his abstention to appear divinely vindicated.

The familiar legal question (whether the statement bans women from political office) therefore comes *after* a more basic historical one: **who first placed this on the Prophet’s tongue, and why?**

## The Battle of the Camel Explains the Tradition

The political context is more illuminating than a generic dispute about gender. Baṣra was divided during the First Fitna. Some supported ʿĀʾisha’s coalition, some supported ʿAlī, and others wanted to avoid Muslim civil war altogether. Abū Bakra belonged to the latter category in the sources that construct his public image.

Elizabeth Urban’s *Conquered Populations in Early Islam* devotes an entire chapter to Abū Bakra. She identifies the Battle of the Camel as one of the key events through which later Muslims remembered him. He is portrayed not simply as an enemy of ʿĀʾisha, but as a figure who repeatedly advocated political withdrawal and reconciliation. Urban also traces how stories of Abū Bakra’s origins, manumission, relationship with his half-brother Ziyād b. Abīhi and legal controversies were shaped by the competing needs of historical and hadith literature.[^3]

<HadithCriticBlog id="prophecies-eschatology/85-al-hasans-reconciliation-prophecy-and-al-amashs-kufan-dive" />

The proposed origin of the woman-ruler hadith is that he articulated a *justification for not joining either side*—and did so in a way that specifically rendered ʿĀʾisha’s command illegitimate in retrospect. The wider stance was anti-*fitna*; this particular proof was gendered.

The structure of the story tells us a lot. First comes the purported prediction about Persia; then Abū Bakra’s temptation to join the Camel forces; then his recollection of the prediction; finally, the defeat that seems to prove his recollection correct. It goes from a contestable political decision into obedience to a Prophetic warning. It also protects Abū Bakra from the charge that he had abandoned a righteous cause. According to his account, he possessed knowledge that those who went to battle failed to heed.

This kind of narrative is not rendered historically secure simply because it uses the first person. When a source explicitly tells us that a character’s decision was justified by a prophecy which the subsequent event appeared to fulfill, that provides a potential *Sitz im Leben*—the time and place where the report would have done its most useful work. For this saying, that setting is **post-Camel Baṣra**, not the Sasanian court during Muḥammad’s lifetime.

## Why Abū Bakra Is the Source

Hadith collections contain many chains for this report, but a large number of chains is not the same as a large number of independent witnesses. To determine independence we must ask where the lines converge.

The principal lines of transmission all return to **Abū Bakra**. The dominant Basran branch runs through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110 AH), who has at least three identifiable pupils passing on versions of the saying: **ʿAwf b. Abī Jamīla**, **Ḥumayd al-Ṭawīl**, and **Mubārak b. Faḍāla**. Their versions differ in expression and narrative setting, but not in their claimed Companion authority. The ʿAwf branch contains al-Bukhārī’s explicit Camel explanation; the Ḥumayd branch contains al-Tirmidhī’s reference to ʿĀʾisha’s arrival; and the Mubārak branch often preserves just the short statement.[^4]

A further channel bypasses al-Ḥasan: **Abū Bakra → ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Jawshan → ʿUyayna b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān**. The saying then branches to several students of ʿUyayna, including Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Qaṭṭān, Muḥammad b. Bakr, Yazīd b. Hārūn and Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī. Its usual wording is:

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tayalisi/34">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“A people who assign their affair to a woman will never prosper.”[^5]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

This makes ʿUyayna a **partial common link** (PCL); the text demonstrably circulated through several of his pupils.

The same distinction applies to Abū Bakra’s household. Al-Bazzār records one path through his son **ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz**, via Abū al-Minhāl al-Bakrāwī; this version identifies Kisrā’s daughter as **Būrān**. Another path runs through his son **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān**, via ʿAlī b. Zayd b. Judʿān, and survives in Aḥmad 20508. Its peculiar opening, *wa-qāla Abū Bakra*, is part of a sequence inheriting its isnād from the preceding report: it is not a free-standing witness without a chain. Both family paths, notwithstanding their individual weaknesses, still end with Abū Bakra.[^6]

A related household narrative, through **Bakkār b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz**, portrays the Prophet being informed that an enemy people were governed by a woman, after which he says, **<Arabic>هَلَكَتِ الرِّجَالُ حِينَ أَطَاعَتِ النِّسَاءَ</Arabic>**—“Men perished when they obeyed women.” This is *not the same wording* as the main maxim and should not be counted as a duplicate witness. But it shows a wider female-rule theme associated with Abū Bakra and his descendants.[^7]

The overall picture is thus clear:

The common link in the reconstruction advanced here is Abū Bakra. The one figure shared by the substantial Companion-attributed transmission network. Al-Ḥasan and ʿUyayna are downstream distributors, not alternative Companion origins. As with any common-link argument, this identifies the likeliest historically recoverable author of the attribution rather than mechanically proving everything above or below him. The person-specific Camel setting gives us the independent historical reason to identify Abū Bakra, not just the shape of the isnāds.

## The Persian Story Keeps Changing

If the Prophet had made a memorable prediction concerning a particular royal succession, one might expect the event to have remained comparatively stable. In the surviving recensions, however, the Persian story and even the maxim’s verb are conspicuously mobile.

- Al-Bukhārī’s ʿAwf version says that **the people of Persia appointed Kisrā’s daughter**. 
- The Ḥumayd version in al-Tirmidhī says that **Kisrā died**, after which the Prophet asked who succeeded him. 
- A report through Abū Bakra’s son **names the daughter Būrān**. A different Ḥumayd variant includes a **Persian messenger** arriving to announce events at the court.
- Al-Ḥākim 8599 even makes the royal referent uncertain: **“Kisrā or one of the kings of the non-Arabs.”** 
- A version of the same Ḥumayd → al-Ḥasan → Abū Bakra chain refers not to a Persian ruler at all, but to **the king of Dhū Yazan**, moving the occasion to a South Arabian setting.[^8]

The report also oscillates among several ways of describing female rule:

<HadithBlock source="Recension comparison: Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī, no. 4425; al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921; al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, nos. 20438 and 20517">

**<Arabic>وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةً</Arabic>** — “They entrusted their affair to a woman.”

**<Arabic>أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>** — “They assigned their affair to a woman.”

**<Arabic>تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>** — “A woman rules them.”

**<Arabic>يَلِي أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>** — “A woman administers their affair.”

</HadithBlock>

These reflect real changes in diction, accompanying stories and geographical referents. Yet the practical judgment remains constant: a people led by a woman will fail.

That is what one would expect if the political function were more stable than the remembered occasion. The Persian royal story supplies an ancient-looking setting for a maxim that acquired pointed relevance during the Battle of the Camel. It can be abbreviated, expanded or replaced without dislodging the claim that Abū Bakra had possessed advance knowledge of ʿĀʾisha’s failure.


## The Missing Second Companion

If Abū Bakra originated the report, how should we explain the hadith that attributes the same statement to **Jābir b. Samura**?

This is the only other Companion-level attribution of the *same maxim* identified in the corpus examined here. It appears in al-Ṭabarānī’s *al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ*, no. 4861, through the following chain:

<HadithBlock translation={"ʿAbd al-Wārith b. Ibrāhīm, from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala, from Abū ʿAwāna, from Simāk b. Ḥarb, from Jābir b. Samura."} source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861 (chain excerpt)" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>عَبْدُ الْوَارِثِ بْنُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ ← عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنُ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ ← أَبُو عَوَانَةَ ← سِمَاكُ بْنُ حَرْبٍ ← جَابِرُ بْنُ سَمُرَةَ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The reported saying is:

<HadithBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper.”[^9]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

At a glance this looks like independent corroboration. Jābir is a different Companion; Simāk and Abū ʿAwāna are familiar hadith transmitters. But an isnād’s *appearance* of independence is not evidence that the particular matn was actually circulated by those earlier figures.

Al-Ṭabarānī explicitly identifies the point at which the Jābir version becomes visible:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861 (authorial uniqueness note)" sourceUrl="https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-13/h/335995">

“This hadith is not transmitted from Jābir b. Samura except through this isnād. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala alone transmitted it.”[^10]

**<Arabic>لَا يُرْوَى هَذَا الْحَدِيثُ عَنْ جَابِرِ بْنِ سَمُرَةَ إِلَّا بِهَذَا الْإِسْنَادِ، تَفَرَّدَ بِهِ عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنُ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

That observation changes the entire evidentiary picture. We do **not** have a second, multiply branching network through Jābir. We have **one isolated narrator**, Ibn Jabala, attributing a familiar Abū Bakra maxim to another Companion. No independent pupil of Simāk or Abū ʿAwāna has been identified preserving *this saying* through Jābir. One later pupil, ʿAbd al-Wārith b. Ibrāhīm, carries it onward to al-Ṭabarānī; al-Haythamī could not identify that transmitter with confidence.[^10]

This is what hadith historians mean by a possible *spread of isnāds* or *diving*: a known report is given the appearance of an alternative earlier source by adding a chain that bypasses its actual point of dissemination. Here the dive does not bypass al-Ḥasan alone; it bypasses **Abū Bakra**, supplying an entirely different Companion.

## Ibn Jabala: The Man Behind the Jābir Isnād

The question is therefore no longer whether Jābir and Abū Bakra both transmitted the maxim. It is **why Ibn Jabala is the only identifiable person to transmit it through Jābir**.

The answer becomes more revealing when we examine what the classical hadith critics actually said about him. Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī reports encountering Ibn Jabala in Baṣra and writing down his hadith before rejecting them:

<QuoteBlock source="Mīzān al-Iʿtidāl, al-Dhahabī, 2:580, entry ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala" sourceUrl="https://www.taraajem.com/persons/175728/عبد-الرحمن-بن-عمرو-بن-جبلة">

“I wrote his hadith down in Baṣra. He used to lie, so I struck out his hadith.”[^11]

**<Arabic>كَتَبْتُ عَنْهُ بِالْبَصْرَةِ، وَكَانَ يَكْذِبُ، فَضَرَبْتُ عَلَى حَدِيثِهِ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

More importantly, al-Dāraquṭnī did not merely regard Ibn Jabala as a narrator prone to memory errors. He explicitly accused him of *fabricating hadith*—and he did so while investigating an entirely different tradition in which Ibn Jabala had supplied a suspiciously connected isnād.

When the **only** independent-looking Companion witness to the woman-ruler maxim passes through a transmitter so described, the Jābir version ceases to be a serious independent counterweight to the Abū Bakra corpus. The observed bottleneck and Ibn Jabala’s documented behavior point in the same direction.

## A Control Case: Turning a *Mursal* Report into a Companion Hadith

Al-Dāraquṭnī’s *Sunan* contains an especially important comparison concerning laughter during prayer. In one version of the story, a blind man falls into a well; some worshippers laugh; the Prophet allegedly orders them to repeat their ablution and prayer.

In the version cited by al-Dāraquṭnī no. 603, Ibn Jabala claims to transmit through **Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → Qatāda → Abū al-ʿĀliya and Anas b. Mālik**. The addition of **Anas**, a Companion, turns the material into a connected Prophetic report. Al-Dāraquṭnī comments:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 603" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58">

“No one narrated this from Salām except ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala. He is abandoned and fabricates hadith.”[^12]

**<Arabic>لَمْ يَرْوِهِ عَنْ سَلَّامٍ غَيْرُ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ، وَهُوَ مَتْرُوكٌ يَضَعُ الْحَدِيثَ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

Al-Dāraquṭnī then compares the other transmissions from Qatāda. In no. 610 he states:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 610" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58">

“Thus these five trustworthy transmitters narrated it from Qatāda, from Abū al-ʿĀliya, as *mursal*.”[^13]

**<Arabic>فَهَؤُلَاءِ خَمْسَةٌ ثِقَاتٌ رَوَوْهُ عَنْ قَتَادَةَ عَنْ أَبِي الْعَالِيَةِ مُرْسَلًا</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

He is referring to the versions carried by Maʿmar, Abū ʿAwāna, Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba, Saʿīd b. Bashīr and Salm b. Abī al-Dhayyāl. In that reconstruction the report passes through **Abū al-ʿĀliya without the companion connection** *that Ibn Jabala supplies*. (The Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ claimed by Ibn Jabala and the Salm b. Abī al-Dhayyāl named among the alternative witnesses are different people.)

**We have a second case in which Ibn Jabala uniquely furnishes a Companion-connected report where the better-supported version lacks that connection.** Al-Dāraquṭnī both identifies the competing *mursal* distribution and expressly labels Ibn Jabala a fabricator.

A further detail links Ibn Jabala to the right **Basran environment**. In al-Ṭabarānī’s *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*, he transmits several other sayings through **Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → Qatāda → al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī → Samura b. Jundab**. The topics vary: contracts, enclosed land, consultation and dress. The isnāds occur at nos. 6843, 6867, 6914 and 6915.[^14] Those reports show the kind of al-Ḥasan-associated Basran material that Ibn Jabala claimed to possess. This was precisely the milieu in which the Abū Bakra woman-ruler saying circulated.

## Where Did Ibn Jabala Get the Wording?

The most revealing clue is the Arabic *matn* itself. We can compare the isolated Jābir text not against a generic English paraphrase but against the precise recensions circulating within Abū Bakra’s network.

**The Jābir version through Ibn Jabala:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">

<div class="hc-report__en">

“A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper.”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>

</HadithBlock>

**The Mubārak b. Faḍāla version through al-Ḥasan and Abū Bakra:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, nos. 20478 and 20517" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/10/42">

<div class="hc-report__en">

“A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.[^15]

</div>

</HadithBlock>

**The ʿUyayna family version through Abū Bakra:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tayalisi/34">

<div class="hc-report__en">

“A people who assign their affair to a woman will never prosper.”

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>**.[^5]

</div>

</HadithBlock>

All three begin with **<Arabic>لن يفلح قوم</Arabic>** (“a people will never prosper”), but the second and first also share the **<Arabic>ملك</Arabic>** (“rule or possess”) root. Mubārak’s sentence means “a woman rules them”; Ibn Jabala’s means “a woman rules their affair.” The change is exactly the sort of small recomposition that can occur when a familiar saying is retold. Inserting **<Arabic>أمرهم</Arabic>** (“their affair”) would have been easy because that phrase already circulated in the other Abū Bakra recensions, notably **<Arabic>ولوا أمرهم</Arabic>** (“they entrusted their affair”) and **<Arabic>أسندوا أمرهم</Arabic>** (“they assigned their affair”).

This makes the **Mubārak/al-Ḥasan family** a better textual donor than ʿUyayna’s. It is not simply that the two sayings resemble one another: Mubārak’s own descendants of transmission preserve the bare maxim with both **<Arabic>لا</Arabic>** (“not”) and **<Arabic>لن</Arabic>** (“will not”); Ibn Ḥibbān 4516 and Aḥmad 20517 preserve precisely the **<Arabic>لن يفلح ... تملكهم</Arabic>** (“will never prosper … a woman rules them”) form most closely resembling the Jābir saying.[^15]

There is also an overlooked narrative witness from the same family. In *Fatḥ al-Bārī*, Ibn Ḥajar cites **ʿUmar b. Shabba through Mubārak b. Faḍāla, from al-Ḥasan**, reporting that ʿĀʾisha sent to Abū Bakra. His reply was:

<HadithBlock source="Fatḥ al-Bārī, Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī, vol. 13, commentary on the woman-ruler report (quoting ʿUmar b. Shabba)" url="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/2605/46">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“You are indeed a mother, and your right is great. But I heard the Messenger of God say: ‘A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.’”[^16]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِنَّكِ لَأُمٌّ، وَإِنَّ حَقَّكِ لَعَظِيمٌ، وَلَكِنْ سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ يَقُولُ: لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is not a newly discovered independent eyewitness account of a conversation. It's a later citation of ʿUmar b. Shabba’s report and is **crucial to the history of the Mubārak textual family**. The same branch that preserves the compact **<Arabic>تملكهم امرأة</Arabic>** (“a woman rules them”) maxim is also represented as preserving its direct use against ʿĀʾisha’s appeal for support. It therefore combines *both* of the features needed for the borrowing reconstruction: the nearest wording to Ibn Jabala’s version and the Basran Camel setting that explains the content.

**Ibn Jabala borrowed a Mubārak-type short maxim**, already circulating through al-Ḥasan’s Basran circle, and reassigned it to Jābir. The donor is identifiable at the level of the **recension and its regional transmission network**.

The false-independent chain is no collection of random names. It is especially interesting because the sequence **Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk b. Ḥarb → Jābir b. Samura** is attested in other hadiths *without* Ibn Jabala. One such report is preserved in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2919b:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ, Muslim b. al-Ḥajjāj, no. 2919b" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim/54/18">
<div class="hc-report__en">

“A group of Muslims—or believers—will certainly open the treasure of the house of Kisrā at al-Abyaḍ.”[^18]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَتَفْتَحَنَّ عِصَابَةٌ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ أَوْ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ كَنْزَ آلِ كِسْرَى الَّذِي فِي الْأَبْيَضِ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Aḥmad 20821 and al-Ṭabarānī 1975 transmit the **same Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk → Jābir** line for the *Kisrā-treasure* report. Other pupils of Simāk, including Shuʿba, preserve the treasure prediction as well. In other words, this was an actual, recognized isnād available in the corpus; **it simply transmitted a different saying**.[^19]

This makes its appearance in Ibn Jabala’s woman-ruler report far easier to explain. The *content* could be borrowed from the Basran Abū Bakra tradition, and the *authority* could be supplied by a pre-existing Jābir chain. The Persian connection is suggestive: the recognized Jābir line transmitted a prophecy involving Kisrā, while the Abū Bakra maxim had a Persian royal frame. We should not claim to know that the word “Kisrā” personally prompted Ibn Jabala’s choice. But the match shows that he did not need to invent a wholly implausible chain. He only needed to attach new content to an already plausible line.

(1) a closely matching Abū Bakra text was circulating;
(2) Ibn Jabala claimed access to al-Ḥasan-associated Basran transmissions;
(3) the substitute Jābir chain circulated independently with different content;
(4) only Ibn Jabala appears attaching the woman-ruler maxim to it; and
(5) in another report, classical critics identify him as uniquely supplying a suspect Companion connection.

The geographical and chronological pieces fit. Abū Bakra and al-Ḥasan belong to Baṣra; so do Mubārak and the tradition circle through which the saying was distributed. Abū ʿAwāna (d. 175 AH) bridges the transmission milieus of Wāsiṭ and Iraq, while Simāk (d. 123 AH) belongs to Kūfa. Abū Ḥātim specifically encountered Ibn Jabala in **Baṣra**, where the maxim would already have been available in several forms. The precise date at which Ibn Jabala first recited this forged chain cannot be established from the surviving record, but the event being explained had occurred long before his transmission activity.[^20]

## Reconstructing the Spread of the Tradition

The evidence can now be assembled into a single, historically coherent sequence.

**First**, following ʿĀʾisha’s campaign and defeat at the Battle of the Camel, Abū Bakra had a powerful motive to justify his decision not to participate. A judgment against a people commanded by a woman could be presented as a prediction made years earlier in connection with Persian succession. That framing made his neutrality appear not merely prudent but obedient to Prophetic foreknowledge. Its close fit with his own remembered conduct identifies **Abū Bakra as the likeliest originator of the Prophetic attribution**.

**Second**, the report spread among Basran transmitters. Al-Ḥasan’s students distributed different versions, from elaborate scenes about ʿĀʾisha and Persia to bare maxims. ʿUyayna’s network and Abū Bakra’s descendants supplied additional paths, all still anchored to Abū Bakra. The lines formed a substantial transmission fan with identifiable partial common links, while preserving enough variation to show that the wording and setting were still being reshaped.

**Third**, the short **<Arabic>لن يفلح قوم تملكهم امرأة</Arabic>** (“A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.”) wording circulated through Mubārak b. Faḍāla’s al-Ḥasan branch. The ʿUmar b. Shabba report quoted by Ibn Ḥajar further links Mubārak’s rendition to the specific Abū Bakra–ʿĀʾisha encounter. This was the most plausible form for a later Basran narrator to borrow: it was recognizable and needed no historical introduction to make sense as a maxim.

**Fourth**, Ibn Jabala encountered that circulating wording—or a very close version of it—reformulated **<Arabic>تملكهم</Arabic>** (“she rules them”) as **<Arabic>يملك أمرهم</Arabic>** (“rules their affair”), and attached it to a recognized **Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk → Jābir** chain. The new attribution supplied what the Abū Bakra corpus otherwise lacked: an ostensibly independent Companion confirmation. Ibn Jabala’s otherwise isolated role, his known Basran location, and al-Dāraquṭnī’s detailed criticism of his isnād construction in another report identify him as the likeliest creator of the dive.

**Finally**, the Jābir version survived through one identified later route and entered al-Ṭabarānī’s collection. Its preservation alongside the much larger Abū Bakra family gave the illusion of two Companion witnesses when the transmission evidence actually points to **one early Basran origin and one later reassignment**.

An alternative explanation would require that Jābir genuinely taught the same maxim to Simāk, who taught it to Abū ʿAwāna, and that it then vanished from all the other recovered pupils of those well-known transmitters while surviving uniquely through a man accused of fabricating and inflating reports. That possibility is not logically impossible. It is simply far less economical than the reconstruction above, which accounts simultaneously for the bottleneck, the close matn resemblance, the already available substitute isnād and Ibn Jabala’s documented practices.

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">

The hadith is regularly discussed as though its point of origin were the Persian succession to Kisrā. Its own most informative recensions tell a different story: **the statement is mobilized to defend Abū Bakra’s conduct during the Battle of the Camel**. That is the setting which best explains why a general condemnation of female leadership would have become a memorable Prophetic prediction and why the same Basran Companion anchors every substantial transmission branch.

The common-link and matn evidence therefore points to **Abū Bakra as the creator of the Prophetic attribution**, with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, ʿUyayna and other family-connected transmitters as its subsequent distributors. The report’s Sasanian framing and precise wording proved flexible, but its retrospective political usefulness remained remarkably stable.

The apparent independent witness through Jābir b. Samura does not overturn that conclusion. It first becomes visible through **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala**, a Basran transmitter explicitly condemned for fabrication. The short Jābir wording most closely resembles **Mubārak b. Faḍāla’s recension from al-Ḥasan and Abū Bakra**, while the Jābir isnād’s backbone is independently attested for a different prophecy concerning Kisrā. Al-Dāraquṭnī’s comparison of Ibn Jabala’s report about prayer with multiple *mursal* witnesses provides a particularly close parallel for the manufacture of a connected authority.

Put simply: **Abū Bakra supplied the prophecy after the event; Ibn Jabala supplied the false appearance of a second Companion who had heard it.** The traditional claim of independent corroboration collapses into a history of transmission, appropriation and isnād substitution.

</VerdictBox>
