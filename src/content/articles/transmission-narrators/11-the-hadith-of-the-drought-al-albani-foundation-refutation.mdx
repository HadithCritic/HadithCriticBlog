<ClaimBox title="Thesis">

Fatwa 73 tries to neutralize the report of Mālik al-Dār by treating the narration as defective: Mālik is allegedly unknown, Ibn Ḥajar allegedly authenticated only part of the route, the normal drought practice was ṣalāt al-istisqāʾ, and the man at the grave is unnamed. Those objections only work if the standards are tightened exactly where the report becomes theologically embarrassing. By the ordinary Sunni hadith method, the report has enough support to survive. By the Qurʾān, however, the practice described in it is a direct appeal to a dead messenger for posthumous intervention, which is precisely why the report creates a problem.

</ClaimBox>

If the Sunni hadith method is applied consistently, the Mālik al-Dār report is difficult to dismiss. If the Qurʾānic standard is applied consistently, the report is religiously unacceptable. The contradiction is produced by the hadith framework itself: it authenticates a story that later theology has to weaken.

The wording at issue is not a vague appeal to the Prophet’s status. The man goes to the Prophet’s grave and addresses him directly: “O Messenger of God, ask God for rain for your community.” The request is directed to a dead prophet after his death. The story then presents the Prophet as appearing in a dream, instructing the man to go to ʿUmar, and ʿUmar responding emotionally rather than condemning the act.

## The Report in Its Earliest Accessible Form

The report is also transmitted through al-Bayhaqī’s route as cited by Ibn Kathīr. After giving the chain through Abū Muʿāwiya, al-Aʿmash, Abū Ṣāliḥ, and Mālik, Ibn Kathīr states: “This chain is authentic” (وهذا إسناد صحيح). Ibn Ḥajar likewise cites Ibn Abī Shayba’s route in _Fatḥ al-Bārī_ with the phrase “with an authentic chain” (بإسناد صحيح). The attempt to escape those two assessments is the center of the dispute.

The fatwa’s structure reveals the pressure point. It does not merely say, “This report is weak.” It has to explain why two major later hadith authorities could speak of an authentic chain while still preserving a theology in which direct address to the dead is impermissible. The result is a set of objections that would become destructive if applied consistently across hadith literature.

## Mālik al-Dār Was Not a Blank Narrator

The first objection is that Mālik al-Dār is unknown and that only Ibn Ḥibbān treated him as reliable. This is too narrow. Ibn Ḥajar identifies him as Mālik b. ʿIyāḍ, the freedman of ʿUmar, known as Mālik al-Dār. He states that Mālik had _idrāk_, heard from Abū Bakr, narrated from Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, Muʿādh, and Abū ʿUbayda, and was narrated from by Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān and his two sons ʿAwn and ʿAbd Allāh. Ibn Ḥajar also preserves reports that he served in trusted administrative capacities under ʿUmar and then under ʿUthmān.

<ContextNote title="Biographical Weight">

Administrative service is not the same thing as a formal hadith grading. A person can be trusted with money and still be weak in transmission. The problem is that Fatwa 73 does more than deny a formal grading; it rhetorically turns Mālik into an unknown nobody. The biographical notices do not allow that. He is named, located, connected to senior Companions, attached to ʿUmar’s administration, and known to later transmitters.

</ContextNote>

Al-Khalīlī’s notice is even harder for the fatwa to absorb. He describes Mālik al-Dār as an early tābiʿī, “agreed upon” (متفق عليه), praised by the tābiʿūn, and not abundant in narration. A critic can debate how much force to give this wording, but it cannot be reduced to “only Ibn Ḥibbān mentioned him.” The statement “only Ibn Ḥibbān authenticated him” leaves out the broader evidence that made later scholars comfortable citing the report.

This matters because the fatwa’s argument depends on a strong form of ignorance. If Mālik were only a name floating in the chain with no profile, the objection would have force. Once he is a known client of ʿUmar, connected to Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, noticed by Ibn Saʿd, al-Khalīlī, Ibn Ḥajar, and others, the objection becomes more selective. It may still be possible to argue that he falls below the highest hadith standard, but it becomes much harder to explain why Ibn Ḥajar and Ibn Kathīr treated this exact route as sound.

## Ibn Ḥajar’s Statement Is Being Rewritten

The second objection is the most strained one. Ibn Ḥajar says that Ibn Abī Shayba narrated the report “with an authentic chain from the narration of Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān from Mālik al-Dār.” Fatwa 73 turns this into a claim that Ibn Ḥajar authenticated the route only up to Abū Ṣāliḥ, leaving Mālik outside the authenticated portion.

That reading asks the phrase to do too much. Ibn Ḥajar is not giving a full isnād line by line. He is summarizing the report and identifying the route by its lower link: Abū Ṣāliḥ from Mālik al-Dār. In Arabic hadith prose, “from the narration of X from Y” names the route through which the report is known. It does not automatically mean that the authentication stops before Y. If Ibn Ḥajar had wanted to flag Mālik as the unresolved problem, this was the obvious place to do so. Instead, he calls the route authentic and continues to use the report in the chapter discussion.

<HadithBlock
  label="Ibn Ḥajar's Wording"
  source="Ibn Ḥajar, Fatḥ al-Bārī, under the chapter on asking the imam for rain during drought"
  arabic={`وروى ابن أبي شيبة بإسناد صحيح من رواية أبي صالح السمان عن مالك الدار وكان خازن عمر قال: أصاب الناس قحط في زمن عمر فجاء رجل إلى قبر النبي صلى الله عليه وسلم فقال: يا رسول الله استسق لأمتك فإنهم قد هلكوا...`}
  translation={`Ibn Abī Shayba narrated with an authentic chain, from the narration of Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān, from Mālik al-Dār—who was ʿUmar’s treasurer—that the people suffered drought in the time of ʿUmar, so a man came to the grave of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, and said: “O Messenger of God, ask for rain for your community, for they have perished...”`}
/>

Even if someone insists on forcing a “partial authentication” reading onto Ibn Ḥajar, Ibn Kathīr blocks that maneuver. Ibn Kathīr cites the Bayhaqī chain and then says, without this alleged grammatical ambiguity, “This chain is authentic.” The fatwa therefore has to escape both Ibn Ḥajar’s wording and Ibn Kathīr’s wording. The escape becomes less like technical precision and more like theological damage control.

## The ʿAbbās Report Does Not Cancel the Mālik Report

The fatwa appeals to the established practice of asking God for rain through the living, especially through al-ʿAbbās, the Prophet’s uncle. That report is genuinely important, but it does not prove what the fatwa needs it to prove.

<HadithBlock
  label="Authenticated Counter-Practice"
  source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1010; also 3710"
  arabic={`أن عمر بن الخطاب رضي الله عنه كان إذا قحطوا استسقى بالعباس بن عبد المطلب فقال: اللهم إنا كنا نتوسل إليك بنبينا فتسقينا، وإنا نتوسل إليك بعم نبينا فاسقنا. قال: فيسقون.`}
  translation={`Whenever drought struck them, ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb would seek rain through al-ʿAbbās b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib and say: “O God, we used to seek a means to You through our Prophet and You would give us rain. Now we seek a means to You through the uncle of our Prophet, so give us rain.” And they would be given rain.`}
/>

This report can be used to argue that ʿUmar’s public and formal method was to ask a living righteous person to supplicate. It cannot automatically erase a separate report in which a man goes to the Prophet’s grave. Hadith collections regularly preserve parallel practices, exceptional incidents, private actions, public rituals, and reports that sit uneasily beside one another. The existence of one accepted practice does not, by itself, disprove another report. A contradiction has to be argued, not assumed.

The real issue is theological. The ʿAbbās report is safe for Sunni theology because the supplication is addressed to God and the intermediary is alive. The Mālik al-Dār report is dangerous because the man addresses the dead Prophet at the grave and asks him to act. The fatwa therefore treats the safe report as “the Sunnah” and the dangerous report as an error. That move may protect doctrine, but it is not a neutral hadith-critical argument.

## The Anonymous Man Objection Would Destroy Too Much

The fourth objection asks: who was the man who went to the grave? What was his rank? What was his status? This sounds serious until the same standard is applied elsewhere.

Hadith literature constantly contains unnamed actors: a man came to the Prophet, a Bedouin asked, a woman complained, a person saw a dream, a man recited a sūrah, and so on. Their anonymity does not automatically weaken the report because they are not the transmitters carrying the narration. The evaluation concerns the chain that reports the story. If an unnamed character inside a matn invalidated the report, a large portion of accepted hadith literature would become unstable.

<ContextNote title="The Bilāl b. al-Ḥārith Identification">

Some later discussions identify the man as Bilāl b. al-Ḥārith al-Muzanī, a Companion. That identification is not necessary for the argument and should not carry the weight of the proof, since it is tied to the problematic route through Sayf. The stronger point is simpler: the report’s validity does not depend on knowing the visitor’s name. The report is carried by Mālik al-Dār from an event in ʿUmar’s time, and the story itself says the matter reached ʿUmar.

</ContextNote>

The fatwa’s question about the man’s status is therefore a distraction. The story does not ask the anonymous visitor to function as an independent legal authority. It says he performed the act, received a dream-message, and informed ʿUmar. If the chain is accepted, the report places ʿUmar inside the episode as the recipient of the information, weeping and responding rather than condemning the act as shirk. That is precisely why the narration is inconvenient.

## The al-Aʿmash Objection Does Not Rescue the Fatwa

Some later critics add an objection not emphasized in Fatwa 73: al-Aʿmash narrates with ʿanʿana, and al-Aʿmash is known for tadlīs. This objection sounds more technical than the Mālik objection, but it still fails to explain the later authentications.

Al-Aʿmash’s route through Abū Ṣāliḥ is not an obscure or isolated pairing. The Bukhārī corpus itself contains numerous reports through al-Aʿmash from Abū Ṣāliḥ, sometimes with explicit hearing and sometimes with ʿanʿana. A critic can raise tadlīs in individual cases, but he cannot pretend that the mere presence of al-Aʿmash → Abū Ṣāliḥ is automatically disqualifying in Sunni hadith practice. Ibn Ḥajar and Ibn Kathīr knew al-Aʿmash’s profile. Their authentication of this route therefore cannot be dismissed by reciting the word “tadlīs” after the fact.

This is where the problem becomes methodological. When the content supports ordinary Sunni law or virtue literature, the system has room for narrators like al-Aʿmash and routes like Abū Ṣāliḥ. When the content depicts a man appealing to the Prophet at his grave, the same system suddenly needs extra caution, extra suspicion, and extra reinterpretation.

## The Qurʾānic Problem: The Dead Are Not a Channel of Appeal

The hadith dispute is only half the issue. The deeper problem is that the narration, if accepted, normalizes a practice the Qurʾān does not permit. The Qurʾān repeatedly denies that messengers or invoked beings possess independent access to the unseen, control over harm and benefit, or the ability to respond to the calls of the living after death.

<QuranVerse verse="5:109" />

The scene in 5:109 is devastating for later saint-veneration logic. The messengers are asked about their communities and answer by disclaiming knowledge: God alone knows the unseen. The Qurʾān does not present dead messengers as supervising the requests of their communities from the grave.

<QuranVerse verse="72:20-21" />

The Prophet is commanded to announce that he possesses no power to harm or guide. This is the Qurʾānic frame for prophetic authority: he delivers the message; he does not become a posthumous object of invocation.

<QuranVerse verse="23:99-100" />

Death places a barrier between the deceased and the world until resurrection. The Mālik al-Dār report moves in the opposite direction. It depicts a man addressing the Prophet after death and receiving a dream-response that validates the appeal.

<QuranVerse verse="35:14" />

This verse is especially direct: those called upon besides God do not hear the call, and even if they heard it, they could not respond. The verse then says they will disown that shirk on the Day of Resurrection. The report at the center of this article describes a man doing exactly what this verse warns against: calling upon someone other than God for assistance in a crisis, even if the assistance is framed as “ask God for us.”

<ClaimBox title="The Actual Contradiction">

The hadith method can preserve the report because the chain can be defended. The Qurʾānic standard rejects the practice because the request is directed to the dead. Al-Albani’s school senses the theological danger, so it tightens the hadith method at the exact point where the narration becomes damaging.

</ClaimBox>

## What This Shows About Hadith Authentication

This report is a useful case study because it exposes how theological commitments shape hadith criticism. The narration has a recognizable chain, known transmitters, classical defenders, and explicit later authentication from Ibn Kathīr and Ibn Ḥajar. The objections raised against it are not impossible, but they are applied with unusual urgency because the content threatens a later doctrinal boundary.

A Qurʾān-alone critique has no reason to defend the practice in the report. The man’s act is not made acceptable because a hadith chain can be defended. The point is that Sunni hadith methodology, when pressed on its own terms, can authenticate a story that depicts behavior the Qurʾān condemns. The later scholar then faces a choice: follow the chain and inherit the theological problem, or protect the theology by weakening the chain through selective criteria.

The second option is what Fatwa 73 does. Mālik al-Dār becomes “unknown” despite substantial biographical notices. Ibn Ḥajar’s phrase becomes “partial” despite its normal reading and despite Ibn Kathīr’s fuller statement. The ʿAbbās report becomes a cancellation mechanism instead of a separate practice. The unnamed man becomes a fatal problem despite the routine presence of anonymous characters in accepted narrations.

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">

The Mālik al-Dār drought report should not be used to justify calling upon the dead. It should be used to expose the instability of hadith authentication. By Sunni hadith standards, the report is defensible. By the Qurʾān, the act it describes is indefensible. Fatwa 73 tries to solve the contradiction by weakening the report, but its arguments depend on standards that are not applied consistently elsewhere.

</VerdictBox>
