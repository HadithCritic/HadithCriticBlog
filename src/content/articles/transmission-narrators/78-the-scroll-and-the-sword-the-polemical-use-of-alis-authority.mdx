Seyfeddin Kara has argued, across thirty-five hadith organized into five clusters, that the tradition in which ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib denies possessing any secret Prophetic knowledge beyond the Qurʾān and a document on his sword-scabbard traces back to ʿAlī himself — that ʿAlī may have compiled the earliest known legal text in Islamic history. On his reading, the five clusters are partial testimonies to a single document: different questioners heard different passages on different occasions, and their divergent contents are simply different fragments of one comprehensive original.

This piece takes Kara seriously on his own methodological terms. It does not dispute that isnad-cum-matn analysis (ICMA) is the right tool. It disputes that thirty-five variants are enough material to sustain the conclusion. Applied to the wider surviving corpus, the same method does not refine Kara's picture — it overturns the premise it rests on.

<ClaimBox>
The Ṣaḥīfat ʿAlī tradition is not one document remembered in five fragments. It is five independent fabrications, each built on a shared rhetorical mold — ʿAlī is asked if he has secret Prophetic knowledge, denies it, and then reveals a scabbard document with enumerable contents — and each filled with different legal content by a different transmitter answering a different, dateable communal crisis. Al-Shaʿbī originated the frame against Mukhtār's revolt; Qatāda, al-Aʿmash, Abū al-Ṭufayl, and Sharīk each independently reused it for their own city's needs.
</ClaimBox>

## Why Kara's Method Doesn't Get There

ICMA can date a tradition to its earliest common link (CL). What it cannot do on its own is verify the single strand running from that CL up to the named Companion above him — that strand has no fan-out to check it against. Kara identifies several independent CLs all reporting on ʿAlī's scroll and treats their multiplicity, plus their shared content, as corroboration that some such scroll existed. That inference only holds if the CL-strands are genuinely independent of each other.

Narrator substitution — a transmitter reissuing material he received through one line under a different, more prestigious or strategically useful authority, while keeping the wording essentially stable — is a documented, classically-acknowledged practice, not a modern suspicion.[^1] If even one or two of Kara's supposedly independent CLs acquired their material from a shared, unacknowledged source and re-attributed the isnād above it, his method has no way to detect this. It would register multiple independent lines converging on ʿAlī where there is really only one — or none — laundered through invented authorities. Matn corroboration across chains cannot, by itself, rule this out, and Kara's own treatment of weaker links elsewhere shows the pattern: a transmitter graded "closer to weak" is nonetheless accepted because the content matches other clusters, which quietly makes the conclusion immune to the very possibility of a false attribution.[^2]

## The Shared Architecture

Every variant in every cluster, regardless of transmission line, geography, or legal content, follows the same fourfold shape: ʿAlī is asked whether he possesses anything from the Prophet beyond the Qurʾān; he denies it; the questioner presses; ʿAlī reveals a document in his sword-scabbard and lists what is in it. Not one cluster departs from this. That invariance is the single most important fact about the corpus, and it is exactly what a single-document theory cannot explain on its own — because the five clusters diverge in every other respect that matters. Different companions are present (Abū Juḥayfa in one, Abū al-Ṭufayl in another). Different cities produce them (Kufa, Baṣra, Mecca). And the legal registers are not compatible fragments of one code — blood-money and captive-ransom law in one cluster, constitutional protection for combatants in another, anti-tax-farming fiscal law in a third. Read as one document, the Ṣaḥīfa would have to be a complete Prophetic legal corpus. Read as five independent productions, the divergence is exactly what the theory predicts.

## Cluster I — al-Shaʿbī: The Originator

Eight partial common links carry Muṭarrif's redaction forward: Sufyān b. ʿUyayna (to al-Shāfiʿī, al-Ḥumaydī, Ibn Abī Shayba, Aḥmad, al-Bazzār, al-Nasāʾī), Sufyān al-Thawrī (to ʿAbd al-Razzāq), Zuhayr b. Muʿāwiya (to al-Bukhārī and al-Bayhaqī), Hushaym b. Bashīr (to al-Tirmidhī), Abū Bakr b. ʿAyyāsh (to Ibn Mājah), Yazīd b. ʿAṭāʾ (to al-Ṭayālisī), Jarīr b. ʿAbd al-Ḥamīd (to al-Dārimī), and Asbāṭ b. Muḥammad (to al-Ṭaḥāwī).[^3] Comparing what is invariant across all eight recovers a stable ur-text: the oath formula with its collective "we," the *fahm* exception, and a fixed three-item scroll — blood-money, captive-ransom, and the homicide ruling.

Below that layer, the variation is diagnostic rather than random. The dominant wording bars killing a Muslim for a *kāfir* (unbeliever generally); a narrower sub-line — Yazīd b. ʿAṭāʾ, Jarīr, and one branch under Zuhayr — narrows this to *mushrik* (polytheist specifically).[^4] The same Zuhayr split is the clearest evidence for reading *kāfir* as original: his student Aḥmad b. Yūnus, who fed Bukhārī, kept *kāfir*; his student ʿAmr b. Marzūq, who fed al-Bayhaqī, has *mushrik* and also uniquely preserves Muṭarrif's own teaching glosses on the two technical terms — evidence that a single PCL sub-line juridically narrowed the rule while also carrying the most explicit record of Muṭarrif's classroom explanation of it.[^5]

<ContextNote title="Why Al-Shaʿbī, Not Just Muṭarrif">
Muṭarrif is the checkable common link, but he is not the originator: nothing about his own biography explains why this exact material was needed. Al-Shaʿbī does. Born c. 17–19 AH into a Hamdānī family in Kufa, he fled the city for Medina out of fear of Mukhtār al-Thaqafī's movement, later served as judicial administrator under al-Ḥajjāj, was personally requisitioned by Caliph ʿAbd al-Malik as a court narrator, and died c. 103/721 as Kufa's most respected scholar.[^6] He is independently placed inside Mukhtār's own documentary world — present when Mukhtār produced a letter attributed to Muḥammad b. al-Ḥanafiyya to win over Ibrāhīm b. al-Ashtar, and described as the one who physically handed the letter over.[^7]
</ContextNote>

Mukhtār's movement claimed that Prophetic knowledge was physical (transmitted in private documents to ʿAlī), private (passed only to ʿAlī and then the Imāms), and divinely delegated (*ʿahida ilaykum*, "entrusted specifically to you" — a formula that surfaces, tellingly, in the weakest sub-strand of this very cluster).[^8] Al-Shaʿbī's tradition answers all three claims at once. The only physical item ʿAlī possesses is a scroll of three bureaucratic rulings — not cosmology, not esoterica. What he has beyond the Qurʾān is *fahm*, an understanding God may grant any man, not a household monopoly. And if the household possesses nothing beyond ordinary jurisprudential insight, no theological basis for exclusive political authority survives.

The three rulings are not a random legal sample. They answer the three zones of disorder Mukhtār's revolt actually produced: *al-ʿaql* converts the *thaʾr* (vengeance) ideology driving Mukhtār's movement into adjudicable, court-administered liability;[^9] *fakāk al-asīr* answers a live captive-execution controversy on both sides of the civil war, including one report of al-Ḥajjāj ordering a captive killed and Ibn ʿUmar refusing by citing the Qurʾān's own "favor or ransom" clause;[^10] and *lā yuqtalu muslimun bi-kāfir* answers a real Iraqi *qawād* dispute over executing a Muslim for killing a protected non-Muslim, amid al-Ḥajjāj's unstable dhimmī and mawālī fiscal policy.[^11] A single Umayyad-aligned scholar asserting outright that ʿAlī had no secret knowledge would have been dismissed as propaganda. Putting the denial in ʿAlī's own mouth, through a minor Kufan companion resident in exactly the milieu al-Shaʿbī administered, is what let it circulate.

Every isnād in this cluster converges on Qatāda ibn Diʿāma al-Sadūsī (d. 117/735), a blind Baṣran scholar of the generation after the Companions, independently documented as a *mudallis* and Qadarī in his theology.[^12] Below him the tradition splits into two structurally distinct pathways: one through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī to Qays b. ʿUbād, carried almost entirely by Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba; the other bypassing al-Ḥasan entirely, reaching ʿAlī directly through Abū Ḥassān al-Aʿraj.

The two pathways' cleanest forms — ʿUmar b. ʿĀmir's and Ḥajjāj b. Minhāl's versions of Ḥammām's own transmission — carry only the minimalist constitutional triad, with no Medina material at all.[^13] But a version of Ḥammām's own line, carried by Bahz b. Asad into Aḥmad's *Musnad* and by Hudba b. Khālid into al-Bayhaqī's *Maʿrifat al-Sunan*, adds a full Medinan sanctuary passage — the two lava-plain boundary markers, an Abrahamic invocation, a tree-cutting prohibition, and a "reluctance before disclosure" narrative motif.[^14] Rijāl assessment of Ḥammām ibn Yaḥyā (d. 164/780) explains why: Abū Ḥātim called him "trustworthy, truthful, but there is something in his memory," al-Dhahabī records his own admission "we used to err," and Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Qaṭṭān specifically criticized disruptions in his transmissions from Qatāda.[^15] The most economical reading is that Ḥammām merged a separate, genuinely Medinan sanctuary tradition — one that reached Baṣra via al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's own transmission network, most plausibly formulated in the aftermath of the Battle of al-Ḥarra (63 AH) — into his recitation of Qatāda's scroll.

A further sub-tradition, reaching al-Dāraquṭnī's *Sunan* through Ḥajjāj (identified by al-Dāraquṭnī himself as Ḥajjāj b. Arṭāt, d. 145/762), carries the same Medina-Haram material but suppresses Ḥammām's name entirely, restructures Abū Ḥassān into "Muslim al-Ajrad," and inserts Mālik al-Ashtar as questioner in his place.[^16] Ibn al-Mubārak independently accuses Ḥajjāj b. Arṭāt of exactly this kind of *tadlīs* elsewhere — falsely attributing a hadith directly to a transmitter's own teachers, skipping the actual intermediate source.[^17] Al-Dāraquṭnī's own verdict, weighing the evidence himself, prefers Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba's uncontaminated version.[^18]

<ContextNote title="The Azāriqa Crisis">
Qatāda's childhood and adolescence coincided with the most violent phase of the Azāriqa insurgency in Baṣra, 64–78 AH — a movement that, unlike Khārijism broadly, endorsed *istiʿrāḍ*: indiscriminate killing of anyone judged an unbeliever, including polytheist children, and the reclassification of ordinary Basran Muslims as legitimate targets.[^19] Abū Bilāl Mirdās, by contrast, explicitly rejected *istiʿrāḍ* and fought only those who fought him — Khalīfa ibn Khayyāṭ records that his own uprising "killed no one, and did not molest the road or property."[^20] Qatāda's own tribe, Sadūs, produced the poet ʿImrān b. Ḥiṭṭān, who elegized Abū Bilāl — placing Qatāda's constitutional cluster inside a documented intra-tribal argument over how far Khārijite sympathy should extend.[^21]
</ContextNote>

Each clause of the scroll answers a specific Azraqī claim.[^22] "Believers are equal in blood" blocks the Azraqī move of reclassifying mainstream Basran Muslims as killable. "One hand against outsiders" inverts the Azraqī camp/*dār kufr* model that treated the Muslim community itself as split into believers and enemies. "The lowest of them may extend protection" — later applied by Abū Yūsuf even to a slave's grant of *amān* — directly answers *istiʿrāḍ*'s refusal to honor protection extended by anyone but its own leadership. And the treaty clause answers Najda ibn ʿĀmir's own accusation that Nāfiʿ ibn al-Azraq refused to honor trusts.[^23]

This cluster's ur-text, recovered by comparing every substantive PCL, contains three elements found nowhere else in the corpus: camel-age and wound-compensation schedules, a Medina sanctuary declaration bounded "between ʿAyr and Thawr," and a prohibition on falsely claiming or reassigning *walāʾ* (clientage) ties.[^24] It conspicuously lacks the *fahm* exception, the blood-equality triad, and the killing-prohibition formula — this is not a variant of al-Shaʿbī's or Qatāda's material, but an independently constructed cluster sharing only the frame.

<ContextNote title="A Deliberately Wrong Boundary">
Thawr is a mountain in Mecca. It has no place in Medina's own geography, whose conventional southern and eastern boundaries are marked by two lava plains (al-lābatān) and, separately, ʿAyr — never Thawr.[^25] Al-Aʿmash, a careful and well-connected Kufan scholar, is argued to have known the correct toponymy and substituted the Meccan landmark deliberately.[^26] The effect is to redirect a curse that genuinely belongs to the Umayyad sack of Medina during the Battle of al-Ḥarra (63/683) — three days of killing and plunder under Muslim b. ʿUqba, ordered by Yazīd I — toward al-Ḥajjāj's own siege and bombardment of Mecca in 692 CE, ensuring the Prophetic curse lands on al-Ḥajjāj by name rather than on the Umayyad house that actually sacked Medina.
</ContextNote>

Ibrāhīm al-Taymī, al-Aʿmash's own named source, is independently reported to have been imprisoned by al-Ḥajjāj for refusing to betray Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, and to have died there.[^27] The dhimma and *walāʾ* clauses read as a direct broadside against al-Ḥajjāj's mawālī policy in the wake of the Ibn al-Ashʿath revolt (83/701): converts expelled from garrison cities back to their villages of origin, jizya collected from them despite their conversion, mawālī barred from leading prayer or serving as judges.[^28] The dhimma clause specifically protects a subordinate's grant of *amān* from being overridden — precisely what al-Ḥajjāj is recorded doing at al-Zāwiya, where men who surrendered under a guarantee of safety were killed once they laid down arms.[^29] The *walāʾ* prohibition is unique to this cluster and targets the administrative manipulation of clientage ties among Kufa's large mawālī population for fiscal purposes.[^30]

This is the one cluster in the entire corpus where two PCLs genuinely diverge in a way that looks like independent memory rather than shared fabrication: different question-wording (*khaṣṣakum*, open, versus *yusirru ilayka*, presupposing a confidence), a physical document in one strand and pure oral recitation in the other, and a consistent lexical split (*saraqa*, "stole," in al-Qāsim's line; *ghayyara*, "altered," in Manṣūr's).[^31] Both nonetheless converge on the same four cursed acts and the same fourfold frame — the divergence is at the PCL level, the frame itself is still shared with every other cluster.

<ContextNote title="A Life Lived Against the Umayyads">
Abū al-Ṭufayl (b. 3/625, d. 100–110/718–728) fought at Ṣiffīn on ʿAlī's side and is described by al-Mizzī as "among ʿAlī's shīʿa."[^32] Al-Balādhurī records him confronting Muʿāwiya to his face over ʿUthmān's killing, retorting, "What prevented you from helping him, when you had the people of Syria with you?"[^33] Al-Ḥajjāj later imprisoned him; he was released only through the intercession of al-Ḥajjāj's own son-in-law, and his son Muṭarrif was killed fighting alongside Ibn al-Ashʿath in the anti-Ḥajjāj revolt of 83/701 — Abū al-Ṭufayl composed a lament for him and died not long after.[^34]
</ContextNote>

Each curse targets something concrete in his lifetime. The sacrifice curse — rendered *ahalla* (the ritual utterance) rather than simply *dhabaḥa* (slaughtered) in one strand — targets the Zoroastrian *zamzama*, the mandatory priestly muttering before slaughter, preserved administratively because the Sawād's dīwān remained staffed by Persian-speaking dahāqīn until Arabicization around 80–83 AH.[^35] The parental-cursing clause activates against Muʿāwiya's institutionalized cursing of ʿAlī from the pulpit — a practice Abū al-Ṭufayl's own teacher, Zayd b. Arqam, is independently recorded publicly rebuking an Umayyad commander for continuing even after ʿAlī's death.[^36] The boundary-markers curse tracks al-Ḥajjāj's canal-building and land seizures in the Sawād, which al-Balādhurī records collapsing the province's kharāj revenue from 100 million dirhams under ʿUmar to 40 million under al-Ḥajjāj as land was alienated into Marwānid *iqṭāʿ*.[^37] And the *muḥdith*-shelter curse fits ʿUbaydallāh b. Ziyād's reception by the Marwānid court after fleeing Baṣra — al-Balādhurī records the Umayyads sheltering him and dispatching him safely to Syria, after which he commanded Marwānid forces against Mukhtār's own army at the Battle of Khāzir.[^38]

Sharīk b. ʿAbdullāh al-Nakhaʿī (d. 177/793), al-Aʿmash's own student and his successor as Kufa's leading juristic authority, later served as Chief Judge of Kufa under the Abbasid caliph al-Mahdī.[^39] Every isnād in this cluster is Kufan end to end — no Medinan, Basran, or Syrian link appears anywhere in it. Below Sharīk, seven partial common links carry the report into Aḥmad's *Musnad*, Ibn Abī Shayba's *Muṣannaf*, and al-Jaṣṣāṣ's Ḥanafī legal literature.[^40]

The content nests three descriptions of the same fiscal material — *farāʾiḍ al-ṣadaqa* ("the obligations of charity"), *farāʾiḍ al-ibil* ("the obligations of camels"), and *asnān al-ibil* ("camel age-brackets") — plausibly master code, chapter, and schedule of the same document.[^41] Two live Kufan-versus-Medinan legal disputes turn on exactly this schedule. For herds above 120 camels, Medina's rule alternates tax brackets by exact herd size; Kufa's simpler flat bracket can yield fewer animals owed depending on herd size, a real fiscal variable for Iraq's wealthy tribal herders.[^42] For the standard hundred-camel indemnity in accidental killing, Kufa's schedule of five equal age-groups (including male camels) is cheaper than Medina's schedule, which excludes males and demands proportionally older females.[^43] Al-Jaṣṣāṣ cites Sharīk's report explicitly as Ḥanafī evidence resolving both disputes in Kufa's favor.[^44] Notably, the same camel-tax mathematics recurs in early Kufan Imāmī Shīʿī sources transmitted via Imām al-Ṣādiq[^45] — Kufan Ḥanafīs and Kufan Shīʿīs, theologically opposed, sharing identical local tax arithmetic, which points to a regional administrative convention rather than a sectarian invention.

Sharīk was not a passive link in a chain. He was the sitting judge whose own court needed exactly this ruling, constructing an isnād concentrated entirely on *farāʾiḍ al-ṣadaqa* in apparent response to earlier Medinan constructions (Nāfiʿ, Thumāma, and especially al-Zuhrī, whose version elevates the sword-scroll to the Prophet himself and routes it through Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, bypassing ʿAlī altogether) that had already put competing tax law into circulation under the same documentary form.[^46]

## What the Five Clusters Are, Together

None of this requires believing that every transmitter below each cluster's originator was dishonest. Muṭarrif, Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba, and the many PCLs feeding the canonical collections were, in the ordinary case, faithfully passing on what they had received. The fabrication — where it occurred — sits at the cluster's point of origin, not throughout its downstream transmission.

What the five clusters share is not a document. It is a documentary *form*: early Islamic legal culture repeatedly located public-law rulings inside physical objects — swords, scabbards, sheets, treaties — because a ruling housed inside an ancient, tangible, Prophetic artifact carries authority a bare legal opinion does not. Al-Shaʿbī needed that authority to answer Kaysānī theology. Qatāda needed it to answer Azraqī doctrine. Al-Aʿmash needed it to indict al-Ḥajjāj without naming him directly. Abū al-Ṭufayl needed it to curse the Umayyads by name across two generations of grievance. Sharīk needed it to settle a live tax dispute from the bench. Each reused a mold that was already available and already carried ʿAlī's name — not because a real scroll existed to remember, but because the mold itself had become a portable form of legal authority any community could fill with what it needed.

<VerdictBox>
The Ṣaḥīfat ʿAlī tradition is not one Alid legal archive partially remembered by five witnesses. It is five separate common-link fabrications — al-Shaʿbī, Qatāda, al-Aʿmash, Abū al-Ṭufayl, and Sharīk — each dateable to a specific city and a specific crisis between the 60s and 170s AH, each filling a shared rhetorical frame with the legal content its own community needed. The frame's invariance across clusters that share no transmitter, no companion, and no legal register is not evidence of one remembered document. It is evidence of one reusable polemical instrument, deployed competitively and repeatedly for a century, in ʿAlī's name but never, on the evidence recoverable here, in his hand.
</VerdictBox>
