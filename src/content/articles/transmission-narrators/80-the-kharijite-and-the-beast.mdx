There is an old hadith that says a man who has sex with an animal should be killed, and the animal should be killed with him. It sounds like a single simple ruling. It is not. Once you pull together every place this saying shows up, it turns into three different versions that do not agree with each other, plus a fourth report where Ibn Abbas himself says there is no fixed punishment for this at all. This piece walks through where each version came from, who is responsible for the differences, and why the harsh version most likely was not around before three specific men started passing it on.

<ClaimBox>
Every raised version of this hadith passes through one man, Ikrima, the freed slave of Ibn Abbas. Below Ikrima the story splits into three branches that each look different, and two of those three branches are separately said by classical critics to be reliable everywhere except when they are quoting Ikrima. A fourth, completely separate report has Ibn Abbas himself denying any fixed punishment exists. The most likely explanation is that a severe ruling started circulating through Ikrima and his students, in a legal argument that was still very much open, rather than the ruling going back cleanly to the Prophet.
</ClaimBox>

## Four different things people call the same hadith

People often talk about "the beast hadith" as if it is one saying. It is really four separate things that got grouped together because they touch the same topic.

Only the first three come from Ikrima. The fourth is a rival claim about what Ibn Abbas actually believed, and it does not share a single transmitter with the other three. That last point matters a lot, because it means two different people, at two different points, both said "this is what Ibn Abbas told me," and they said the opposite thing.

## The plain version, from Amr b. Abi Amr

<HadithBlock
  label="Amr b. Abi Amr, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas"
  arabic="من وجدتموه يقع على بهيمة فاقتلوه واقتلوا البهيمة معه. فقيل لابن عباس ما شأن البهيمة فقال ما سمعت من رسول الله في ذلك شيئا ولكن أرى رسول الله كره أن يؤكل من لحمها أو ينتفع بها بعد ذلك العمل"
  translation="Whoever you find having sex with an animal, kill him and kill the animal with him. Ibn Abbas was then asked what the animal had done wrong. He said he had not heard anything specific about that from the Prophet, but he thought the Prophet disliked the idea of eating the animal's meat or getting any use from it after what had happened to it."
  source="al-Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud, al-Nasai, Ahmad, and al-Bayhaqi, all through Amr b. Abi Amr, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas"
/>

This is the version everyone reaches for when they want a short, clean proof text for the death penalty. Notice the second half of the report though. Someone is already uncomfortable with the idea of killing an animal that did not choose anything, so an explanation gets tacked on. That explanation changes from version to version. In one place it is about the meat not being eaten. In another place, preserved by al-Bayhaqi, the reasoning is different again, that the animal is killed so people stop pointing at it and saying this is the one it happened to. Two different reasons for the same rule is a sign that the rule came first and the justification was added afterward by different people trying to make sense of it.

Amr b. Abi Amr was a Medinan, and he was generally seen as a decent transmitter. Malik used him. Bukhari and Muslim both relied on him elsewhere. But when it comes to this specific material, the material he says he got from Ikrima, the judgment is much narrower. Al-Bukhari's own verdict on him was that he was honest, but that what he passed on from Ikrima specifically contained anomalies.[^1] Abu Dawud said the same report was simply not strong.[^2] So the criticism is not aimed at Amr as a person. It is aimed at exactly the one thing this whole piece is about, his material from Ikrima.

## The combined version, from Dawud b. al-Husayn

<HadithBlock
  label="Dawud b. al-Husayn, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas"
  arabic="من وقع على ذات محرم فاقتلوه ومن وقع على بهيمة فاقتلوه واقتلوا البهيمة"
  translation="Whoever has sex with a woman who is forbidden to him by close family ties should be killed. Whoever has sex with an animal should be killed, and the animal should be killed too."
  source="Ibn Majah, Ahmad, and Abd al-Razzaq's Musannaf, through Dawud b. al-Husayn, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas"
/>

Here the beast case is no longer standing alone. It gets folded together with a completely different offense, sex with a close relative, and in some versions a third offense as well, the act of the people of Lot. One expanded form of this same branch even adds two unrelated insults as separate crimes, calling a man effeminate or calling him a Jew, each earning forty lashes.[^3] That is not one ruling growing naturally. That looks like a small legal code being built up over time out of separate pieces, all riding on Ikrima's name.

Dawud b. al-Husayn also gives us the clearest single piece of evidence in this entire investigation. Classical sources describe him this way.

<ContextNote title="A transmitter reliable everywhere except one place">
Dawud b. al-Husayn is described in the biographical literature as trustworthy, except regarding Ikrima, and he is also said to have held Kharijite views.[^4] Separately, Abu Dawud said his hadiths from his other teachers were sound, but his hadiths from Ikrima specifically were anomalies.[^5] A third source records a critic being challenged over why he transmitted from Dawud b. al-Husayn at all, given that Dawud was accused of Qadari views.[^6]
</ContextNote>

Put plainly, this is the exact same shape of criticism found on Amr b. Abi Amr, and it comes from a completely separate source. Two different men, both otherwise treated as reliable, both get the identical warning label attached to them, and the warning label always points at the same name, Ikrima. When a pattern like that shows up twice independently, it stops looking like two unrelated coincidences.

## The borrowed version, from Abbad b. Mansur

<HadithBlock
  label="Abbad b. Mansur, from Ikrima, from Ibn Abbas"
  arabic="اقتلوا الفاعل والمفعول به"
  translation="Kill the active and passive participant, said regarding a man who has sex with an animal."
  source="Ibn Abi Shayba's Musannaf (recorded as a saying of Ibn Abbas rather than the Prophet), and al-Hakim's Mustadrak (recorded as going back to the Prophet)"
/>

This phrase, active and passive participant, belongs to a different crime altogether, the act of the people of Lot, where two people are involved. Applied to an animal it barely makes sense, since an animal cannot be a legal participant in anything. The most natural explanation is that this wording was borrowed from the Lot tradition and pasted onto the beast case by mistake or by habit, since the two topics already traveled together in Dawud's branch.

Abbad b. Mansur was Basran, and he was a weaker transmitter than the other two. He is separately accused of holding Qadari views.[^7] His own material even disagrees with itself about whether this is something the Prophet said or just something Ibn Abbas said on his own authority, with one collection recording it one way and another collection recording it the other way. That kind of instability is exactly what you would expect from the weakest of the three branches.

## The denial, a completely different Ibn Abbas

<HadithBlock
  label="Asim, from Abu Razin, from Ibn Abbas"
  arabic="من أتى بهيمة فلا حد عليه"
  translation="Whoever has sex with an animal, there is no fixed punishment for him."
  source="Abd al-Razzaq's Musannaf, Ibn Abi Shayba's Musannaf, al-Hakim's Mustadrak, and cited by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi as weighing against the Amr b. Abi Amr report"
/>

This line goes back to Ibn Abbas a completely different way, through two Kufans, Asim and Abu Razin, and it shares no transmitter at all with Ikrima's branch. Abu Dawud says plainly that this report weakens the earlier report from Amr b. Abi Amr.[^8] Al-Tirmidhi goes further and says this report is more reliable than the first one.[^9]

This was not some fringe opinion either. It matched what most of the early jurists actually held.

Every major early school eventually landed on discretionary punishment, not execution. That is not what you would expect if a clear command from the Prophet to kill the man and the animal had really been circulating from the very beginning.

## Why the harsh version does not look early

Two things point the same direction. First, the harsh Ikrima version never shows up in the earliest Medinan legal writing. Malik's Muwatta is the obvious place to look, since two of the three men who carried this tradition, Amr and Dawud, were both Medinan themselves. It is not there. Every mention of the word for animal in the Muwatta is about something else entirely, an unrelated hadith about newborn babies and a Quran verse about livestock. If a Prophetic order to execute someone for this crime had genuinely been circulating among Medinan scholars in that generation, this is exactly the kind of ruling Malik's book was built to collect, and it collected nothing.

Second, the harsh version is missing from the oldest Kufan legal material too, the material that later became the base of the Hanafi school. What that material does contain is the opposite ruling, the no punishment position, traced back to Umar through Ibrahim al-Nakhai himself, preserved even inside Abu Hanifa's own collection of reports. So the city that had every reason to use a severe execution ruling if it existed simply used the mild one instead.

<ContextNote title="What the silence tells us">
A saying missing from the exact books that should have recorded it, if it had really been in circulation, is not proof by itself that the saying is false. But when that silence shows up in two separate cities, and lines up with independent criticism of the very transmitters who supposedly carried the saying between those cities, the silence stops being a small detail and starts being real evidence.
</ContextNote>

## Who was probably responsible

Nobody needs to accuse Ikrima's students of lying. The far simpler explanation is that a severe ruling was already circulating under Ikrima's name by the time these three men were teaching, and each of them handled that inherited material differently. Amr kept it short and added an explanation for the part that troubled him. Dawud folded it into a bigger legal package alongside other serious sex crimes. Abbad reused language from a nearby offense because the two topics were already sitting next to each other in circulation.

What makes Ikrima himself the most likely point of origin, rather than any one of his three students, is the pattern repeating across all of them. Ikrima's own reputation already carried an association with Kharijite sympathies. Dawud b. al-Husayn independently carries both a Kharijite accusation and a Qadari accusation, and that Abbad b. Mansur independently carries a Qadari accusation, and that two of the three students are separately described in the exact same words, reliable in general but specifically unreliable from Ikrima. Three different names, three different cities, and the same warning sign keeps appearing in the same place. That is not the kind of coincidence that happens by accident.

## Putting it together

<VerdictBox>
The severe version of this ruling cannot be traced further back than Ikrima with any confidence. Below him it splits into three inconsistent forms, two of which are separately flagged by name as reliable everywhere except from Ikrima. A rival, unconnected report has Ibn Abbas denying any fixed punishment exists, and this denial matches what most early jurists actually taught. The harsh version is missing from the earliest legal writing in both Medina and Kufa, exactly the two cities where it should have shown up if it were genuinely old. The most reasonable conclusion is that a severe capital ruling entered circulation through Ikrima and his circle during a period when the actual legal question was still open, and that it competed with, but never replaced, an earlier and milder position.
</VerdictBox>
