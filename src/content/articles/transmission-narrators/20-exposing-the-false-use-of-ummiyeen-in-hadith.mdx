The word **ummiyyūn** is often translated as “illiterates,” and later hadith literature uses that meaning to build religious claims about the Prophet’s community. This article examines two hadith clusters where the term is made to mean a people who do not write, calculate, or read books. The first appears in the fasting material: “We are an ummī nation; we neither write nor calculate.” The second appears in the seven-aḥruf material: “I was sent to an ummī nation” made up of old men, women, children, and people who never read a book.

The argument here is narrower than a general discussion of whether the Prophet could read or write. The Qurʾān uses **ummiyyūn** in contrast with scriptural communities, especially the People of the Book. These hadiths move the term into a different register: social illiteracy. That shift matters because it creates doctrines that the Qurʾān itself does not state.

<ClaimBox title="Central Claim">
The fasting and seven-aḥruf reports do not simply translate a Qurʾānic word. They reframe it. In the Qurʾān, **ummiyyūn** functions primarily as a scriptural identity category, meaning those outside a prior scripture-bearing community or those without real knowledge of the Book. In these hadith clusters, the word is turned into a claim about the community’s inability to write, calculate, or read. The transmission maps show that this interpretive shift travels through identifiable narrow corridors.
</ClaimBox>

## The Qurʾānic baseline: ummiyyūn against the People of the Book

Before reading the hadiths, the Qurʾānic usage should be placed on the table. In 3:20, the Prophet is told to address “those given the Book” and “the ummiyyūn.” That pairing makes best sense as a contrast between scriptural communities and those outside them.

<QuranVerse verse="3:20" />

The same contrast appears more explicitly in 3:75, where some among the People of the Book are described as saying they have no liability toward the **ummiyyūn**. Several translators render the term here as “Gentiles,” because the sentence is about how a scriptural community speaks about outsiders.

<QuranVerse verse="3:75" />

The mission verse in Sūrat al-Jumuʿah also says the messenger was raised among the **ummiyyīn** and then immediately explains his work as reciting God’s verses, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom. The issue is scriptural instruction, not a census claim that the whole population could not decode letters.

<QuranVerse verse="62:2" />

<ContextNote title="A necessary qualification">
Qurʾān 2:78 uses **ummiyyūn** for people who do not truly know the Book except through wishful claims. That verse can be read as “unlettered” in the sense of unlearned in scripture, but it still does not require the meaning “biologically or socially unable to read and write.” It is about relation to the Book. The later hadiths examined below move beyond that and attach the word to writing, calculation, and the need for multiple recitation modes.
</ContextNote>

The important point is that this is not a weak report by later Sunni standards. It is canonically strong in the traditional system because it appears in Bukhārī and Muslim. The critique therefore has to be more precise. The report gives a legal reason for lunar practice: the community allegedly does not write or calculate. The word **ummiyyah** is no longer merely a Qurʾānic contrast with scripture-bearing peoples. It is now glossed by the report itself as **lā naktub wa-lā naḥsub**: we do not write and we do not calculate.

Classical commentators understood the phrase in exactly the way the hadith pushes them to understand it. Ibn Ḥajar’s discussion in *Fatḥ al-Bārī* treats “ummī” here as non-writing and non-calculation, including explanations that connect it to remaining upon the original state in which people were born. That confirms the point: this hadith became a proof-text for a social meaning of **ummī**, not merely a lexical translation.

<ContextNote title="The issue is not astronomy alone">
This report is often cited in debates over lunar calculation. The deeper linguistic issue is that a Qurʾānic identity term is made to carry a legal anthropology: the Prophet’s community supposedly does not write or calculate, so religious timekeeping should avoid calculation. Whether a jurist accepts or rejects astronomical calculation is a separate question. The hadith’s definition of **ummī** is the problem under review here.
</ContextNote>

## Cluster Two: “I was sent to an ummī nation” and the seven aḥruf

The second cluster makes the semantic shift even more visible. Here the Prophet is made to say that he was sent to an **ummī** nation consisting of old men, old women, boys, girls, and people who have never read a book. Gabriel then answers that the Qurʾān was revealed in seven aḥruf. The logic depends on **ummī** meaning “unable to read,” because the report uses that alleged illiteracy to explain recitational accommodation.

The seven-aḥruf report has a more obvious motive than the fasting report because the explanation is built into the matn. The Prophet says he was sent to an **ummī** nation, and the examples immediately define that as people who would struggle with a written or fixed recitational form: elderly people, children, and someone who has never read a book. Gabriel’s answer then supplies seven aḥruf as a solution.

<ContextNote title="The semantic collapse">
If **ummiyyūn** means “Gentiles” or “non-scriptural people,” the list that follows becomes awkward. Being outside a previous scripture-bearing community does not make an elderly man, a woman, a child, or a man who never read a book require seven aḥruf. The report only works smoothly if **ummī** is already being read as illiterate or untrained in reading. That is why this hadith is valuable evidence for the later interpretive shift.
</ContextNote>

## ʿĀṣim is important, but the argument should stay disciplined

The draft version of this article blamed ʿĀṣim b. Abī al-Najūd directly for fabricating the seven-aḥruf report. That is possible as a hypothesis, but it is stronger to state what the evidence can support. ʿĀṣim is a major Kūfan reciter and a central lower transmitter in these cited forms. He is praised in the rijāl literature, but he is also criticized for mistakes in ḥadīth. Ibn Saʿd reportedly called him trustworthy while saying he made many errors in his hadith, and other critics commented on his memory.

That means the report’s seven-aḥruf explanation should be treated with caution. A reciter-linked Kūfan channel is not neutral evidence for why the Qurʾān allegedly came in seven aḥruf. It is exactly the type of channel in which one would expect later recitational theology to be explained, defended, and projected backward.

## Epigraphy also weakens the “illiterate Arabia” assumption

The hadith picture often depends on an older civilizational myth: pre-Islamic Arabia as overwhelmingly oral and textless. Modern Arabian epigraphy has complicated that picture. Ahmad al-Jallad’s work on Safaitic and other Ancient North Arabian inscriptions has emphasized the scale and variety of writing in pre-Islamic Arabia. The existence of tens of thousands of inscriptions does not prove that every Arab was literate, and it does not by itself prove the Prophet’s literacy. It does, however, make the blanket phrase “we do not write” historically suspicious when treated as a description of the whole community.

<ContextNote title="What the inscriptions prove and what they do not prove">
Epigraphy does not turn every nomad into a scribe. It shows that writing was present, diverse, and culturally meaningful in parts of Arabia before Islam. That is enough to challenge a later religious slogan that makes non-writing part of the community’s identity.
</ContextNote>

The pattern is clear. The Qurʾān uses **ummiyyūn** inside a scriptural map. The hadith clusters use the term to produce later legal and recitational doctrines. The reports are therefore not harmless translations. They are interpretive engines.

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">
The two hadith clusters should be treated as later interpretive uses of **ummiyyūn**, not as the Qurʾān’s own definition of the term. The fasting report turns the word into non-writing and non-calculation. The seven-aḥruf report turns it into lack of reading ability and then uses that meaning to justify recitational plurality. Both moves are traceable through narrow transmitter corridors, and both sit uneasily beside the Qurʾān’s scriptural contrast between the People of the Book and the ummiyyūn.
</VerdictBox>
