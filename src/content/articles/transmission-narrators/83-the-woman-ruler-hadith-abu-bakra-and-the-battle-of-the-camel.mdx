In 36 AH (656 CE), ʿĀʾisha, accompanied by Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, led an armed coalition into Baṣra. The coalition fought the forces of ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib, and the encounter became known as the Battle of the Camel. ʿĀʾisha's side lost. One Basran Companion, Abū Bakra Nufayʿ b. al-Ḥārith, is remembered for refusing to join her, and his refusal was later explained in a hadith.

<HadithBlock translation={"God benefited me during the days of the Camel through a saying that I had heard from the Messenger of God, after I had almost joined the people of the Camel and fought alongside them … 'A people who entrust their affair to a woman will never prosper.'"} source={"Ṣaḥīḥ, no. 4425"}  arabic={"لَقَدْ نَفَعَنِي اللَّهُ بِكَلِمَةٍ سَمِعْتُهَا مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ أَيَّامَ الْجَمَلِ بَعْدَ مَا كِدْتُ أَنْ أَلْحَقَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْجَمَلِ فَأُقَاتِلَ مَعَهُمْ قَالَ لَمَّا بَلَغَ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ أَنَّ أَهْلَ فَارِسَ قَدْ مَلَّكُوا عَلَيْهِمْ بِنْتَ كِسْرَى قَالَ لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةً"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/64/83"} />

Prophet Muḥammad allegedly learned the Persians had made Kisrā's daughter their ruler, and that he then pronounced the hadith in which entrusting the affair of leadership to women, would cause them to never prosper. Abū Bakra remembered it decades later, when ʿĀʾisha's movement forced him to choose a side between hers and Ali’s.[^1] Another major recension is more explicit.[^2]

<HadithBlock translation={"When ʿĀʾisha arrived, that is in Baṣra, I remembered the statement of the Messenger of God, and God protected me through it."} source={"al-Jāmiʿ, al-Tirmidhī, no. 2262"}  arabic={"عَصَمَنِي اللَّهُ بِشَيْءٍ سَمِعْتُهُ مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ لَمَّا هَلَكَ كِسْرَى قَالَ مَنِ اسْتَخْلَفُوا قَالُوا ابْنَتَهُ فَقَالَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةً قَالَ فَلَمَّا قَدِمَتْ عَائِشَةُ يَعْنِي الْبَصْرَةَ ذَكَرْتُ قَوْلَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ فَعَصَمَنِي اللَّهُ بِهِ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tirmidhi/33/75"} />

The narrative already interprets itself. The Persian episode is not recalled for its own sake as Sasanian history. It frames a judgment about a much later Iraqi conflict. The warning appears to have been made for the moment its narrator needed it, when a woman led a coalition and Abū Bakra wanted his abstention to look divinely approved. The saying turned a contested decision into a verdict that any listener in Baṣra could apply. The argument is that the hadith was made to work as political persuasion, and that its target was the Basran public deciding whether to follow ʿĀʾisha or Ali.

<ClaimBox title="Central thesis">The woman-ruler report is interpreted here as a Basran intervention in the Battle of the Camel, with Abū Bakra as the proposed earliest source and the isolated Jābir report evaluated as an isnād-diving attribution.</ClaimBox>

## The Battle of the Camel and the Politics of Persuasion



The political context explains the tradition better than a general dispute about gender does. Baṣra was divided during the First Fitna. Some supported ʿĀʾisha's coalition, some supported ʿAlī, and others wanted to stay out of a Muslim civil war. In the sources that construct Abū Bakra's public image, he belongs to the last group. Elizabeth Urban's *Conquered Populations in Early Islam* devotes a full chapter to Abū Bakra. She identifies the Battle of the Camel as one of the main events through which later Muslims remembered him. Urban presents him as a figure who repeatedly argued for withdrawal and reconciliation She also traces how stories about his origins, his manumission, his relationship with his half-brother Ziyād b. Abīhi, and his legal controversies were shaped by the needs of historical and hadith literature.[^3]

I argue this woman-ruler hadith was utilized as Basran anti-ʿĀʾisha  propaganda. Its purpose was to convince ordinary people in Baṣra that supporting ʿĀʾisha was a mistake, and that the Prophet had already said so implicitly. Abū Bakra's own refusal to fight provided the example. The hadith went beyond justifying one man's abstention. It told the public that a woman's command was illegitimate in advance. The Persian succession supplied a setting that was remote enough to avoid direct accusation and familiar enough to be repeated in any gathering without naming a contemporary leader. A story told this way is built to persuade, since the outcome has already been foretold to the listener. The useful question is what the words did. The hadith made a specific political choice look like a religious duty, and that effect explains why a Basran audience would have found it worth repeating.

## Why the Persian Frame Mattered


The choice of Persia was deliberate. Sasanian royal succession had real precedents for female rule, which gave the saying historical texture that a flat condemnation would have lacked. The events were distant enough to be safe and familiar enough to be believed. The frame also allowed a useful ambiguity. The Persian ruler could stand for another polity, so the judgment could be spoken without naming a Muslim leader. A listener in Baṣra could apply it to ʿĀʾisha without the speaker saying so. One report through Abū Bakra's son names the daughter Būrān, who held the Sasanian throne briefly in 630–631 CE.[^4] Whether or not the name was original to the report, the link to a documented ruler would have given the story credibility among listeners who knew something about Persian politics.

## The Queen of Sheba Dilemma

<QuranVerse verse="27:32" />

<QuranVerse verse="27:44" />

A ruling against female leadership collides with the Qurʾān, which describes a female ruler favorably. The account of the Queen of Sheba (Q 27:22–44) shows a ruler who receives a letter from Solomon, consults her council, and decides to send a gift to Solomon and awaited his response. When she eventually recognized the truth of Solomon’s message, she submitted to God (Q 27:44). The narrative passes no judgment against her authority, and it presents her decisions with respect. This creates a problem for the woman-ruler saying. A Prophetic warning that a people ruled by a woman will never prosper contradicts a scriptural example of one that *did* prosper and submitted to God.

Later scholars responded to the Sheba problem, and their responses are part of the record. Exegetes and jurists offered several answers. Some argued that Bilqis’s rule was an exceptional circumstance, and others argued that her submission to Solomon placed her in a different category from a people who choose a woman as their ruler. These answers show that the tension was recognized and debated. They also show that the saying could not be reconciled with the Qurʾān without the Persian frame, which is one reason it needed that frame from the start.

## Why Abū Bakra Is the Source



Hadith collections contain many chains for this report, but many chains do not make many independent witnesses. Independence depends on where the lines converge. Nearly all the principal lines return to Abū Bakra. The main Basran branch runs through al Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110 AH), who had at least three identifiable pupils passing on versions of the saying: ʿAwf b. Abī Jamīla, Ḥumayd al-Ṭawīl, and Mubārak b. Faḍāla. The ʿAwf branch carries al-Bukhārī's explicit Camel explanation, the Ḥumayd branch carries al-Tirmidhī's reference to ʿĀʾisha's arrival, and the Mubārak branch often preserves only the short statement.[^5]

A second channel bypasses al-Ḥasan:[^6] Abū Bakra → ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Jawshan → ʿUyayna b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān. From ʿUyayna the saying spreads to several pupils, including Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al Qaṭṭān, Muḥammad b. Bakr, Yazīd b. Hārūn, and Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī. Its usual wording is:

## The Persian Story Keeps Changing


If the Prophet had made a memorable prediction about a particular royal succession, the event might be expected to stay fairly stable. The surviving recensions do not stay stable. The Persian story and even the verb of the maxim vary considerably.[^7][^8]

- Al-Bukhārī's ʿAwf version says that the people of Persia appointed Kisrā's daughter.

- The Ḥumayd version in al-Tirmidhī says that Kisrā died, and the Prophet then asked who succeeded him.

- A report through Abū Bakra's son names the daughter Būrān. Another Ḥumayd variant adds a Persian messenger who brings news from the court.

- Al-Ḥākim leaves the royal referent uncertain: "Kisrā or one of the kings of the non Arabs."

- One version of the Ḥumayd → al-Ḥasan → Abū Bakra chain names the king of Dhū Yazan instead, which moves the occasion to South Arabia.[^8]

The verb and the description of female rule also shift:

- They entrusted their affair to a woman.

- They assigned their affair to a woman.

- A woman rules them.

- A woman administers their affair.

A campaign message gets adapted to its audience and its moment. A Persian scene with a messenger suits a story that dramatizes the Camel, and a bare maxim suits a slogan that can be repeated without context. The frame could expand, shrink, or move to South Arabia depending on who told it and what the listener needed to feel. The verdict stayed the same in every version: *a people led by a woman will fail*. A flexible frame carried a stable message.

## The Missing Second Companion



If Abū Bakra originated the report, how should we explain the hadith that attributes the same statement to Jābir b. Samura? This is the only other Companion-level attribution of the same maxim in the corpus examined here. It appears in al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ*, no. 4861, through this chain: ʿAbd al-Wārith ibn Ibrāhīm → ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAmr ibn Jabala → Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk ibn Ḥarb → Jābir ibn Samura.

The reported hadith reads:[^9]

<HadithBlock translation={"A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper."} source={"al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861"}  arabic={"لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132"} />

On a first reading, this looks like independent corroboration. Jābir is a different Companion, and Simāk and Abū ʿAwāna are familiar transmitters. An isnād can look independent, though, without showing that those earlier figures actually transmitted this matn. Al-Ṭabarānī himself identifies where the Jābir version becomes visible:[^10]

<HadithBlock translation={"This hadith is not transmitted from Jābir b. Samura except through this isnād. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala alone transmitted it."} source={"al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861 (authorial uniqueness note)"}  arabic={"لم يرو هذا الحديث عن جابر بن سمرة إلا بهذا الإسناد تفرد به عبد الرحمن بن عمرو بن جبلة"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132"} />

The Jābir version has no second network behind it. It has one isolated narrator, Ibn Jabala, who attributes a familiar Abū Bakra maxim to another Companion. No independent pupil of Simāk or Abū ʿAwāna has been found passing this saying through Jābir. A later pupil, ʿAbd al-Wārith b. Ibrāhīm, carries it to al-Ṭabarānī, and al-Haythamī could not identify him with confidence.[^11] Scholars call this kind of construction *a spread of isnāds*, or *diving*. A known report gets a chain that bypasses its actual point of origin, so it appears to have an earlier source. Here the chain bypasses Abū Bakra and supplies a different Companion. For a propagandist, a second Companion would be useful, since one man's testimony would become a shared account.

## Ibn Jabala: The Man Behind the Jābir Isnād



The question is therefore not whether Jābir and Abū Bakra both transmitted the maxim. The question is why Ibn Jabala is the only identifiable person who transmits it through Jābir. Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī reports that he wrote down Ibn Jabala's hadith in Baṣra and then rejected them:[^12]

<QuoteBlock source={"Mīzān al-Iʿtidāl, al-Dhahabī, 2:580; Ibn Abī Ḥātim, al-Jarḥ wa-l-Taʿdīl, 5:267, no. 1260"} sourceUrl={"https://www.islamarchive.cc/a3lam/94091"}>
I wrote his hadith down in Baṣra. He used to lie, so I struck out his hadith.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">كتبت عنه بالبصرة وكان يكذب فضربت على حديثه</p>
</QuoteBlock>

Al-Dāraquṭnī went further. He charged Ibn Jabala with fabricating hadith, and he did so while examining a different tradition in which Ibn Jabala had supplied a suspiciously connected isnād. Once the Jābir version is traced to a narrator with that record, it stops working as an independent counterweight to the Abū Bakra corpus. The single bottleneck and Ibn Jabala's documented conduct point the same way.[^13]

## A Control Case: Turning a Mursal Report into a Companion Hadith


Al-Dāraquṭnī's Sunan includes a useful comparison about laughter during prayer. In one version, a blind man falls into a well, some worshippers laugh, and the Prophet supposedly orders them to repeat their ablution and prayer. In the version cited at al-Dāraquṭnī no. 603, Ibn Jabala transmits through Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → Qatāda → Abū al-ʿĀliya and Anas b. Mālik. The addition of Anas, a Companion, turns the material into a connected Prophetic report. Al-Dāraquṭnī comments:[^14]

<QuoteBlock source={"al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 603"} sourceUrl={"https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58"}>
No one narrated this from Salām except ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala. He is abandoned and fabricates hadith.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">لَمْ يَرْوِهِ عَنْ سَلَّامٍ غَيْرُ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ وَهُوَ مَتْرُوكٌ يَضَعُ الْحَدِيثَ</p>
</QuoteBlock>

He then compares the other transmissions from Qatāda. In no. 610 he writes:[^15]

<QuoteBlock source={"al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 610"} sourceUrl={"https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58"}>
Thus these five trustworthy transmitters narrated it from Qatāda, from Abū al-ʿĀliya, as mursal.
<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">فَهَؤُلَاءِ خَمْسَةٌ ثِقَاتٌ رَوَوْهُ عَنْ قَتَادَةَ عَنْ أَبِي الْعَالِيَةِ مُرْسَلًا</p>
</QuoteBlock>

The five transmitters he names are Maʿmar, Abū ʿAwāna, Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba, Saʿīd b. Bashīr, and Salm b. Abī al-Dhayyāl. In their version the report passes through Abū al-ʿĀliya with no Companion link, the link that Ibn Jabala adds. This is a second case in which Ibn Jabala alone supplies a Companion connection that the better attested version lacks. Al-Dāraquṭnī both identifies the competing mursal distribution and calls Ibn Jabala a fabricator for it.

## Where Did Ibn Jabala Get the Wording?



The clearest evidence is the Arabic matn itself. The isolated Jābir text can be compared with the exact recensions circulating in Abū Bakra's network.

The Jābir version through Ibn Jabala:

<HadithBlock translation={"A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper."} source={"al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861"}  arabic={"لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132"} />

The Mubārak b. Faḍāla version through al-Ḥasan and Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock translation={"A people ruled by a woman will never prosper."} source={"al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, nos. 20478 and 20517"}  arabic={"لَا يُفْلِحُ قَوْمٌ تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/10/42"} />

The ʿUyayna family version through Abū Bakra:

<HadithBlock translation={"A people who assign their affair to a woman will never prosper."} source={"al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921"}  arabic={"لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/tayalisi/34"} />

All three begin with “*a people will never prosper.*” The Jābir and Mubārak versions share the root ملك (to rule or possess). Mubārak’s sentence means “*a woman rules them,*” and Ibn Jabala’s means “*a woman rules their affair.*” This is the kind of small change that happens when a familiar slogan is retold. Adding “*their affair*” would have been easy, since that phrase appears in other Abū Bakra recensions, notably “*they entrusted their affair*” and “*they assigned their affair.*” The Mubārak/al-Ḥasan family is therefore a better textual source for Ibn Jabala’s wording than ʿUyayna’s family. Mubārak’s transmission preserves the bare maxim in both forms, with “*not*” and with “*will not*.” Ibn Ḥibbān 4516 and Aḥmad 20517 preserve “*will never prosper … a woman rules them*” form, which is the closest to the Jābir saying.[^16]

In Fatḥ al-Bārī, Ibn Ḥajar cites ʿUmar b. Shabba through Mubārak b. Faḍāla, from al-Ḥasan, reporting that ʿĀʾisha sent to Abū Bakra. His reply was:[^17]

<HadithBlock translation={"You are indeed a mother, and your right is great. But I heard the Messenger of God say: ‘A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.’"} arabic={"إنك لأم وإن حقك لعظيم ولكن سمعت رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم يقول لن يفلح قوم تملكهم امرأة"} source={"Fatḥ al-Bārī, Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī, vol. 13, commentary on the woman-ruler report"} url={"https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/2605/46"} />


This is a later citation of ʿUmar b. Shabba's report, not a new eyewitness account. It matters for the history of the Mubārak family. The branch that preserves the compact “*a woman rules them”* maxim also reports its use against ʿĀʾisha's appeal for support. The branch therefore supplies both the closest wording to Ibn Jabala's version and a Basran Camel setting that explains the content. The slogan appears in the same argument, in the same city. Ibn Jabala borrowed a short Mubārak-type maxim that was already circulating in al-Ḥasan's Basran circle, and he assigned it to Jābir, a separate companion whom he has used prior in order to fabricate better-attested chains according to Dāraquṭnī and other scholars.

This explains Ibn Jabala's chain. The content could come from the Basran Abū Bakra tradition, and the authority could come from a Jābir chain that already existed. The recognized Jābir line carried a prophecy about Kisrā[^18], and the Abū Bakra maxim had a Persian royal frame. We can show that he did not need to invent an implausible chain. He needed only to attach new content to a plausible line. The resulting sequence appears below:

The borrowing hypothesis rests on five points:

1) A closely matching Abū Bakra text was in circulation.

2) Ibn Jabala claimed access to al-Ḥasan-associated Basran transmissions.

3) The substitute Jābir chain circulated independently, with different content.

4) Only Ibn Jabala attaches the woman-ruler maxim to that chain.

5) In another report, classical critics identify him as the sole source of a suspect Companion connection.

The geography and chronology fit.[^19] Abū Bakra and al-Ḥasan belong to Baṣra, and so do Mubārak and the circle through which the saying was distributed. Abū ʿAwāna (d. 175 AH) connects the transmission milieus of Wāsiṭ and Iraq, and Simāk (d. 123 AH) belongs to Kūfa. Abū Ḥātim met Ibn Jabala in Baṣra, where the maxim already existed in several forms. The surviving record does not show when Ibn Jabala first recited this chain. The event the saying explains, however, took place long before his transmission activity.[^19]

## Reconstructing the Spread of the Tradition


First, a Basran audience had to decide whether to support ʿĀʾisha's campaign. A verdict against a people commanded by a woman, presented as a Prophetic judgment, gave them the answer to oppose her. Abū Bakra, who had refused to fight, was in a position to supply that form of propaganda. The hadith presented his abstention as foreknowledge and presented ʿĀʾisha's cause as doomed. The content, the Persian frame, and the timing all match this purpose. They make Abū Bakra the most likely originator of the attribu‐ tion.

Second, the message moved beyond the circles that later collected hadith. Its audience was the Basran public, and its carriers were people for whom the saying was an instrument of persuasion. The hadith collections preserve only the part of that circulation that passed into transmission. The surviving network around al-Ḥasan, ʿUyayna, and Abū Bakra’s descendants is the trace of that part. It does not map the whole campaign of regular non-transmitters who heard the narrative and decided to either support or oppose ʿĀʾisha's campaign based off it.

Third, the short “*a people ruled by a woman will never prosper*” wording moved through Mubārak b. Faḍāla's al-Ḥasan branch. The ʿUmar b. Shabba report that Ibn Ḥajar cites links this form to the specific Abū Bakra–ʿĀʾisha encounter. It was the most portable version of the saying. People could recognize it and use it as a slogan without any background.

Fourth, Ibn Jabala encountered this wording, or a very close version, and attached it to a recognized Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk → Jābir chain[^10]. The new attribution gave the Abū Bakra corpus an apparently independent Companion confirmation. Ibn Jabala's isolated position in the transmission record, his Basran location, and al-Dāraquṭnī's detailed charge against his isnād construction in another report all point to him as the likeliest creator of the substitute chain.

Finally, the Jābir version survived through one identified later route into al-Ṭabarānī's collection. Its survival next to the much larger Abū Bakra family created the appearance of two Companion witnesses. The transmission evidence points to one early Basran origin and one later reassignment.

An alternative explanation would require that Jābir taught the maxim to Simāk, that Simāk taught it to Abū ʿAwāna, and that it then disappeared from every other recovered pupil of those transmitters while surviving only through a man accused of fabrication. That explanation is much less economical than the reconstruction above, which accounts at for the bottleneck, the close match in the matn, the existing substitute chain, and Ibn Jabala's documented conduct. In short, Abū Bakra supplied a prophecy so that a Basran audience would turn against ʿĀʾisha. Ibn Jabala, a documented fabricator of isnads, supplied the appearance of a second Companion attestation.

<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">The source-critical reconstruction advanced above distinguishes attested transmission nodes and wording differences from the proposed political motives and routes of borrowing. The latter remain historical inferences rather than directly witnessed events.</VerdictBox>
