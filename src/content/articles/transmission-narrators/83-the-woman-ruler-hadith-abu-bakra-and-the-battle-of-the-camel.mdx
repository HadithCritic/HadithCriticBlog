*How a saying about the Persians became an argument against ʿĀʾisha's leadership in Baṣra, and how later hadith transmission preserved and reshaped it.*

## A Prophecy with a Convenient Ending

In 36 AH (656 CE), ʿĀʾisha, accompanied by Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr, led an armed coalition into Baṣra. The coalition fought the forces of ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭālib, and the encounter became known as the Battle of the Camel. ʿĀʾisha's side lost. One Basran Companion, Abū Bakra Nufayʿ b. al-Ḥārith, is remembered for refusing to join her.

His refusal was later explained with a hadith. According to the version in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 4425, Abū Bakra said:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī, no. 4425" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/64/83">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"God benefited me during the days of the Camel through a saying that I had heard from the Messenger of God, after I had almost joined the people of the Camel and fought alongside them … 'A people who entrust their affair to a woman will never prosper.'"

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَقَدْ نَفَعَنِي اللَّهُ بِكَلِمَةٍ سَمِعْتُهَا مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ أَيَّامَ الْجَمَلِ بَعْدَ مَا كِدْتُ أَنْ أَلْحَقَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْجَمَلِ فَأُقَاتِلَ مَعَهُمْ ... لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمْ امْرَأَةً</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The report says that Muḥammad learned the Persians had made Kisrā's daughter their ruler, and that he then pronounced the warning. Abū Bakra remembered it decades later, when ʿĀʾisha's movement forced him to choose a side.[^1]

Another major recension is more explicit. In al-Tirmidhī 2262, Abū Bakra says:

<HadithBlock source="al-Jāmiʿ, al-Tirmidhī, no. 2262" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tirmidhi/33/75">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"When ʿĀʾisha arrived, that is in Baṣra, I remembered the statement of the Messenger of God, and God protected me through it."[^2]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>فَلَمَّا قَدِمَتْ عَائِشَةُ، يَعْنِي الْبَصْرَةَ، ذَكَرْتُ قَوْلَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ فَعَصَمَنِي اللَّهُ بِهِ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The narrative already interprets itself. The Persian episode is not recalled for its own sake as Sasanian history. It frames a judgment about a much later Iraqi conflict. The prediction appears to have been made for the moment its narrator needed it, when a woman led a coalition and Abū Bakra wanted his abstention to look divinely approved. The saying turned a contested decision into a verdict that any listener in Baṣra could apply.

The legal question, whether the saying bans women from political office, comes after a more basic one: who first put this statement on the Prophet's tongue, and why? This article argues that the saying was made to work as political persuasion, and that its target was the Basran public deciding whether to follow ʿĀʾisha.

## The Battle of the Camel and the Politics of Persuasion

The political context explains the tradition better than a general dispute about gender does. Baṣra was divided during the First Fitna. Some supported ʿĀʾisha's coalition, some supported ʿAlī, and others wanted to stay out of a Muslim civil war. In the sources that construct Abū Bakra's public image, he belongs to the last group.

Elizabeth Urban's *Conquered Populations in Early Islam* devotes a full chapter to Abū Bakra. She identifies the Battle of the Camel as one of the main events through which later Muslims remembered him. Urban presents him as a figure who repeatedly argued for withdrawal and reconciliation, and not simply as an enemy of ʿĀʾisha. She also traces how stories about his origins, his manumission, his relationship with his half-brother Ziyād b. Abīhi, and his legal controversies were shaped by the needs of historical and hadith literature.[^3]

This article argues that the woman-ruler saying was propaganda. Its purpose was to convince ordinary people in Baṣra that supporting ʿĀʾisha was a mistake, and that the Prophet had already said so. Abū Bakra's own refusal to fight provided the example. The saying went beyond justifying one man's abstention. It told the public that a woman's command was illegitimate in advance, and it made staying home look like obedience.

Propaganda of this kind relies on simple, portable claims. It names no living person, because naming one would invite a reply. It draws on a distant authority, the Prophet, and on a setting that seems historical rather than partisan. The Persian succession supplied that setting. It was remote enough to avoid direct accusation and familiar enough to be repeated in any gathering without naming a contemporary leader.

The narrative structure supports this reading. It moves from a prediction about Persia, to the temptation to join the Camel forces, to the recollection of the prediction, and finally to the defeat that seems to confirm it. The sequence turns a debatable political choice into a matter of obedience. It also shields Abū Bakra from the charge that he abandoned a righteous cause, because he is shown acting on knowledge that the fighters ignored. A story told this way is built to persuade, since the outcome has already been foretold to the listener.

Propaganda works whether or not its author believed it. The useful question is what the words did. Whether Abū Bakra thought the Prophet had said them matters less. The saying made a specific political choice look like a religious duty, and that effect explains why a Basran audience would have found it worth repeating.

## Why the Persian Frame Mattered

The choice of Persia was deliberate. Sasanian royal succession had real precedents for female rule, which gave the saying historical texture that a flat condemnation would have lacked. The events were distant enough to be safe and familiar enough to be believed.

The frame also allowed a useful ambiguity. The Persian ruler could stand for another polity, so the judgment could be spoken without naming a Muslim leader. The maxim itself stayed general: a people entrusting its affair to a woman. A listener in Baṣra could apply it to ʿĀʾisha without the speaker saying so.

Some transmitters tied the story to real events. One report through Abū Bakra's son names the daughter Būrān, who held the Sasanian throne briefly in 630–631 CE. Whether or not the name was original to the report, the link to a documented ruler would have given the story credibility among listeners who knew something about Persian politics.

## The Bilqis Problem

A ruling against female leadership collides with the Qurʾān, which describes a female ruler favorably. The account of the Queen of Sheba (Q 27:22–44) shows a ruler who receives a letter from Solomon, consults her council, and says, "I have never decided an affair until you have witnessed it" (Q 27:32). When she recognizes the truth of Solomon's message, she submits to God alongside him (Q 27:44). The narrative passes no judgment against her authority, and it presents her decisions with respect.

This creates a problem for the woman-ruler saying. A Prophetic warning that a people ruled by a woman will never prosper seems to contradict a scriptural example that the audience would have known. Anyone using the saying against ʿĀʾisha would face the obvious objection: the Qurʾān praises a queen who led her people.

The Persian frame let the saying avoid that objection. It confined the judgment to a non-Arab, pre-Islamic polity and placed Bilqis in a different story with a different moral. The saying could then be applied to a Muslim leader in Baṣra without an explicit contradiction of the Qurʾān appearing on the surface. The framing itself is evidence of design. A sincere report about the Prophet's judgment would not need this kind of fencing, while a report built for a political argument would.

Later scholars responded to the Sheba problem, and their responses are part of the record. Exegetes and jurists offered several answers. Some argued that Bilqis's rule was exceptional, and others argued that her submission to Solomon placed her in a different category from a people who choose a woman as their ruler. These answers show that the tension was recognized and debated. They also show that the saying could not be reconciled with the Qurʾān without the Persian frame, which is one reason it needed that frame from the start.

## Why Abū Bakra Is the Source

Hadith collections contain many chains for this report, but many chains do not make many independent witnesses. Independence depends on where the lines converge.

Nearly all the principal lines return to **Abū Bakra**. The main Basran branch runs through al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī (d. 110 AH), who had at least three identifiable pupils passing on versions of the saying: **ʿAwf b. Abī Jamīla**, **Ḥumayd al-Ṭawīl**, and **Mubārak b. Faḍāla**. Their versions differ in wording and narrative setting, but all of them claim the same Companion source. The ʿAwf branch carries al-Bukhārī's explicit Camel explanation, the Ḥumayd branch carries al-Tirmidhī's reference to ʿĀʾisha's arrival, and the Mubārak branch often preserves only the short statement.[^4]

A second channel bypasses al-Ḥasan: **Abū Bakra → ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Jawshan → ʿUyayna b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān**. From ʿUyayna the saying spreads to several pupils, including Yaḥyā b. Saʿīd al-Qaṭṭān, Muḥammad b. Bakr, Yazīd b. Hārūn, and Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī. Its usual wording is:

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tayalisi/34">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"A people who assign their affair to a woman will never prosper."[^5]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Because the text circulated through several of ʿUyayna's pupils, ʿUyayna is a **partial common link** (PCL).

Abū Bakra's household gives a similar picture. Al-Bazzār records one path through his son **ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz**, via Abū al-Minhāl al-Bakrāwī, and this version names Kisrā's daughter as **Būrān**. Another path runs through his son **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān**, via ʿAlī b. Zayd b. Judʿān, and survives in Aḥmad 20508. Its opening, *wa-qāla Abū Bakra*, continues a chain taken from the preceding report, so it is not a separate witness. Both family paths, despite their individual weaknesses, end with Abū Bakra.[^6]

A related narrative through **Bakkār b. ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz** has the Prophet told that an enemy people were governed by a woman, after which he says **<Arabic>هَلَكَتِ الرِّجَالُ حِينَ أَطَاعَتِ النِّسَاءَ</Arabic>**, "Men perished when they obeyed women." The wording differs from the main maxim, so it should not be counted as a duplicate witness. It does show a wider theme of female rule connected to Abū Bakra and his descendants.[^7]

The overall picture is:

Abū Bakra is the one figure shared by the substantial Companion-attributed network. Al-Ḥasan and ʿUyayna distributed the saying downstream, and they are not separate Companion origins. As with any common-link argument, this identifies the most likely source of the attribution. It does not prove every link above and below him. The Camel setting gives an independent historical reason to name Abū Bakra, beyond the shape of the isnāds.

The network has a limit that needs stating. It records the saying as scholars collected and preserved it. It does not measure how far the saying traveled in ordinary speech. A message meant for civilians choosing sides would move through conversation, recitation at gatherings, letters, and sermons, most of which never entered an isnād. The chains therefore show which circles later gathered the saying, not who heard it when it was first used. Motive has to come from the content, the timing, and the narrative, and the isnāds serve a different question, that of authenticity and borrowing.

## The Persian Story Keeps Changing

If the Prophet had made a memorable prediction about a particular royal succession, the event might be expected to stay fairly stable. The surviving recensions do not stay stable. The Persian story and even the verb of the maxim vary considerably.

- Al-Bukhārī's ʿAwf version says that **the people of Persia appointed Kisrā's daughter**.
- The Ḥumayd version in al-Tirmidhī says that **Kisrā died**, and the Prophet then asked who succeeded him.
- A report through Abū Bakra's son **names the daughter Būrān**. Another Ḥumayd variant adds a **Persian messenger** who brings news from the court.
- Al-Ḥākim 8599 leaves the royal referent uncertain: **"Kisrā or one of the kings of the non-Arabs."**
- One version of the Ḥumayd → al-Ḥasan → Abū Bakra chain names **the king of Dhū Yazan** instead, which moves the occasion to South Arabia.[^8]

The verb and the description of female rule also shift:

<HadithBlock source="Recension comparison: Ṣaḥīḥ, al-Bukhārī, no. 4425; al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921; al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, nos. 20438 and 20517">

**<Arabic>وَلَّوْا أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةً</Arabic>**: "They entrusted their affair to a woman."

**<Arabic>أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>**: "They assigned their affair to a woman."

**<Arabic>تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**: "A woman rules them."

**<Arabic>يَلِي أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**: "A woman administers their affair."

</HadithBlock>

This variation is informative. A campaign message gets adapted to its audience and its moment. A Persian scene with a messenger suits a story that dramatizes the Camel, and a bare maxim suits a slogan that can be repeated without context. The frame could expand, shrink, or move to South Arabia depending on who told it and what the listener needed to feel. The verdict stayed the same in every version: a people led by a woman will fail. A flexible frame carried a stable message.

## Dating the Saying

No source before the Battle of the Camel attests the woman-ruler saying. The earliest attestations come from second-century transmitters and appear in collections compiled later still. Mālik's *Muwaṭṭāʾ* (d. 179 AH) is one of the earliest surviving hadith compilations, and it was written roughly a century and a half after the events it describes.

The absence needs careful reading. Silence in the sources before 36 AH says little, because the surviving corpus is late and does not record most of what circulated in the early decades. A claim that the saying must be invented because it is absent before the Camel would be weak. Absence is what the propaganda reading predicts, but it would also appear if the saying were authentic and simply unrecorded.

The stronger point concerns fit. The first attestations come only after the political situation that would make the saying useful. Its content, a general condemnation of female rule, is tied to one contested decision in one city. The timing fits a message made for a specific campaign, though it does not prove one. The accurate summary is this: nothing before the Camel attests the saying, and the first witnesses come from after the Camel, when it could serve a purpose.

## The Missing Second Companion

If Abū Bakra originated the report, how should we explain the hadith that attributes the same statement to **Jābir b. Samura**?

This is the only other Companion-level attribution of the *same maxim* in the corpus examined here. It appears in al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ*, no. 4861, through this chain:

<HadithBlock translation={"ʿAbd al-Wārith b. Ibrāhīm, from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala, from Abū ʿAwāna, from Simāk b. Ḥarb, from Jābir b. Samura."} source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861 (chain excerpt)" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>عَبْدُ الْوَارِثِ بْنُ إِبْرَاهِيمَ ← عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ ← أَبُو عَوَانَةَ ← سِمَاكُ بْنُ حَرْبٍ ← جَابِرُ بْنُ سَمُرَةَ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The reported saying reads:

<HadithBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper."[^9]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

On a first reading, this looks like independent corroboration. Jābir is a different Companion, and Simāk and Abū ʿAwāna are familiar transmitters. An isnād can look independent, though, without showing that those earlier figures actually transmitted this matn.

Al-Ṭabarānī himself identifies where the Jābir version becomes visible:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861 (authorial uniqueness note)" sourceUrl="https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-13/h/335995">

"This hadith is not transmitted from Jābir b. Samura except through this isnād. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala alone transmitted it."[^10]

**<Arabic>لَا يُرْوَى هَذَا الْحَدِيثُ عَنْ جَابِرِ بْنِ سَمُرَةَ إِلَّا بِهَذَا الْإِسْنَادِ، تَفَرَّدَ بِهِ عَبْدُ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنُ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

This changes the evidence. The Jābir version has no second network behind it. It has one isolated narrator, Ibn Jabala, who attributes a familiar Abū Bakra maxim to another Companion. No independent pupil of Simāk or Abū ʿAwāna has been found passing *this saying* through Jābir. A later pupil, ʿAbd al-Wārith b. Ibrāhīm, carries it to al-Ṭabarānī, and al-Haythamī could not identify him with confidence.[^10]

Hadith historians call this kind of construction a *spread of isnāds*, or *diving*. A known report gets a chain that bypasses its actual point of origin, so it appears to have an earlier source. Here the chain bypasses Abū Bakra and supplies a different Companion. For a propagandist, a second Companion would be useful, since one man's testimony would become a shared account.

## Ibn Jabala: The Man Behind the Jābir Isnād

The question is therefore not whether Jābir and Abū Bakra both transmitted the maxim. The question is why Ibn Jabala is the only identifiable person who transmits it through Jābir.

The classical critics' remarks about him are revealing. Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī reports that he wrote down Ibn Jabala's hadith in Baṣra and then rejected them:

<QuoteBlock source="Mīzān al-Iʿtidāl, al-Dhahabī, 2:580, entry ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala" sourceUrl="https://www.taraajem.com/persons/175728/عبد-الرحمن-بن-عمرو-بن-جبلة">

"I wrote his hadith down in Baṣra. He used to lie, so I struck out his hadith."[^11]

**<Arabic>كَتَبْتُ عَنْهُ بِالْبَصْرَةِ، وَكَانَ يَكْذِبُ، فَضَرَبْتُ عَلَى حَدِيثِهِ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

Al-Dāraquṭnī went further. He charged Ibn Jabala with fabricating hadith, and he did so while examining a different tradition in which Ibn Jabala had supplied a suspiciously connected isnād.

Once the Jābir version is traced to a narrator with that record, it stops working as an independent counterweight to the Abū Bakra corpus. The single bottleneck and Ibn Jabala's documented conduct point the same way.

## A Control Case: Turning a *Mursal* Report into a Companion Hadith

Al-Dāraquṭnī's *Sunan* includes a useful comparison about laughter during prayer. In one version, a blind man falls into a well, some worshippers laugh, and the Prophet supposedly orders them to repeat their ablution and prayer.

In the version cited at al-Dāraquṭnī no. 603, Ibn Jabala transmits through **Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → Qatāda → Abū al-ʿĀliya and Anas b. Mālik**. The addition of **Anas**, a Companion, turns the material into a connected Prophetic report. Al-Dāraquṭnī comments:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 603" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58">

"No one narrated this from Salām except ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala. He is abandoned and fabricates hadith."[^12]

**<Arabic>لَمْ يَرْوِهِ عَنْ سَلَّامٍ غَيْرُ عَبْدِ الرَّحْمَنِ بْنِ عَمْرِو بْنِ جَبَلَةَ، وَهُوَ مَتْرُوكٌ يَضَعُ الْحَدِيثَ</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

He then compares the other transmissions from Qatāda. In no. 610 he writes:

<QuoteBlock source="al-Sunan, al-Dāraquṭnī, no. 610" sourceUrl="https://hadithunlocked.com/daraqutni/1/58">

"Thus these five trustworthy transmitters narrated it from Qatāda, from Abū al-ʿĀliya, as *mursal*."[^13]

**<Arabic>فَهَؤُلَاءِ خَمْسَةٌ ثِقَاتٌ رَوَوْهُ عَنْ قَتَادَةَ عَنْ أَبِي الْعَالِيَةِ مُرْسَلًا</Arabic>**.

</QuoteBlock>

The five transmitters he names are Maʿmar, Abū ʿAwāna, Saʿīd b. Abī ʿArūba, Saʿīd b. Bashīr, and Salm b. Abī al-Dhayyāl. In their version the report passes through **Abū al-ʿĀliya with no Companion link**, the link that Ibn Jabala adds. (The Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ claimed by Ibn Jabala and the Salm b. Abī al-Dhayyāl among the alternative witnesses are different people.)

This is a second case in which Ibn Jabala alone supplies a Companion connection that the better-attested version lacks. Al-Dāraquṭnī both identifies the competing *mursal* distribution and calls Ibn Jabala a fabricator.

Ibn Jabala also fits the right **Basran environment**. In al-Ṭabarānī's *al-Muʿjam al-Kabīr*, he transmits several other sayings through **Salām b. Abī Muṭīʿ → Qatāda → al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī → Samura b. Jundab**. The subjects are contracts, enclosed land, consultation, and dress, and the isnāds appear at nos. 6843, 6867, 6914, and 6915.[^14] These reports show the al-Ḥasan-related Basran material Ibn Jabala claimed to hold. The woman-ruler saying circulated in that same milieu.

## Where Did Ibn Jabala Get the Wording?

The clearest evidence is the Arabic *matn* itself. The isolated Jābir text can be compared with the exact recensions circulating in Abū Bakra's network.

**The Jābir version through Ibn Jabala:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Muʿjam al-Awsaṭ, al-Ṭabarānī, no. 4861" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tabarani-awsat/16/132">

<div class="hc-report__en">

"A people whose affair is governed by a woman will never prosper."

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ يَمْلِكُ أَمْرَهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>

</HadithBlock>

**The Mubārak b. Faḍāla version through al-Ḥasan and Abū Bakra:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, nos. 20478 and 20517" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/10/42">

<div class="hc-report__en">

"A people ruled by a woman will never prosper."

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.[^15]

</div>

</HadithBlock>

**The ʿUyayna family version through Abū Bakra:**

<HadithBlock source="al-Musnad, Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, no. 921" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/tayalisi/34">

<div class="hc-report__en">

"A people who assign their affair to a woman will never prosper."

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ أَسْنَدُوا أَمْرَهُمْ إِلَى امْرَأَةٍ</Arabic>**.[^5]

</div>

</HadithBlock>

All three begin with **<Arabic>لن يفلح قوم</Arabic>** ("a people will never prosper"). The Jābir and Mubārak versions share the root **<Arabic>ملك</Arabic>** ("to rule or possess"). Mubārak's sentence means "a woman rules them," and Ibn Jabala's means "a woman rules their affair." This is the kind of small change that happens when a familiar slogan is retold. Adding **<Arabic>أمرهم</Arabic>** ("their affair") would have been easy, since that phrase appears in other Abū Bakra recensions, notably **<Arabic>ولوا أمرهم</Arabic>** ("they entrusted their affair") and **<Arabic>أسندوا أمرهم</Arabic>** ("they assigned their affair").

The Mubārak/al-Ḥasan family is therefore a better textual source for Ibn Jabala's wording than ʿUyayna's family. Mubārak's transmission preserves the bare maxim in both forms, with **<Arabic>لا</Arabic>** ("not") and with **<Arabic>لن</Arabic>** ("will not"). Ibn Ḥibbān 4516 and Aḥmad 20517 preserve the **<Arabic>لن يفلح ... تملكهم</Arabic>** ("will never prosper … a woman rules them") form, which is the closest to the Jābir saying.[^15]

A narrative witness from the same family adds weight. In *Fatḥ al-Bārī*, Ibn Ḥajar cites **ʿUmar b. Shabba through Mubārak b. Faḍāla, from al-Ḥasan**, reporting that ʿĀʾisha sent to Abū Bakra. His reply was:

<HadithBlock source="Fatḥ al-Bārī, Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī, vol. 13, commentary on the woman-ruler report (quoting ʿUmar b. Shabba)" url="https://ablibrary.net/book_content/b/2605/46">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"You are indeed a mother, and your right is great. But I heard the Messenger of God say: 'A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.'"[^16]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>إِنَّكِ لَأُمٌّ، وَإِنَّ حَقَّكِ لَعَظِيمٌ، وَلَكِنْ سَمِعْتُ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ يَقُولُ: لَنْ يُفْلِحَ قَوْمٌ تَمْلِكُهُمُ امْرَأَةٌ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

This is a later citation of ʿUmar b. Shabba's report, not a new eyewitness account. It matters for the history of the Mubārak family. The branch that preserves the compact **<Arabic>تملكهم امرأة</Arabic>** ("a woman rules them") maxim also reports its use against ʿĀʾisha's appeal for support. The branch therefore supplies both the closest wording to Ibn Jabala's version and a Basran Camel setting that explains the content. The slogan appears in the same argument, in the same city.

Ibn Jabala borrowed a short Mubārak-type maxim that was already circulating in al-Ḥasan's Basran circle, and he assigned it to Jābir. The donor can be identified at the level of the recension and its regional network.

The chain itself is not random. The sequence **Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk b. Ḥarb → Jābir b. Samura** appears in other hadiths *without* Ibn Jabala. Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2919b preserves one example:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ, Muslim b. al-Ḥajjāj, no. 2919b" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim/54/18">
<div class="hc-report__en">

"A group of Muslims, or believers, will certainly open the treasure of the house of Kisrā at al-Abyaḍ."[^18]

</div>
<div class="hc-report__ar" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لَتَفْتَحَنَّ عِصَابَةٌ مِنَ الْمُسْلِمِينَ أَوْ مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ كَنْزَ آلِ كِسْرَى الَّذِي فِي الْأَبْيَضِ</Arabic>**.

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Aḥmad 20821 and al-Ṭabarānī 1975 carry the same Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk → Jābir line for the Kisrā-treasure report, and other pupils of Simāk, including Shuʿba, preserve the treasure prediction too. This was a real, recognized isnād in the corpus, and it carried a different saying.[^19]

This explains Ibn Jabala's chain more easily. The content could come from the Basran Abū Bakra tradition, and the authority could come from a Jābir chain that already existed. The Persian connection is suggestive. The recognized Jābir line carried a prophecy about Kisrā, and the Abū Bakra maxim had a Persian royal frame. We cannot show that the word "Kisrā" prompted Ibn Jabala's choice. We can show that he did not need to invent an implausible chain. He needed only to attach new content to a plausible line.

The borrowing hypothesis rests on five points:

1. A closely matching Abū Bakra text was in circulation.
2. Ibn Jabala claimed access to al-Ḥasan-associated Basran transmissions.
3. The substitute Jābir chain circulated independently, with different content.
4. Only Ibn Jabala attaches the woman-ruler maxim to that chain.
5. In another report, classical critics identify him as the sole source of a suspect Companion connection.

The geography and chronology fit. Abū Bakra and al-Ḥasan belong to Baṣra, and so do Mubārak and the circle through which the saying was distributed. Abū ʿAwāna (d. 175 AH) connects the transmission milieus of Wāsiṭ and Iraq, and Simāk (d. 123 AH) belongs to Kūfa. Abū Ḥātim met Ibn Jabala in **Baṣra**, where the maxim already existed in several forms. The surviving record does not show when Ibn Jabala first recited this chain. The event the saying explains, however, took place long before his transmission activity.[^20]

## Reconstructing the Spread of the Tradition

The evidence fits into one sequence. The reconstruction separates the campaign that produced the saying from the later process by which hadith transmitters preserved it.

**First**, after ʿĀʾisha's campaign and defeat at the Battle of the Camel, a Basran audience had to decide whether to support her coalition. A verdict against a people commanded by a woman, presented as a Prophetic judgment made in advance, gave them a ready answer. Abū Bakra, who had refused to fight, was in a position to supply it. The saying presented his abstention as foreknowledge and presented ʿĀʾisha's cause as doomed. The content, the Persian frame, and the timing all match this purpose. They make Abū Bakra the most likely originator of the attribution.

**Second**, the message moved beyond the circles that later collected hadith. Its audience was the Basran public, and its carriers were people for whom the saying was an instrument of persuasion and not a scholarly item. The hadith collections preserve only the part of that circulation that passed into transmission. The surviving network around al-Ḥasan, ʿUyayna, and Abū Bakra's descendants is the trace of that part. It does not map the whole campaign.

**Third**, the short **<Arabic>لن يفلح قوم تملكهم امرأة</Arabic>** ("A people ruled by a woman will never prosper.") wording moved through Mubārak b. Faḍāla's al-Ḥasan branch. The ʿUmar b. Shabba report that Ibn Ḥajar cites links this form to the specific Abū Bakra–ʿĀʾisha encounter. It was the most portable version of the saying. People could recognize it and use it as a slogan without any background.

**Fourth**, Ibn Jabala encountered this wording, or a very close version, changed **<Arabic>تملكهم</Arabic>** ("she rules them") to **<Arabic>يملك أمرهم</Arabic>** ("rules their affair"), and attached it to a recognized **Abū ʿAwāna → Simāk → Jābir** chain. The new attribution gave the Abū Bakra corpus what it lacked, an apparently independent Companion confirmation. Ibn Jabala's isolated position in the transmission record, his Basran location, and al-Dāraquṭnī's detailed charge against his isnād construction in another report all point to him as the likeliest creator of the substitute chain.

**Finally**, the Jābir version survived through one identified later route into al-Ṭabarānī's collection. Its survival next to the much larger Abū Bakra family created the appearance of two Companion witnesses. The transmission evidence points to **one early Basran origin and one later reassignment**.

An alternative explanation would require that Jābir taught the maxim to Simāk, that Simāk taught it to Abū ʿAwāna, and that it then disappeared from every other recovered pupil of those transmitters while surviving only through a man accused of fabrication. That explanation is possible. It is much less economical than the reconstruction above, which accounts at once for the bottleneck, the close match in the matn, the existing substitute chain, and Ibn Jabala's documented conduct.

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">

The hadith is often discussed as though its origin lay in the Persian succession to Kisrā. Its most informative recensions point elsewhere. The saying defends Abū Bakra's conduct during the Battle of the Camel, and it was built to persuade the people of Baṣra that supporting ʿĀʾisha was a mistake and that the Prophet had already said so. The Persian frame kept the verdict at a safe distance from a living Companion. The qualification that the Bilqis precedent requires shows that its makers knew the saying could not be squared with the Qurʾān without that frame. The campaign explains why a general condemnation of female leadership became a memorable Prophetic prediction, and why its verdict stayed fixed while its setting changed.

The common-link and matn evidence supports a narrower claim. Abū Bakra is the likeliest author of the Prophetic attribution. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, ʿUyayna, and the family-connected transmitters preserved and distributed it. The network records what the hadith collections kept, not the full reach of the campaign. Its value for the argument about motive lies in the saying's content and timing. Its value for the argument about authenticity lies in how the saying was carried and reshaped.

The apparent independent witness through Jābir b. Samura does not overturn this. It first appears through **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. ʿAmr b. Jabala**, a Basran transmitter explicitly condemned for fabrication. The short Jābir wording most closely resembles **Mubārak b. Faḍāla's recension from al-Ḥasan and Abū Bakra**. The backbone of the Jābir isnād is attested independently, for a different prophecy about Kisrā. Al-Dāraquṭnī's comparison of Ibn Jabala's prayer report with several *mursal* witnesses is a close parallel for how a connected authority gets manufactured.

In short, Abū Bakra supplied a prophecy after the event, shaped so that a Basran audience would turn against ʿĀʾisha. Ibn Jabala supplied the appearance of a second Companion who had heard it. The traditional claim of independent corroboration breaks down into a history of transmission, appropriation, and isnād substitution, and the political purpose of the saying is still visible beneath that history.

</VerdictBox>
