<ClaimBox title="The argument in one sentence">
A hadith can be a human report about something the Prophet may have said, done, approved, remembered, inferred, or taught; it cannot logically become *wahi* itself when its wording, context, legal force, and even historical setting reach us through fallible, meaning-based transmission.
</ClaimBox>

The argument that hadith are *wahi* usually begins with a sound instinct: the Prophet was not an ordinary religious commentator. He was a messenger. His mission was not merely to recite isolated verses and leave the community with no guidance about worship, judgment, or moral life. The Qurʾān commands obedience to the Messenger, speaks of God teaching the Book and Wisdom, and describes the Prophet as a recipient of revelation.

The question is not whether the Prophet was guided by God. The question is whether later hadith reports, collected through human chains, often transmitted by meaning, stabilized centuries after the events they describe, can themselves be classified as divine revelation. Those are not the same question. A prophet can be guided without every later report about him being revelation. A report can preserve a valuable memory without becoming scripture. A narration can carry a true meaning without preserving the revealed form in which that meaning was first known.

This distinction matters because the traditional claim often slides between three different things: God’s revelation to the Prophet, the Prophet’s own guidance and judgment, and the later hadith archive. Once those layers are separated, the usual argument becomes much weaker.

## The word *wahi* is broader than “scripture”

The first problem is semantic. In the Qurʾān, *wahi* does not always mean a scripture revealed to a prophet. God “reveals” to the bee, inspires the mother of Moses, communicates to the angels, and even says that devils “inspire” their allies. The word can denote divine communication, command, inspiration, prompting, or disclosure depending on context.[^1]

<QuranVerse verse="16:68" />
<QuranVerse verse="28:7" />
<QuranVerse verse="8:12" />
<QuranVerse verse="6:121" />
<QuranVerse verse="41:12" />

That broad usage does not make the bee’s instinct scripture. It does not make the mother of Moses a prophetess whose words became a binding book. It does not make every inspired action a second Qurʾān. The Qurʾān’s language therefore cannot be reduced to a simple formula: “wahi equals binding textual revelation.” If someone wants to call the Prophet’s non-Qurʾānic guidance *wahi* in some broad sense, he still has to explain what kind of *wahi* it is, how it was preserved, and whether the later hadith text is identical with it.

This is where the argument usually moves too fast. It says: the Prophet received *wahi*; the Prophet taught things outside the Qurʾān; hadith report those teachings; therefore hadith are *wahi*. But the conclusion does not follow. At most, it would show that some prophetic guidance may have had divine support. It does not show that every later report about that guidance is itself divine revelation.

The distinction is simple: the Qurʾān can use *wahi* broadly, but the theological status of the Qurʾān is not produced by the word *wahi* alone. It is produced by the Qurʾān’s own claim to be a recited, guarded, detailed, public revelation from God. A hadith report does not inherit that status merely because it is about the Prophet.

## The An-Najm proof does not carry the hadith corpus

The most common proof-text is Sūrat al-Najm:

<QuranVerse verse="53:1-5" />

The argument is familiar: the Prophet does not speak from desire; it is only revelation revealed; therefore the Prophet’s speech is revelation; therefore hadith are revelation. But that reading imports far more into the passage than the passage says.

The immediate context is the Prophet’s revelatory encounter and the message he recites. The passage defends the Messenger against the charge that he is astray or inventing the recitation. It does not say that every later report attributed to him, every paraphrased legal formulation, every remembered judgment, every personal instruction, and every narrator’s reconstruction of an event is *wahi* in the same sense as the Qurʾān.[^2]

There is also an internal Qurʾānic problem. The Qurʾān corrects the Prophet in several places. It asks why he permitted something before God made the matter clear, rebukes him in the episode of the blind man, challenges a restriction he imposed on himself, and denies that he has control over certain divine decisions.[^3]

<QuranVerse verse="9:43" />
<QuranVerse verse="80:1-10" />
<QuranVerse verse="66:1" />
<QuranVerse verse="3:128" />

These passages do not diminish the Prophet. They clarify his role. He is a messenger guided and corrected by God, not an independent source whose every human judgment is already identical with God’s revealed speech. If every prophetic utterance were automatically *wahi* in the same theological sense as Qurʾān, then the correction passages would lose their force. Correction assumes a difference between the Prophet’s human judgment before correction and God’s revealed judgment after correction.

<ContextNote title="The point is not anti-prophetic">
The argument does not deny the Prophet’s authority in his own lifetime. It denies a later category mistake: turning fallible reports about the Prophet into a second body of revelation with divine status.
</ContextNote>

## “Obey the Messenger” is not “canonize every report”

A second proof-text is the Qurʾānic command to obey the Messenger. This is a stronger argument than the careless use of Sūrat al-Najm, because the Qurʾān genuinely commands obedience to the Prophet. But it still does not prove that the hadith corpus is *wahi*.

<QuranVerse verse="4:59" />
<QuranVerse verse="59:7" />

There is a difference between obeying a living messenger and granting scriptural status to a later archive. The first is a direct Qurʾānic duty. The second requires a separate theory of preservation, attribution, wording, scope, contradiction, abrogation, and legal force. The Qurʾān can command obedience to the Messenger without telling later Muslims to treat every report graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by medieval specialists as divine revelation.

The logic becomes clearer if we put the categories side by side.

The traditional theory of *wahi ghayr matlūw*, “unrecited revelation,” emerged to solve this gap. It allows Sunnah or hadith to be treated as revelation while preserving the Qurʾān’s unique status as recited revelation. Modern scholars often describe this as part of the long process by which *sunna* became increasingly identified with hadith literature, especially after al-Shāfiʿī’s legal theory placed prophetic reports at the center of proof.[^4]

But that theory does not remove the logical problem. It relocates it. If hadith are unrecited revelation, then where is that revelation now? Is it the exact Arabic wording of each report? The meaning behind the wording? The compiler’s selected version? The jurist’s extracted ruling? The reconstructed common denominator across variants? Once the answer becomes “the meaning,” the claim has shifted from “this text is revelation” to “a fallible human process may have preserved a revealed meaning.” That is a much weaker claim.

## The Qurʾān treats its own wording as revelation, not merely its gist

The Qurʾān’s status depends on its revealed wording. Muslims do not recite paraphrases of al-Fātiḥa in prayer. They do not replace *qul huwa God aḥad* with a theologically equivalent sentence and call it Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. Meaning matters, but the Qurʾān’s revealed status is tied to the words themselves.

<QuranVerse verse="112:1-4" />

Consider the difference:

    <p>“Say: He is God, One.”</p>

    <p class="arabic">قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ الْوَاحِدُ</p>
    <p>“Say: He is God, the Unique One.”</p>

The second sentence may be true. It may convey a nearly identical theological idea. But it is not Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. It is not the revealed Qurʾānic wording. It cannot replace the revealed verse in prayer. Its truth does not make it scripture.

This is the heart of the hadith problem. If a hadith is admitted to be transmitted by meaning, then the surviving formulation is not the revealed form. If the wording is not the revealed form, then the hadith text before us is not *wahi*. At most it is a report that may convey a meaning thought to derive from prophetic guidance. Calling that “revelation” blurs the distinction between God’s speech and human paraphrase.

## Hadith theory itself allows narration by meaning

Classical hadith theory does not require every narrator to preserve the Prophet’s exact wording. The permission of *riwāya bi-l-maʿnā*, narration by meaning, is one of the most important admissions in the tradition’s own theory of transmission.

Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ explains the rule in the section on transmitting and performing hadith. If a narrator does not understand the words, their meanings, and the differences between expressions, he may not narrate by meaning. But if he is qualified, “the sounder view” allows it. Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ explicitly states that the Companions and early predecessors often transmitted one meaning in one matter with different wordings, because their reliance was on the meaning rather than the exact wording.[^5]

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
وَالْأَصَحُّ: جَوَازُ ذَلِكَ فِي الْجَمِيعِ، إِذَا كَانَ عَالِمًا بِمَا وَصَفْنَاهُ قَاطِعًا بِأَنَّهُ أَدَّى مَعْنَى اللَّفْظِ الَّذِي بَلَغَهُ؛ لِأَنَّ ذَلِكَ هُوَ الَّذِي تَشْهَدُ بِهِ أَحْوَالُ الصَّحَابَةِ وَالسَّلَفِ الْأَوَّلِينَ، وَكَثِيرًا مَا كَانُوا يَنْقُلُونَ مَعْنًى وَاحِدًا فِي أَمْرٍ وَاحِدٍ بِأَلْفَاظٍ مُخْتَلِفَةٍ، وَمَا ذَلِكَ إِلَّا لِأَنَّ مُعَوَّلَهُمْ كَانَ عَلَى الْمَعْنَى دُونَ اللَّفْظِ.
</div>

> “The sounder view is that this is permissible in all of it, if he is knowledgeable in the way we have described and certain that he has conveyed the meaning of the wording that reached him. This is what the practice of the Companions and the first predecessors bears witness to. They often transmitted one meaning in one matter with different wordings, and that was only because their reliance was on the meaning rather than the wording.”
>
> **Source:** Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ, *ʿUlūm al-ḥadīth*, type 26, on *riwāya bi-l-maʿnā*.
</HadithBlock>

Al-Suyūṭī gives the later summarized rule in *Tadrīb al-rāwī*: the majority of the early and later scholars allowed narration by meaning if the narrator was certain that he conveyed the meaning. He adds that the narrator should say something like “or as he said,” “or the like of it,” when there is uncertainty.[^6]

This is a devastating admission for the *wahi* claim. It means that the hadith system itself often authenticates a report without claiming that the exact wording is preserved. But revelation, in the Qurʾānic sense, is not merely a historian’s confidence that a meaning survived. It is God’s disclosed communication. If the surviving text is a narrator’s wording, then the surviving text is not God’s revelation.

<ContextNote title="The distinction traditional theory tries to avoid">
If the wording is prophetic but not divine, and later transmitters may replace even that prophetic wording with equivalent wording, then the final hadith text is two steps away from revelation: first from divine meaning to prophetic expression, then from prophetic expression to narrator paraphrase.
</ContextNote>

## Ibn Ḥajar’s practical admission: wide transmission produces variant wording

Ibn Ḥajar makes the same point in a different setting. While discussing comprehensive reports and the problem of identifying the Prophet’s exact concise wording, he notes that the way to know such wording is for a hadith’s routes to be few and its wording to agree. If the routes multiply, the wording rarely agrees, because most transmitters restrict themselves to narration by meaning according to what seems to them to preserve the intended sense.[^7]

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
والطريق إلى معرفة ذلك أن تَقِلَّ مخارج الحديث وتتفق ألفاظه، وإلا فإن مخارج الحديث إذا كثرت قلّ أن تتفق ألفاظه؛ لتوارد أكثر الرواة على الاقتصار على الرواية بالمعنى بحسب ما يظهر لأحدهم أنه واف به.
</div>

> “The way to know that is that the routes of the hadith be few and that its wordings agree. Otherwise, when the routes of a hadith are many, its wordings rarely agree, because most transmitters converge on limiting themselves to narration by meaning according to what appears to each of them to have conveyed it.”
>
> **Source:** Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī, cited from *Fatḥ al-Bārī* 13:250.
</HadithBlock>

This is not a hostile modern claim. It is a classical hadith-critical observation. Multiple routes do not necessarily preserve multiple identical copies of the Prophet’s wording. Often, they preserve multiple approximations of a remembered meaning. That may be acceptable for ordinary historical transmission. It is not acceptable for calling the surviving wording *wahi*.

## Even the corpus preserves a boundary around Qurʾān

One report in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* is especially important because it preserves a memory of documentary separation between Qurʾān and non-Qurʾānic material. Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī reports that the Prophet said:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لاَ تَكْتُبُوا عَنِّي، وَمَنْ كَتَبَ عَنِّي غَيْرَ الْقُرْآنِ فَلْيَمْحُهُ، وَحَدِّثُوا عَنِّي وَلاَ حَرَجَ، وَمَنْ كَذَبَ عَلَيَّ — قَالَ هَمَّامٌ: أَحْسِبُهُ قَالَ — مُتَعَمِّدًا، فَلْيَتَبَوَّأْ مَقْعَدَهُ مِنَ النَّارِ.
</div>

> “Do not write from me. Whoever wrote from me anything other than the Qurʾān, let him erase it. Narrate from me, and there is no harm. Whoever lies against me — Hammām said: I think he said ‘deliberately’ — let him take his seat in the Fire.”
>
> **Source:** Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 3004.
</HadithBlock>

Traditional scholars usually explain this report by saying that the Prophet temporarily forbade writing hadith to prevent confusion with the Qurʾān, then later allowed writing. That explanation may solve a legal question about writing. It does not erase the theological boundary preserved in the report. The Qurʾān is the thing that must not be confused with the Prophet’s other speech. Even within the hadith corpus, the Qurʾān is treated as a distinct written revelation whose documentary status differs from non-Qurʾānic reports.[^8]

Michael Cook’s study of early opposition to writing tradition is useful here. He shows that the anti-writing material was not a fantasy produced in an illiterate world. It belonged to a literate environment in which some transmitters still valued oral transmission and resisted turning traditions into fixed written texts. He describes the standard prophetic anti-writing report as a major tradition with a recognizable early transmission profile.[^9]

The point is not that all hadith-writing was permanently forbidden. The point is that early Muslims themselves knew there was a category difference between the Qurʾān and other transmitted material. The later doctrine of hadith as unrecited revelation tries to narrow that difference. The early documentary anxiety widens it.

## The women-leadership report as a test case

The strongest way to see the problem is to follow a concrete hadith across variants. The report about a people ruled by a woman is useful because it is famous, legally and politically consequential, and transmitted through recognizable routes from Abū Bakra.

Al-Bazzār, after citing one version, makes a revealing source-critical note:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
وَهَذَا الْحَدِيثُ قَدْ رُوِيَ عَنْ أَبِي بَكْرَةَ مِنْ وُجُوهٍ، وَلَا نَعْلَمُ رَوَاهُ غَيْرُ أَبِي بَكْرَةَ عَنِ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ.
</div>

> “This hadith has been narrated from Abū Bakra through several routes, and we do not know that anyone besides Abū Bakra narrated it from the Prophet.”
>
> **Source:** al-Bazzār, *Musnad*, no. 3113.
</HadithBlock>

That is already important. The report is not a public prophetic maxim preserved by many Companions. It is a bottlenecked Companion memory with several later routes. When we compare the wording, the report begins to behave exactly as hadith theory predicts: the meaning is relatively stable, but the wording and framing shift.

The report’s legal meaning is recognizable across the variants, but the exact form is not. The negation, verbs, syntax, framing, historical referent, dialogue, and political application shift across the tradition family. That does not automatically prove fabrication. It proves something narrower and more relevant: the hadith is not being transmitted as a fixed revealed text. It is being transmitted as a remembered meaning, adapted into different narrative frames.[^10]

That is enough to break the *wahi* claim. The issue is not whether the report is morally acceptable, legally valid, historically plausible, or politically weaponized. The issue here is textual status. A revealed text cannot be “the same revelation” when one version says Persia, another says Dhū Yazan, one says “they entrusted their affair,” another says “a woman rules them,” and another inserts a dialogue about succession. These may be narrations about an alleged prophetic statement. They are not a stable revealed utterance.

## If the meaning is revelation, who decides the meaning?

A defender may answer: “The wording is not revelation, but the meaning is revelation.” This is the usual escape route. But it creates a new problem: who determines the revealed meaning when the wordings differ?

In practice, the answer is not “God.” The answer is a human discipline: transmitter criticism, matn comparison, juristic preference, compiler selection, reconciliation, abrogation theory, and legal interpretation. The revealed meaning is reconstructed by scholars. That reconstruction may be careful. It may be pious. It may be intellectually impressive. But it is not identical with revelation.

This is why the word *wahi* becomes misleading. It gives divine certainty to a human inferential process. A hadith critic can say: “This report probably preserves a prophetic teaching.” A jurist can say: “This report is sufficient for legal action.” A theologian can say: “The Prophet was guided by God in his teaching.” But none of those statements logically entails: “This transmitted hadith text is divine revelation.”

## The āḥād problem: probability cannot become revelation by naming it revelation

A further problem is epistemological. Large parts of the hadith corpus are solitary or limited-channel reports, *akhbār al-āḥād*. Many classical legal theorists held that such reports yield probable knowledge, *ẓann*, while still being legally actionable. That is already a concession that the report’s epistemic status is not the same as the Qurʾān’s public recited certainty.[^11]

This creates a dilemma:

  <div class="dilemma-card">
    <h3>If hadith are probable reports</h3>
    <p>Then they may be used as historical or legal evidence by those who accept that method, but they are not revelation itself.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Traditionalists sometimes try to solve this by saying that sound reports can produce certainty when supported by external indicators, communal acceptance, or canonical placement. But that again confirms the point: the certainty is produced by a later scholarly apparatus. It is not identical with the original revelatory event. A report does not become *wahi* because a later community decides to treat it as reliable.

## Hadith qudsī does not solve the problem

The same logic applies to *ḥadīth qudsī*. These reports are introduced as words from God conveyed by the Prophet, but they remain hadith reports. They have chains, variants, gradings, and transmission histories. They are not recited as Qurʾān, not arranged in the muṣḥaf, not protected by the same liturgical and textual system, and not treated as Qurʾānic wording.

A *ḥadīth qudsī* may say “God said,” but the report that God said it is still a report. The historian still asks who transmitted it, through which route, with what wording, in what collection, and with what variants. If the wording differs, the same question returns: is the revelation the wording, the meaning, the compiler’s selection, or the later paraphrase?

<ContextNote title="The problem of quoted divine speech">
A report that claims to quote God is not automatically God’s speech. It is a humanly transmitted claim that God said something. That claim requires authentication before it can be believed, and authentication is not revelation.
</ContextNote>

## The Qurʾān’s own source boundary

The Qurʾān repeatedly presents itself as the revealed criterion, the detailed book, and the sufficient basis for judging religious claims. These verses do not merely say that the Qurʾān is important. They establish a boundary around what is being sent down as binding revelation.

<QuranVerse verse="6:114-115" />
<QuranVerse verse="45:6" />
<QuranVerse verse="77:50" />
<QuranVerse verse="7:3" />

The Qurʾān also warns against following conjecture in matters where one lacks knowledge.

<QuranVerse verse="17:36" />
<QuranVerse verse="10:36" />
<QuranVerse verse="53:28" />

A hadith-centered theologian may respond that authentic hadith are not conjecture because the hadith sciences establish their reliability. That response may work inside the hadith-theoretical system. It does not answer the logical problem. The hadith sciences are still human sciences. They evaluate narrators, compare chains, classify defects, and assign probabilities. They do not convert fallible transmission into divine speech.

The Qurʾān even warns the Prophet himself about attributing speech to God:

<QuranVerse verse="69:44-47" />

This passage makes sense only if there is a strict boundary between what is recited as God’s revelation and what is not. The Prophet is not free to place his own sayings into God’s mouth. Later transmitters are even less free to do so.

## The strongest version of the traditional view, and why it still fails

The strongest traditional view should be stated fairly:

> The Qurʾān is *wahi matlūw*, recited revelation. The Sunnah is *wahi ghayr matlūw*, unrecited revelation. The meanings of the Prophet’s binding teachings come from God, while the Qurʾān alone has the special status of inimitable recited wording.

This position is more sophisticated than saying every hadith wording is God’s literal speech. But it still faces four problems.

<div class="four-problems">
  <div>
    <strong>1. Category problem</strong>
    <p>It identifies God’s guidance to the Prophet with later reports about that guidance.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>2. Textual problem</strong>
    <p>Hadith wording is often transmitted by meaning, so the surviving text is not a revealed wording.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>3. Epistemic problem</strong>
    <p>Many reports rest on limited chains and yield probability, not public scriptural certainty.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>4. Interpretive problem</strong>
    <p>The “revealed meaning” must be reconstructed by jurists and hadith critics from conflicting forms.</p>
  </div>
</div>

The position can be made coherent only by reducing “revelation” to something like “a divine meaning that may lie behind some historically authenticated reports.” But that is not what people usually mean when they tell ordinary Muslims that hadith are *wahi*. They usually mean that hadith possess divine authority comparable to the Qurʾān. The evidence does not support that leap.

## A report about revelation is not revelation

This is the cleanest way to state the argument.

A report may be about revelation without being revelation. A historian may write that God revealed a verse; the historian’s sentence is not the verse. A Companion may report that the Prophet said something; the report is not the prophetic event itself. A compiler may preserve a chain; the compiler’s selected wording is not automatically the original wording. A jurist may extract a rule; the rule is not the revealed speech.

<section class="source-ladder" aria-label="Report about revelation ladder">
  <div class="ladder-step revelation">Revelation</div>
  <div class="ladder-note">God’s disclosed communication</div>
  <div class="ladder-step event">Prophetic event</div>
  <div class="ladder-note">the Prophet teaches, judges, acts, or explains</div>
  <div class="ladder-step memory">Memory</div>
  <div class="ladder-note">a Companion or Successor remembers and frames it</div>
  <div class="ladder-step report">Report</div>
  <div class="ladder-note">a chain and matn are transmitted, often by meaning</div>
  <div class="ladder-step doctrine">Doctrine</div>
  <div class="ladder-note">later scholars classify, reconcile, and legislate</div>
</section>

The traditional claim skips the middle of this ladder. It starts with revelation and ends with the hadith book. But the middle is the entire problem.

## What can still be granted

A precise argument should not overstate itself. It does not need to claim that every hadith is fabricated. It does not need to claim that the Prophet never gave binding instruction. It does not need to deny that early Muslims preserved valuable memories. It does not even need to deny that some hadith may preserve the Prophet’s teachings in substance.

The claim is narrower and stronger:

<ClaimBox title="The narrow thesis">
Even if a hadith preserves a genuine prophetic teaching, the hadith text as transmitted is still a human report. If it is transmitted by meaning, stabilized through variants, and authenticated by probabilistic scholarly judgment, it cannot logically be classified as divine revelation itself.
</ClaimBox>

That is why the *wahi* label is so dangerous. It transfers the certainty of God’s speech to the uncertainty of human transmission. It turns a report into a source. It turns a source into a scripture. Then it calls resistance to that move resistance to the Prophet.

The better distinction is this: the Qurʾān is revelation. The Prophet was a messenger guided by revelation. Hadith are later reports about the Prophet. Those reports may be studied, compared, criticized, and sometimes historically valued. But they are not *wahi*.

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">
Hadith cannot logically be treated as *wahi* in the Qurʾānic sense. The Qurʾān is fixed, recited revelation. Hadith are transmitted reports, often narrated by meaning, preserved through variant wordings, and authenticated by human judgment. The most that can be claimed is that some hadith may preserve traces of prophetic guidance. That does not make the hadith corpus a second revelation. A report about guidance is not the same thing as revelation from God.
</VerdictBox>
