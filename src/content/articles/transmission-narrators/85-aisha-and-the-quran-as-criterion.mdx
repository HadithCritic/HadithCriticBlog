The following is a compilation of reported episodes[^1], including thirteen distinct objections or interpretive challenges. The following compilation brings together nine episodes of Qur’anic scrutiny of attributed claims or rulings and two additional examples of her correcting interpretations of scripture. One episode (al-Bukhārī 4855) contains three distinct claims, yielding thirteen substantive questions across eleven episodes.

<ClaimBox title="The Qur’an as the test">Across the episodes collected here, ʿĀʾisha appeals to Qur’anic language when challenging reports, legal rulings, or scriptural interpretations. The evidence for each case must be judged on its own terms.</ClaimBox>

## When the dead supposedly suffer for somebody else’s crying



<QuranVerse verse="35:18" highlightEnglish={["No soul can carry the sins of another soul", "no other soul can carry any part of it"]} highlightArabic={["وَلَا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَى", "لَا يُحْمَلْ مِنْهُ شَىْءٌ"]} />

During a funeral in Mecca, Ibn ʿUmar invoked the Prophet's alleged instruction that a dead person is punished because relatives cry over him. Ibn ʿAbbās recalled ʿUmar repeating the same principle. When he subsequently told ʿĀʾisha, she disputed the formulation: “*By God, the Messenger of God did not say that God punishes a believer because his family weeps over him.*” She supplied a different account concerning the punishment of an unbeliever and concluded:[^2]

In the broad form, a person's suffering is caused by an action committed by someone else. ʿĀʾisha introduces the Qur’anic rule of individual responsibility as the standard against which that wording must be assessed. The transmitted name of ʿUmar does not make the objection disappear. What cannot be denied is that Āʾisha used the Qur’an to challenge the report as she received it.

## Did the dead at Badr hear the Prophet—or learn the truth?


A different correction concerns Muḥammad addressing the defeated Meccans at the well of Badr. Ibn ʿUmar's narration says that they could hear his words even after death. When this was reported to ʿĀʾisha, she corrected the verb:[^3]

<QuranVerse verse="35:22" highlightEnglish={["You cannot make hearers out of those in the graves"]} highlightArabic={["وَمَا أَنْتَ بِمُسْمِعٍ مَنْ فِي الْقُبُورِ"]} />
<HadithBlock label={"al-Bukhārī 3980 — report and ʿĀʾisha’s correction"} isnad={"ʿUthmān > ʿAbdah > Hishām > his father > Ibn ʿUmar"} translation={"The Prophet ﷺ stood at the well of Badr and said, ‘Have you found true what your Lord promised you?’ Then he said, ‘They now hear what I say.’ This was mentioned to ʿĀʾisha, and she said: ‘The Prophet ﷺ only said: “They now know that what I used to tell them was true.”’ Then she recited, ‘You cannot make the dead hear,’ through the end of the verse."} arabic={"حَدَّثَنِي عُثْمَانُ حَدَّثَنَا عَبْدَةُ عَنْ هِشَامٍ عَنْ أَبِيهِ عَنِ ابْنِ عُمَرَ ؓ قَالَ وَقَفَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ عَلَى قَلِيبِ بَدْرٍ فَقَالَ {هَلْ وَجَدْتُمْ مَا وَعَدَ رَبُّكُمْ حَقًّا ثُمَّ قَالَ إِنَّهُمُ الآنَ يَسْمَعُونَ مَا أَقُولُ} فَذُكِرَ لِعَائِشَةَ فَقَالَتْ إِنَّمَا قَالَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ إِنَّهُمُ الآنَ لَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّ الَّذِي كُنْتُ أَقُولُ لَهُمْ هُوَ الْحَقُّ ثُمَّ قَرَأَتْ {إِنَّكَ لَا تُسْمِعُ الْمَوْتَى} حَتَّى قَرَأَتِ الْآيَةَ"} source={"Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, no. 3980"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/64/8"} highlightEnglish={["They now hear what I say", "They now know that what I used to tell them was true"]} highlightArabic={["إِنَّهُمُ الآنَ يَسْمَعُونَ مَا أَقُولُ", "إِنَّهُمُ الآنَ لَيَعْلَمُونَ أَنَّ الَّذِي كُنْتُ أَقُولُ لَهُمْ هُوَ الْحَقُّ"]} />

The disagreement is extraordinarily narrow but consequential: yasmaʿūna (they hear) versus yaʿlamūna (they know). Her correction denies a particular assertion about the dead's ability to hear, and the Qur’anic verse explains why she resists that assertion. A separate report from Anas contains the stronger claim that the dead heard the Prophet's speech. The existence of that parallel complicates attempts to declare the historical issue resolved merely by quoting ʿĀʾisha.

## Abū Hurayra, unlucky women, and a missing quotation mark



According to Musnad Aḥmad 26088[^4], two men informed ʿĀʾisha that Abū Hurayra narrated the Prophet saying bad omens reside in a woman, a riding animal, and a house. She became angry:

“*By the One who sent down the Qur’an to Abū al-Qāsim, that is not how he used to say it!*”

Her corrected version restored the missing frame. The Prophet was reporting that people of the pre-Islamic period said such things. She then recited:

This is not a disagreement over a small legal qualification, it’s about attribution. “*People used to believe X*” and “*the Prophet taught X*” are opposites when X is a superstition. In her account, a reported belief was transformed into the Prophet's own teaching because the introductory clause had been omitted.[^5]

One encounter in al-Bukhārī 4855[^6] supplies three distinct denials, each backed by the Qur’an. Masrūq asked whether the Prophet had seen his Lord. ʿĀʾisha was alarmed and replied that anyone transmitting the following claims was speaking falsely.

First: the assertion that Muḥammad saw God. She read 6:103 and 42:51, which describes revelation, speech from behind a barrier, or a messenger. She then distinguished the report of a vision of God from the claim that Muḥammad saw Gabriel in his true form.

Second: the assertion that Muḥammad possessed knowledge of tomorrow. She read 31:34. The claim is refuted outright by calling out the verse that contradicts Masrūq’s assertion.

Third: the assertion that Muḥammad concealed part of revelation. She recited 5:67, ordering the messenger to proclaim what had been revealed to him. The significance lies in the arrangement: claim → verse → rejection, repeated three times.

The last disagreement concerns the reading and interpretation of 12:110. Ibn ʿAbbās read *kudhibū* without a doubled consonant. When his view was relayed to ʿĀʾisha, she rejected the implication that the messengers might have thought God’s promise false. This case is about a Qur’anic reading and its implication.

## Companion authority was not immunity from the Qur’an


The most direct example remains the funeral dispute. A saying was associated with ʿUmar and Ibn ʿUmar; ʿĀʾisha denied its unrestricted form and replied, “*The Qur’an is sufficient for you.*” The Badr narration presents the same principle at the scale of a single verb. The omen account exposes what happens when a quoted belief is stripped of its attribution. And al-Bukhārī 4855 preserves three categorical denials, each anchored in a separate verse.

The additional accounts extend the point beyond those famous disputes. The child of adultery bears no inherited guilt. A question about predatory animals is answered by reciting God's stated prohibitions. A question about temporary marriage is referred explicitly to the Book. Abū Hurayra's fasting ruling yields to a Qur’anic appeal. Āʾisha is repeatedly portrayed as treating the reliability or prestige of a narrator and the acceptability of a reported claim as different questions. A Companion could transmit something sincerely and still convey an incomplete, misremembered, or misconstrued statement. The matter was not over simply because a hadith was recited, even if it came from a trusted companion like the likes of Umar ibn al khattab or ABu Hurairah. The lesson that should be extracted from all of these examples is how despite all of the lived experience around her from companions that sat down with, ate with, and spoken with the prophet, the prime example is how these narratvies consistently placed the QUran as the arbitrator of it all \--- regardless of how trustworthy any of these companions could have been.

<VerdictBox title="Methodological conclusion">These reports depict ʿĀʾisha testing transmitted claims against Qur’anic passages, without requiring each episode to be treated as identical in transmission strength or context.</VerdictBox>
