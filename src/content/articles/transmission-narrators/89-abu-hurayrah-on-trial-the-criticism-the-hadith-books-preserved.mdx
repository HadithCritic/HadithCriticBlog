*What do we do when the traditions used to establish a narrator's authority preserve an extensive record of people questioning that very narrator?*

The scene is peculiar. A man repeatedly attributes statements to Muḥammad. His audience questions him. He strikes his forehead in frustration and responds: **“Do you claim that I lie about God's Messenger?”**[^1] This man is Abū Hurayrah, the companion to whom later hadith books attribute more traditions than to any other companion. The exchange is not preserved in a modern attack on Sunnī Islam. It appears in Sunnī hadith collections—including Muslim, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, and al-Bukhārī's *al-Adab al-Mufrad*.

And it is not the only dispute. The transmitted literature remembers ʿĀʾishah correcting the content of his reports, ʿUmar trying to restrain his narrations, a cluster of Kufan scholars declining to use some of his legal traditions, and Shuʿbah describing his indirect transmission as *tadlīs*. It also remembers people defending him. The two bodies of evidence must be read together.

<ClaimBox title="The central finding" facts={[{value:'10',label:'Digital Arabic books reviewed'},{value:'97',label:'Source excerpts indexed'},{value:'1',label:'Question: which claims survive scrutiny?'}]}>

**The history of Abū Hurayrah's authority is not a history of universal, unquestioning acceptance.** The same textual tradition that later elevated his reliability preserves challenges, corrections, retractions, and selective rejection. Those records undermine *automatic certainty* in any particular attribution. They do **not**, without further investigation, prove that every accusation against him was true or that every report attributed to him was fabricated.

</ClaimBox>

## An argument that begins with the wrong number

Two earlier Quran Talk articles called attention to Abū Hurayrah's prolific narration and to accusations made against him.[^2] Their question deserves a much larger historical inquiry. But the familiar figure of **5,374 hadiths** must be understood correctly. It is a traditional tally of reports *attributed* to him, not 5,374 unique, independently verified encounters at which he heard Muḥammad speak. A narrated item may appear repeatedly with different chains or within different compilations. Without a defined and deduplicated corpus, the popular claim that Abū Hurayrah personally accounts for some exact percentage of “all hadith” is not a valid measurement.

Why does the number matter at all? Because Abū Hurayrah's narrations occupied enough of early Islamic religious memory that contemporaries—or transmitters speaking in their names—offered explanations for why he related so much more than some longer-serving companions.

<HadithBlock label="Abū Hurayrah's own defense" source="Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2492d; compare Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2350" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim/44/35" arabic="إِنَّكُمْ تَقُولُونَ إِنَّ أَبَا هُرَيْرَةَ يُكْثِرُ الْحَدِيثَ عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ" translation="You say that Abū Hurayrah narrates excessively from God's Messenger." />

His explanation is that the Emigrants were busy in the marketplace, the Helpers were busy managing property, and he remained near Muḥammad, remembering what others missed. This is a **defense preserved within a narrated tradition**, not independent proof of flawless memory. The proper question is whether particular reports can be corroborated and accurately traced.[^3]

## I. “Do you claim that I lie?” — an accusation in his own reported words

The report begins with an encounter, not a later commentator's verdict. Abū Razīn says he saw Abū Hurayrah striking his forehead and addressing the people of Iraq:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 1 — the Iraqi accusation" source="Al-Bukhārī, al-Adab al-Mufrad 956; al-Nasāʾī 5370; compare Muslim 2098a" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/adab/41/3" arabic="يَا أَهْلَ الْعِرَاقِ أَتَزْعُمُونَ أَنِّي أَكْذِبُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ أَيَكُونُ لَكُمُ الْمَهْنَأُ وَعَلَيَّ الْمَأْثَمُ؟" translation="People of Iraq, do you claim that I lie about God's Messenger? Would the benefit be yours and the sin mine?" />

The meaning of the complaint is plain: the surviving account represents Abū Hurayrah as confronting people who believed he falsely attributed sayings to Muḥammad. Yet that does not tell us *which individuals* made the charge, why they made it, or whether it was justified. It is a representation of a dispute, not an acquittal or conviction.

### Why several collections are not several independent witnesses

The texts also supply a lesson about the transmission of criticism itself. In the versions preserved by al-Bukhārī's *al-Adab al-Mufrad*, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, and Aḥmad, the account commonly moves from Abū Hurayrah to Abū Razīn, from Abū Razīn to al-Aʿmash, and from al-Aʿmash to Abū Muʿāwiyah before appearing through multiple collectors.[^4] Muslim 2098a has a related Abū Razīn → al-Aʿmash line through **Ibn Idrīs**, illustrating an additional downstream route that must be kept distinct from Abū Muʿāwiyah's.

There are also two different sayings attached to the public protest. In one group he cites the prohibition on walking in only one sandal when its strap breaks; in Ibn Mājah's recension he gives an instruction about washing a vessel after a dog licks it. The accusation frame stays recognizable while the illustrative prophetic saying changes.

We should not count five recensions as five separate occasions on which Abū Hurayrah was accused of lying. Nor should we ignore the fact that their framings disagree at the point where he supplies evidence for his reliability. A historian can establish a transmitted controversy here while leaving the exact shape of the original exchange uncertain.

### Ṭalḥah is asked whether Abū Hurayrah attributed words falsely—and defends him

The question is preserved independently in form in al-Kaʿbī's *Qubūl al-Akhbār*, 1:175. A questioner asks Ṭalḥah why Abū Hurayrah narrates things other companions do not hear: did he hear what they missed, or did he attribute words to Muḥammad which Muḥammad never spoke?[^5]

<QuoteBlock author="Question put to Ṭalḥah" source="al-Kaʿbī, Qubūl al-Akhbār, 1:175 (book ID 45053)">

<Arabic>أَمْ هُوَ يَقُولُ عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ مَا لَمْ يَقُلْ؟</Arabic>

“Or does he say things about God's Messenger that he did not say?”

</QuoteBlock>

Ṭalḥah's answer **defends** Abū Hurayrah: the others had households, flocks, and work, whereas Abū Hurayrah remained close to Muḥammad. Quoting the accusation while concealing the defense would distort the report. The historical point is that *the question was imaginable and narratable*, not that Ṭalḥah himself found Abū Hurayrah guilty.

## II. ʿĀʾishah's corrections — where the content, not the chain, was contested

Al-Zarkashī's *al-Ijābah li-Īrād mā Istadrakathu ʿĀʾishah ʿalā al-Ṣaḥābah* contains an exceptionally useful concentrated discussion (uploaded book **6032**, pp. 102–120). He arranges multiple objections attributed to ʿĀʾishah, incorporating different isnāds, earlier compilations, and notes on their weaknesses. **Eleven headings do not equal eleven secure eyewitness confrontations.** Some are strong, some are poorly transmitted, and some concern a doctrine independently associated with several companions.[^6]

### Case 1. The fasting ruling that the account says he withdrew

This is the most valuable episode because it has a beginning, a check by eyewitnesses, and a reported change of position. Abū Hurayrah declares that a person who wakes in a state of *janābah* should not fast. Abū Bakr b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān brings the issue to ʿĀʾishah and Umm Salamah, who knew Muḥammad's domestic practice directly. Both say Muḥammad sometimes began the morning in that state, performed the necessary washing, and continued his fast. Marwān then directs that their testimony be conveyed to Abū Hurayrah.[^7]

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 2 — disputed rule" source="al-Ṭaḥāwī, Sharḥ Mushkil al-Āthār 2:13–20; al-Zarkashī 1:102–103; Muslim 1109" url="https://sunnah.com/muslim:1109a" arabic="مَنْ أَدْرَكَهُ الْفَجْرُ جُنُبًا فَلَا صَوْمَ لَهُ" translation="Whoever reaches dawn in a state of major ritual impurity has no valid fast." />

In the account's continuation he attributes his information to another companion, **al-Faḍl b. ʿAbbās**, and acknowledges that the two wives know the practice better. One recension states explicitly:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 3 — correction and retraction" source="al-Ṭaḥāwī, Sharḥ Mushkil al-Āthār, 2:20 (book ID 2866)" arabic="فَرَدَّ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ فُتْيَاهُ" translation="So Abū Hurayrah withdrew his legal opinion." />

The reports differ in details, and the better-attested version names ʿĀʾishah and **Umm Salamah**, not Ḥafṣah. We should not harmonize these variations silently. But across the important versions the issue is concrete: a narrator had supplied a normative rule, witnesses challenged his report from directly known practice, and a recension explicitly says he retracted it.

**Why it matters:** This establishes at least a transmitted instance of fallible legal narration and *indirect hearing*. It does not establish that he knew the rule was false when he first reported it. That is a different allegation requiring different evidence.

### Case 2. When the beginning of a statement disappears

Another report has Abū Hurayrah transmit that bad omens occur in a woman, a house, and a horse. ʿĀʾishah's response is that he failed to retain the beginning of the statement. On one strand, Muḥammad had been describing what **Jews said**; another strand makes it the saying of **people in the pre-Islamic period**.[^8]

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 4 — an allegedly lost framing clause" source="al-Zarkashī, al-Ijābah, 1:104–106; Aḥmad 26088; compare Ibn Qutaybah, p. 170" url="https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad/15.01" arabic="وَالَّذِي أَنْزَلَ الْقُرْآنَ عَلَى أَبِي الْقَاسِمِ مَا هَكَذَا كَانَ يَقُولُ" translation="By the One who sent down the Qurʾān upon Abū al-Qāsim, that is not how he used to say it!" />

There is a vast difference between these two propositions:

**Without context:** “There are bad omens in a woman, a house, and a horse.”

**With the disputed context restored:** “The people of the pre-Islamic period [or, in another version, the Jews] used to say there are bad omens in a woman, a house, and a horse.”

The first appears to endorse the proposition; the second describes a belief held by other people. In one wording ʿĀʾishah adds that Abū Hurayrah **did not remember** its opening: <Arabic>لَمْ يَحْفَظْ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ</Arabic>. She then cites the Qurʾān to rebut the apparent superstition.

<QuranPassage verse="57:22">
  <Fragment slot="translation">"No disaster strikes on earth or in yourselves without its being recorded before We bring it into existence…" (excerpt)</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="arabic"><Arabic>مَا أَصَابَ مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِي أَنفُسِكُمْ إِلَّا فِي كِتَابٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن نَّبْرَأَهَا</Arabic></Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

This is the point at which a narrator's supposed good character ceases to settle the issue. Even a trustworthy transmitter can reproduce a sentence accurately **from the wrong point in a conversation**. The missing opening reverses its import.

<ContextNote title="A complication that cannot be omitted" collapsible={true}>

Related versions of the bad-omen saying circulate through **Ibn ʿUmar**, not only Abū Hurayrah (for example, Muslim 2225a and al-Bukhārī 5093). That evidence means a criticism of one Abū Hurayrah recension is not, by itself, an explanation of the entire saying. Nor should the different “Jewish belief” and “pre-Islamic belief” corrections be collapsed into a single original scene without isnād analysis.

</ContextNote>

### Case 3. “The child of adultery is the worst of the three” — when the Qurʾān becomes the criterion

A maxim transmitted in Abū Hurayrah's name calls the child born of adultery the “worst of the three.” The objection recorded in ʿĀʾishah's name is straightforward: **the child cannot inherit the moral culpability of its parents**. The Qurʾān says otherwise.[^9]

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 5 — the disputed maxim" source="Abū Dāwūd 3963; al-Ṭaḥāwī, 2:367; al-Zarkashī, 1:108–110" url="https://sunnah.com/abudawud:3963" arabic="وَلَدُ الزِّنَا شَرُّ الثَّلَاثَةِ" translation="The child of adultery is the worst of the three." />

<QuranPassage verse="6:164">
  <Fragment slot="translation">"No bearer of burdens bears the burden of another." (excerpt)</Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="arabic"><Arabic>وَلَا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَى</Arabic></Fragment>
</QuranPassage>

The longer corrective report argues that Muḥammad said this of a **particular offender** and that the specific context was later universalized. One wording makes ʿĀʾishah say <Arabic>أَسَاءَ سَمْعًا فَأَسَاءَ إِجَابَةً</Arabic>—that he heard badly and thereby reported badly.[^10]

Yet here the evidence has **two levels**. The independently transmitted objection to blaming the child is stronger than the extended scene in which ʿĀʾishah directly confronts Abū Hurayrah over multiple hadith. The latter contains contested links and cannot inherit the former's reliability simply because the narrative is more detailed. We must not convert two different source traditions into one supposedly authenticated confrontation.

This case also has an illuminating parallel through al-Shaʿbī: if the child really were the worst of the three, why would the pregnant woman's punishment be postponed until she gave birth? The argument challenges the same generalized maxim without necessarily making a direct accusation against Abū Hurayrah.[^11]

### Case 4. Did a dead person suffer *because* somebody cried?

The formula <Arabic>إِنَّ الْمَيِّتَ لَيُعَذَّبُ بِبُكَاءِ الْحَيِّ</Arabic> (“the deceased is punished because the living weep”) also draws ʿĀʾishah's objection. In one corrective explanation, Muḥammad had observed a deceased Jewish person already being punished while relatives cried. The generalized report allegedly transformed simultaneity into causality.[^12]

A separate version in Abū Yaʿlā 1592 ends with an unnamed man exclaiming <Arabic>صَدَقَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَكَذَبَ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ</Arabic> (“God's Messenger spoke truthfully, and Abū Hurayrah was mistaken/has lied”). This is an explicit report of a dispute, but its grading is not settled, and *kadhaba* can be used for error as well as deliberate fabrication.[^13]

The moral standard behind ʿĀʾishah's objection is intelligible: why would one person's conduct punish somebody else? This also connects to <ArticleLink id="transmission-narrators/86-aisha-and-the-quran-as-criterion">ʿĀʾishah's recorded use of the Qurʾān as a criterion</ArticleLink>, especially in the stronger Ibn ʿUmar mourning dispute.

### Case 5. Other corrections preserved by al-Zarkashī

The less frequently cited objections should not disappear simply because their isnāds are weaker. They reveal the **range** of controversies later authors attributed to ʿĀʾishah, and they offer future candidates for independent investigation:

**Carrying a deceased person.** When told that handling a corpse requires washing or ablution, she asks: <Arabic>أَوَنَجِسٌ مَوْتَى الْمُسْلِمِينَ؟</Arabic>—“Are deceased Muslims impure?” Al-Zarkashī 1:113–115 also discusses the weakness of elevated routes.[^14]

**The status of witr.** An account attributes to Abū Hurayrah the claim that whoever omits witr has no valid prayer; ʿĀʾishah is reported to reject making it a sixth obligatory prayer. The linkage to Abū Hurayrah requires its own chain assessment (al-Zarkashī 1:112).[^15]

**Poetry.** She reportedly says he omitted a qualifier concerning poetry that *attacks* Muḥammad from a maxim condemning filling oneself with verse. Al-Zarkashī 1:115 preserves the report through a route involving al-Kalbī; this is not a secure foundation for an eyewitness claim.[^16]

**Meeting God.** She reportedly says he recited the **end** of a prophetic statement concerning the believer's wish to meet God while omitting the explanatory **beginning**. Al-Zarkashī 1:118 notes a restricted route.[^17]

**Woman passing during prayer.** Her direct testimony about Muḥammad praying in her presence contests the idea that a woman's mere presence necessarily breaks prayer. But ʿĀʾishah's independently attested reply should not be equated uncritically with a stronger claim that she personally accused Abū Hurayrah of invention. Al-Zarkashī 1:119 names him explicitly; the familiar Muslim accounts do not always frame her response that way.[^18]

**Walking in one sandal.** A story has ʿĀʾishah deliberately refuse to follow a single-sandal rule associated with Abū Hurayrah. This is evidence of *reported resistance to a ruling*, not an adjudicated finding that the narration was fabricated (al-Kaʿbī 1:177; al-Zarkashī 1:120).[^19]

**The woman punished over a cat.** Another longer report has her dispute Abū Hurayrah's account on the ground that a relevant condition was omitted. This is a reported **contextual correction** and should be treated according to the strength of its own transmission, not combined with all the other examples as equally secure (al-Zarkashī 1:108).[^20]

<ContextNote title="What a compilation of corrections can—and cannot—prove" collapsible={true}>

Al-Zarkashī wrote many centuries after these reported encounters. He is a **valuable collector**, not an eyewitness. An incident in his compilation should be traced to the earliest recoverable transmitting work; variants sharing ʿUrwah, al-Zuhrī, al-Aʿmash, or another common early transmitter must be compared before a number of genuinely independent routes is claimed.

</ContextNote>

## III. ʿUmar: controlling narration, not simply an allegation of lying

A report circulating through al-Sāʾib b. Yazīd has ʿUmar issue a direct warning to Abū Hurayrah:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 6 — the warning" source="al-Dhahabī, Siyar Aʿlām al-Nubalāʾ, 2:600–601; Abū Zurʿah al-Dimashqī, Tārīkh, no. 1475" arabic="لَتَتْرُكَنَّ الْحَدِيثَ عَنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ أَوْ لَأُلْحِقَنَّكَ بِأَرْضِ دَوْسٍ" translation="You will stop narrating from God's Messenger, or I shall send you back to the land of Daws." />

In the same setting, a warning to **Kaʿb al-Aḥbār** mentions the “land of the monkeys.” The addressees and places must not be interchanged. Later retellings sometimes merge them.[^21]

A separate account in al-Kaʿbī (1:177–182) says Abū Hurayrah feared that ʿUmar would physically discipline him if he narrated certain traditions during his rule. One such route involves Ibn ʿAjlān, who was born too late to have witnessed Abū Hurayrah speak. It is accordingly less secure evidence of Abū Hurayrah's own words than a continuous route would be.[^22]

**What does ʿUmar's intervention show?** In the extant narrative, he did not view unlimited narration as necessarily harmless. That may reflect a policy of restriction, concern about excessive reports, fear of mistaken attribution, or a combination of motives. It does *not* establish that ʿUmar proved Abū Hurayrah was a deliberate forger.

### A separate financial allegation—with an ending often omitted

During his service in Bahrain, Abū Hurayrah is accused of improperly acquiring a substantial sum. The report in al-Dhahabī 2:612–613 depicts ʿUmar demanding an explanation. Abū Hurayrah attributes the wealth to legitimate sources, and the transmitted account says the matter was investigated and **his explanation was accepted**; ʿUmar later offered him another post.[^23]

Any article that quotes the accusation but omits the reported exoneration is using only half the document. A source-critical presentation must keep the entire episode together.

## IV. Ibn ʿUmar: suspicion, correction, and sometimes verification *in Abū Hurayrah's favor*

The juristic question of witr provides an unusually direct wording. In al-Kaʿbī 1:184 and in later legal discussions, a man reports Abū Hurayrah's position on the prayer and Ibn ʿUmar responds:

<QuoteBlock author="Statement attributed to Ibn ʿUmar" source="al-Kaʿbī, Qubūl al-Akhbār, 1:184 (compare transmitted al-Marwazī/al-Nasāʾī routes)">

<Arabic>كَذَبَ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ</Arabic>

“Abū Hurayrah was wrong” / “Abū Hurayrah lied.”

</QuoteBlock>

The Arabic wording is severe, but translation is not interpretation. In classical Arabic, *kadhaba* in a particular context can mean “reported incorrectly,” without establishing a conscious intention to deceive. The competing witr versions and Ibn ʿUmar's legal standpoint must be collated before drawing a stronger psychological claim.[^24]

Another report is more ambiguous. When told Abū Hurayrah included farm dogs among exceptions to a rule about keeping dogs, Ibn ʿUmar replied that Abū Hurayrah **had farmland** (Muslim 1571). That comment may reflect skepticism about a convenient addition, or it may simply explain why Abū Hurayrah remembered an agricultural exception.[^25]

Most revealingly, Ibn ʿUmar challenges Abū Hurayrah on the reward for attending a funeral. Abū Hurayrah takes the matter to ʿĀʾishah, who confirms him. Ibn ʿUmar then accepts the statement and regrets the reward he has missed. The transmitted episode shows **actual verification, followed by acceptance**—not universal disbelief.[^26]

The reported contrast is therefore not between a heroic infallible Ibn ʿUmar and an always-rejected Abū Hurayrah. It is between a practice of **testing claims** and a later temptation to treat narrator status as a substitute for testing them.

## V. The early Kufans did not treat all his reports alike

The Kufan material is perhaps the most important collective evidence. In one version Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, representing students of Ibn Masʿūd, says:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 7 — selective acceptance" source="al-Kaʿbī, Qubūl al-Akhbār, 1:177–178; al-Dhahabī, Siyar, 2:608–609" arabic="كَانُوا يَأْخُذُونَ مِنْ حَدِيثِ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ وَيَدَعُونَ" translation="They would accept some of Abū Hurayrah's hadith and leave some aside." />

Another version gives the stronger formulation that they accepted from his traditions only those concerning Paradise or Hell:

<QuoteBlock author="Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, as transmitted through Manṣūr" source="al-Dhahabī, Siyar, 2:609 (book ID 598)">

<Arabic>مَا كَانُوا يَأْخُذُونَ مِنْ حَدِيثِ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ إِلَّا مَا كَانَ حَدِيثَ جَنَّةٍ أَوْ نَارٍ</Arabic>

“They did not take from Abū Hurayrah's reports except those concerning Paradise or Hell.”

</QuoteBlock>

Al-Dhahabī **rejects** that sweeping characterization, pointing to the long-standing practice of Muslims citing Abū Hurayrah's reports. The difference between selective rejection and a total exclusion from legal reasoning therefore remains contested even within the sources transmitting this statement.[^27]

Two additional narratives bring the issue into focus. In one, followers of Ibn Masʿūd are told a rule about washing the hands before putting them into a vessel after awakening, then ask how Abū Hurayrah would apply this to the large communal stone basin in Medina. This is a practical objection, not a rigorous identification of a fabricated isnād.[^28] In another, someone cites an Abū Hurayrah hadith to Muḥammad al-Shaybānī, who allegedly dismisses it with the curt reply:

<QuoteBlock author="Muḥammad al-Shaybānī (as reported by Abū ʿUbayd)" source="al-Kaʿbī, Qubūl al-Akhbār, 1:177">

<Arabic>إِنَّهُ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ</Arabic>

“But it is Abū Hurayrah.”

</QuoteBlock>

That is a striking polemical fragment; it is **not proof that al-Shaybānī universally rejected every narration from him**. Recovering the incident in earlier textual witnesses—and identifying the specific disputed rule—is necessary.[^29]

The key inference is narrower and more historically defensible than “all early Ḥanafīs considered him a liar.” **At least one transmitted Kufan tradition depicts the acceptance of Abū Hurayrah's legal narrations as selective and disputable.** That requires analysis of the individual rules and pupils' networks, not a backward projection of later madhhab doctrines.

## VI. Did he always hear the material directly from Muḥammad?

Even when the issue is not intentional fabrication, an omitted intermediary matters. The fasting dispute has Abū Hurayrah admit that a report came through al-Faḍl. Shuʿbah, as quoted by Yazīd b. Hārūn, is also reported to have said:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 8 — the tadlīs accusation" source="al-Dhahabī, Siyar Aʿlām al-Nubalāʾ, 2:608; al-Kaʿbī 2:402" arabic="كَانَ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ يُدَلِّسُ" translation="Abū Hurayrah practiced tadlīs." />

Al-Dhahabī immediately responds that companions often transmitted indirectly from other companions and that their collective reliability makes this unobjectionable. Notice the shift: **the objection concerns the visibility of an intermediary**; the response rests on a doctrine of collective uprightness. Even if the omitted person was in fact a companion, an unnamed intermediary is still an inference requiring evidence when the goal is historical reconstruction.[^30]

### When later listeners may have mixed statements of Kaʿb with those of Muḥammad

A report ascribed to Busr b. Saʿīd states that some people hearing Abū Hurayrah narrate both prophetic statements and teachings from Kaʿb al-Aḥbār mixed up their attributions when repeating them. On the account's own wording, **the listeners**, rather than necessarily Abū Hurayrah himself, generate the confusion.[^31]

That distinction matters greatly. It is possible for erroneous prophetic attribution to arise at several points: an original speaker may misremember a source; a pupil may omit an intermediary; a later listener may combine two reports; or a compiler may select one recension. Finding a statement wrongly elevated to Muḥammad does not automatically identify who first elevated it.

A potential example is the seven-day creation report in Muslim 2789, which al-Bukhārī's *al-Tārīkh al-Kabīr* reportedly preferred in a route from Abū Hurayrah **through Kaʿb**, rather than directly from Muḥammad. That comparison lies partly outside the ten books supplied for this project; it remains a priority for a standalone isnād-cum-matn reconstruction, not a solved attributional fraud here.[^32]

### A memory dispute survives in Muslim itself

Abū Hurayrah is also reported to have repeated a statement denying contagion and another forbidding the mixing of sick and healthy livestock. Later he reportedly ceased repeating the first, and al-Ḥārith b. Abī Dhubāb confronted him about the earlier narration. The transmitted commentator asks whether Abū Hurayrah had forgotten or another reason accounted for the change (Muslim 2221; al-Ṭaḥāwī 4:349).[^33]

This does not establish a fraudulent intention. It does establish the **kind of remembered inconsistency** that source critics must address rather than suppressing with an undifferentiated claim of infallible memory.

### Bukhārī 5355: a claimed “confession” that needs to be read correctly

The 2025 Quran Talk article cites Bukhārī 5355, in which Abū Hurayrah attributes a statement about giving charity and supporting dependents to the Prophet, then adds practical examples about a wife, servant, and child. When asked whether he heard **those details** from Muḥammad, he answers:

<HadithBlock label="Exhibit 9 — explicitly marked personal elaboration" source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5355" url="https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5355" arabic="قَالُوا يَا أَبَا هُرَيْرَةَ سَمِعْتَ هَذَا مِنْ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ؟ قَالَ لَا هَذَا مِنْ كِيسِ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ" translation="They said, 'Abū Hurayrah, did you hear this from God's Messenger?' He said, 'No. This is from Abū Hurayrah's own store [his own words].'" />

This is important, but the conclusion must be exact. The report **distinguishes the opening prophetic maxim from a following elaboration explicitly identified as Abū Hurayrah's own**. It does not say he acknowledged fabricating the preceding prophetic sentence. The same source can be used to study the risk of *mudraj* (interpolated commentary) without being misrepresented as a confession to inventing a hadith.[^34]

## VII. The accusations about his relationship with Umayyad power

There is one clear political fact **inside the transmitted record**: Marwān appointed Abū Hurayrah to administer Medina during his own absence. Muslim preserves the episode in connection with a Friday prayer led by Abū Hurayrah (Muslim 877). Al-Dhahabī also transmits accounts connecting Abū Hurayrah with governance under Muʿāwiyah.[^35]

Political proximity matters when assessing possible interests and access. But **holding a position does not prove that a specific narration was invented to gain favor**. The more severe claims come through later polemical transmission, particularly Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd's quotations from Abū Jaʿfar al-Iskāfī.

### What al-Iskāfī alleged

In *Sharḥ Nahj al-Balāghah*, uploaded edition pp. 1012–1016 (book ID **18072**), Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd reproduces allegations that Muʿāwiyah rewarded transmitters of anti-ʿAlid traditions. Abū Hurayrah appears among those accused. One story claims he recited a report about the sanctity of Medina, asserted that ʿAlī had violated the sanctuary, and subsequently received favor or an appointment from Muʿāwiyah.[^36]

<ContextNote title="Levels of attribution: keep them visible" collapsible={false}>

**This text proves that Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd transmitted an accusation ascribed to al-Iskāfī.** It does not independently prove a payment or a fabricated statement in Muʿāwiyah's time. To establish that, one must find the earlier source, date the tradition, assess the named transmitting chain, and test whether a route independent of the later polemical account survives. Al-Muʿallimī disputes the attribution and evidence in *al-Anwār al-Kāshifah*, pp. 209–211 (book ID **6448**).[^37]

</ContextNote>

The strongest insult attributed to ʿAlī is preserved in the same later compilation:

<QuoteBlock author="Attributed to ʿAlī, without a complete chain here" source="Ibn Abī al-Ḥadīd, Sharḥ Nahj al-Balāghah, uploaded edition p. 1016">

<Arabic>وَقَدْ رُوِيَ عَنْ عَلِيٍّ ... أَكْذَبُ النَّاسِ ... عَلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ... أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ الدَّوْسِيُّ</Arabic>

“It has been narrated from ʿAlī ... that the greatest liar concerning God's Messenger ... was Abū Hurayrah al-Dawsī.”

</QuoteBlock>

The prefatory words **<Arabic>وَقَدْ رُوِيَ عَنْ عَلِيٍّ</Arabic>** are not disposable. They mean “it has been narrated from ʿAlī,” *not* “ʿAlī is securely documented as having said this.” Readers deserve the difference. The same applies to an anecdote in this passage presenting Abū Ḥanīfah as excluding Abū Hurayrah from the usual presumption of companion reliability: the earliest traceable version and its attribution to Abū Yūsuf require further verification.[^38]

### A counterexample in the same biographical tradition

Al-Dhahabī preserves an account of Abū Hurayrah openly challenging Marwān, accusing him of acting to please Muʿāwiyah during a conflict over al-Ḥasan's burial. It is evidence that the later literature did not exclusively remember him as a compliant Umayyad client. It, too, must be critically transmitted, but a serious historical narrative cannot treat only the anti-ʿAlid accusations as evidence while discarding inconvenient opposite material.[^39]

## VIII. The critics of Abū Hurayrah had critics of their own

The story of reception is itself part of the evidence. Ibn Qutaybah (d. 276 AH), in *Taʾwīl Mukhtalif al-Ḥadīth*, transmits objections associated with **al-Naẓẓām**: ʿUmar, ʿUthmān, ʿĀʾishah, and ʿAlī are invoked as people who allegedly challenged Abū Hurayrah. Ibn Qutaybah then explicitly **rebuts** those accusations.[^40]

A quotation in a rebuttal proves that a charge was known at the time of the rebuttal; it does **not** make the opponent's allegation true. But it also means that the controversy did not originate with a twentieth-century writer.

Al-Dhahabī preserves critical statements and then directly rejects some of them. Maḥmūd Abū Rayyah's *Aḍwāʾ ʿalā al-Sunnah* (book ID **11271**) assembles further hostile quotations, while al-Muʿallimī's *al-Anwār al-Kāshifah* (book ID **6448**) contests their transmission and reading. Both modern works are therefore **maps back to older claims**; neither is an independent early witness.[^41]

A particularly revealing case appears in the biographical account of ʿUthmān al-Burrī, where al-Dhahabī records him saying <Arabic>كَذَبَ أَبُو هُرَيْرَةَ</Arabic>. The biographer also documents objections to *al-Burrī himself*. The line is evidence that the criticism was transmitted; it is not automatically an independent or credible contemporary verdict on Abū Hurayrah.[^42]

What is the difference between *a report existing in a book* and *an event demonstrably happening*? Sometimes centuries. And what is the difference between ten books and ten witnesses? Sometimes **one early transmitter**.

This applies to criticisms **of** Abū Hurayrah just as it applies to traditions narrated **by** him. A source critic cannot demand an independent chain for a prophetic saying while exempting a hostile accusation because it is rhetorically convenient. Nor can a defender use the presumed uprightness of a companion to make the historically visible disagreements disappear.

## X. The question beneath all these accusations

Imagine two readers. The first is handed a narration and told that the companion at the top is upright, therefore its content is reliable. The second is handed the same narration and asks whether the companion heard it directly, whether other witnesses remembered the same event, whether its beginning or end was omitted, and whether its pupils transmitted substantially different versions. The second reader is applying the kind of verification that the early disputes themselves describe.

The Qurʾān does not require believers to adopt every unverified claim merely because its transmitter belongs to an honored generation. It directs attention to knowledge, testing, and accountability; Q 17:36 warns against following what one does not know, and Q 49:6 instructs verification of consequential reports. For an extended treatment of that principle, see <ArticleLink id="origins-early-history/74-the-quranic-epistemology-of-verification">The Qurʾānic Epistemology of Verification</ArticleLink>.[^43]

  <Fragment slot="translation">"Do not pursue that of which you have no knowledge. Indeed, hearing, sight, and the heart—each will be questioned." (excerpt)</Fragment>

</QuranPassage>

<VerdictBox title="The verdict is about evidence, not a person's soul" status="Source-critical conclusion" facts={[{value:'Attested',label:'Recorded criticism and disagreement'},{value:'Demonstrated in a report',label:'At least one explicit retraction'},{value:'Not established',label:'Universal intentional fabrication'}]}>

The historically responsible conclusion is not that every narration attributed to Abū Hurayrah is false. Nor is it that every criticism of him can be dismissed because he was a companion. **His authority was contested in the surviving record; some of his statements were corrected; at least one disputed ruling was reportedly withdrawn; and some early Kufan transmitters are represented as selecting among his reports.**

Those facts are sufficient to defeat an assumption of *automatic epistemic certainty*. They require each tradition to be examined on its own evidence. The more sweeping charge—that Abū Hurayrah knowingly fabricated hadith for personal or political ends—remains an allegation to be established, case by case, rather than a premise to be repeated.

</VerdictBox>
