<ClaimBox title="The argument in one sentence">
Ḍirār ibn ʿAmr’s *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* is not merely an early rationalist complaint that some ḥadīths are weak. It is an early theory of how sectarian communities manufacture religious certainty by selecting reports that already fit their doctrines, then attributing those doctrines to the Prophet.
</ClaimBox>

Ḍirār ibn ʿAmr al-Ghaṭafānī was a second/eighth-century theologian and judge who lived before the later legal madhhabs had hardened into the familiar Sunnī map. His *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh*, preserved in a unique Yemeni manuscript and published in 2014, is one of the most important early witnesses to the dispute over ḥadīth authority. Sean Anthony describes it as the earliest surviving stand-alone work by a theologian associated with the Muʿtazila, and more specifically as a treatise on “the use and authority of prophetic traditions” in a period when ḥadīth were rapidly proliferating.[^1]

The usual way to tell this story is too simple. It is not enough to say that Ḍirār was “anti-ḥadīth” or that he was a proto-Muʿtazilī rationalist who preferred reason over transmission. His argument is sharper than that. He thinks the crisis begins when each faction discovers that a prophetic report can be made to speak for its own cause. The problem is not only forgery. The problem is the social function of disputed reports. A report becomes a banner. A banner becomes a faction. A faction then claims the Prophet as its spokesman.

That is why the title matters. *Taḥrīsh* means incitement or provocation. Ḍirār takes the word from a report in which the Prophet forbids instigating fights between animals, then applies that image to Muslim disputation. The scholars and sectarians are no longer merely arguing; they are setting communities against each other.[^2]

## The opening question: why did the community split?

The treatise begins as a response to a question: how did the community differ, where did its unity break, and why did its paths scatter? The editors preserve the opening in the manuscript:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="أما بعد، فإنك كتبت إليّ تسألني عن أمر الأمة، كيف اختلفت، ومن أين انقطع ائتلافها وتشتّت طرقها."
  translation="“You wrote to me asking about the affair of the community: how it differed, from where its harmony was cut off, and how its paths scattered.”[^3]"
/>

This is already an important framing. Ḍirār is not writing a neutral catalogue of sects. He is diagnosing a wound. His question is not “which sect is true?” but “what mechanism made sectarian truth-claims possible?” The answer he gives is that the factions learned to narrate the Prophet into their own positions.

A later witness, Ibn al-Rāwandī as preserved through al-Khayyāṭ’s *al-Intiṣār*, recognized the work this way. He described *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* as containing the reports in which “each sect” narrates from the Prophet what agrees with its own doctrine, <span class="arabic">رواية كل فرقة لما هي عليه عن النبي</span>.[^4] This is one of the strongest early confirmations that the book was remembered not as a generic theology manual, but as a critique of sectarian ḥadīth attribution.

## Ḍirār’s core evidence: every faction has its reports

The line that should anchor the article is this:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="وتعلّق كلّ فريق منهم لمذهبه بجنس من الحديث."
  translation="“Each party attached itself, for its doctrine, to a type of ḥadīth.”[^5]"
/>

The wording is precise. Ḍirār is not saying every report is equally forged, nor that every transmitter is lying. He is saying that factions attach themselves to a *jins* of report, a recognizable genre or family of reports that reinforces their position. This is close to what modern hadith criticism often observes at the level of tradition families: once a dispute becomes politically or theologically useful, reports proliferate around the pressure point.

Anthony’s review emphasizes how broad the catalogue is. Ḍirār discusses succession, God’s attributes, free will and predestination, the merits of Syria, poverty, traders, singing, sex, and even debates over Ibn Masʿūd’s Qurʾānic reading.[^6] The range matters because it prevents a narrow reading. Ḍirār is not only attacking Shīʿī reports, or only predestinarian reports, or only legal reports. He is describing a general ecology of religious argument.

The point is not that Ḍirār has produced a modern isnād-cum-matn study. He has not. His method is earlier and cruder. But his intuition is historically valuable: when a doctrinal party repeatedly finds the Prophet endorsing exactly what that party needs, the historian should ask whether the report has been shaped by the controversy rather than merely transmitted through it.

## The “Samaritan” and “Paul” analogy

One of the most striking features of the work is Ḍirār’s use of biblical and Qurʾānic analogy. He says every prophetic community has its own deceiver, comparing the Muslim crisis to the Samaritan who led Israel into the calf and to Paul, who in Islamic anti-Pauline polemic corrupted the message of Jesus.[^7]

This analogy does real work. It means that Ḍirār is not explaining disagreement as a harmless diversity of schools. He is placing the Muslim community inside a larger pattern: after a prophet dies, later religious specialists claim to preserve him while actually producing division in his name. The most dangerous corruption is not an open rejection of revelation. It is the pious attribution of later doctrine to the founding messenger.

That also explains why Ḍirār is so threatening to later ḥadīth-centered orthodoxy. He is not merely saying, “some reports are bad.” Everyone eventually says that. He is saying that factional hadith-use itself can be structurally corrupt even when it speaks in the language of piety.

## His standard of proof: not “no ḥadīth,” but no disputed ḥadīth as sectarian proof

The strongest version of the argument must avoid exaggeration. Ḍirār is not a modern Qurʾān-only writer, and he is not simply rejecting all prophetic reports. Anthony notes that he still shows deference to Wāṣil ibn ʿAṭāʾ’s earlier position that reports enjoying broad consensus, <span class="arabic">الأخبار المجتمع عليها</span>, carry weight.[^8] What Ḍirār rejects is the use of contested reports as binding proof in disputes where the community is already divided.

This is why his appeal to consensus is so important:

<HadithBlock
  arabic="إن الحجة لا تثبت بعد الرسول ﷺ إلا بإجماع الأمة، والإجماع وجه عندي وهو حجة، من خالفها ضلّ ومن اتبعها اهتدى."
  translation="“Proof is not established after the Messenger except by the consensus of the community. Consensus, in my view, is an authoritative path: whoever opposes it has gone astray, and whoever follows it is guided.”[^9]"
/>

This should not be flattened into a slogan that Ḍirār “rejected Sunnah.” His view is more subtle and more historically useful. He accepts the idea that the community can preserve binding knowledge, but denies that rival factions can establish truth by producing rival reports. In other words, the proof must be public, communal, and non-sectarian. A report that only becomes convincing inside one faction’s argumentative world does not settle the dispute. It is part of the dispute.

## Why this belongs before the madhhabs

The title of the blog should not imply that Ḍirār predates all legal activity. He does not. Mālik, Abū Ḥanīfa, al-Awzāʿī, Sufyān al-Thawrī, and other early authorities are already part of the landscape. The point is that he stands before the later settlement in which Sunnī identity became tightly bound to a canon of ḥadīth, a hierarchy of transmitters, and a literature devoted to harmonizing apparently contradictory reports.

This is where Ibn Qutayba becomes crucial. Hansu and Kaskin argue that large sections of Ibn Qutayba’s *Taʾwīl mukhtalif al-ḥadīth* quote or engage Ḍirār’s work, even though Ibn Qutayba does not name him. Anthony thinks the editors convincingly show that Ḍirār and his works on ḥadīth were a key target of Ibn Qutayba’s treatise, while adding the caution that Ibn Qutayba may sometimes be responding to another work of Ḍirār, *Kitāb Tanāquḍ al-ḥadīth*.[^10]

That reception history changes the importance of the text. *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* is not a curiosity from the losing side. It is part of the pressure that helped generate the Sunnī apologetic genre of reconciling contradictory reports. If Ibn Qutayba is answering Ḍirār or his circle, then later Sunnī hadith-theory is not simply developing in isolation. It is responding to an early critique that had already identified the central problem: contradictory reports had become ammunition for rival doctrines.

## Dating the argument: the Abbasid horizon

One of the strongest improvements to the analysis is chronological. A later story attached to al-Jubbāʾī’s *Maqālāt* tries to place *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* before Ḍirār’s full integration into the Muʿtazilī circle. Anthony warns against taking that story too literally. If Ḍirār had written the book before joining Wāṣil’s circle, it would likely have to predate Wāṣil’s death in 131/749 and the Abbasid revolution in 132/750.[^11]

The contents of the treatise make that early date difficult. Anthony notes that *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* shows awareness of Abbasid revolutionary propaganda, including controversies over “the Easterners,” the black banners, and the permissibility of entering the dīwān with the *musawwida*, the black-clad Abbasid movement.[^12] This pushes the text into, or at least very near, the Abbasid revolutionary horizon.

This matters because it makes the book less abstract. Ḍirār is writing in a world in which hadith and apocalyptic reports are not merely classroom material. They are connected to revolutions, dynastic claims, sectarian boundaries, and the making of orthodoxy. The same century that canonizes “the people of Sunnah and the community” also produces polemical reports about rulers, rebels, predestination, sectarian salvation, and eschatological armies. Ḍirār’s complaint belongs to that world.

<ContextNote title="Why the Abbasid horizon matters">
If *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* knows debates about black banners and the black-clad Abbasid movement, then Ḍirār is not only reacting to private scholarly disagreement. He is reacting to a post-revolutionary public sphere in which reports about the Prophet could legitimate regimes, movements, and sectarian identities.
</ContextNote>

## The biographical problem: Muʿtazilī, anti-Shīʿī, or something else?

The old labels do not fully work. Later writers variously attach Ḍirār to the Muʿtazila, exclude him from them, accuse him of predestinarian views, call him a Kūfan, describe him as anti-Shīʿī, and treat him as a dangerous rationalist. The introduction to the Arabic edition preserves reports from al-Balkhī, al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār, al-Jushamī, Ibn al-Nadīm, and al-Jubbāʾī that already show a contested memory of Ḍirār’s school location.[^13]

That contested memory should be used rather than hidden. Ḍirār is valuable precisely because he does not sit comfortably inside a later school. He belongs to the transitional world before labels became clean. He is close enough to the Muʿtazilī problem of reason and consensus to be remembered with them, but odd enough to be excluded by later Muʿtazilīs. He is anti-Shīʿī enough for later Shīʿī theologians to contest him, but his critique of factional reports also threatens Sunnī hadith-apologetics.

The result is a rare voice from before the victorious categories arranged the archive. Later Sunnī heresiographers could make him a deviant. Later Muʿtazilīs could make him embarrassing. Later traditionalists could make him dangerous. But *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* lets us see the argument in his own terms: sectarian ḥadīth-use fragments the community.

## A better way to state the thesis

The strongest version of the thesis is not:

> “Ḍirār proves that all ḥadīth are fabricated.”

That overstates the case and makes the article easier to dismiss. The stronger thesis is:

> “Ḍirār is one of the earliest surviving Muslim authors to treat disputed ḥadīth as a sociological and epistemological problem: reports do not merely transmit doctrine; in sectarian settings, they manufacture doctrine, authorize doctrine, and intensify communal rupture.”

That thesis can be defended from the text. He begins with the breakup of the community. He explains it through factional attachment to report-types. He catalogues rival reports across theology, law, politics, and sectarian identity. He limits binding proof after the Prophet to consensus. And his work was important enough to be remembered by Ibn al-Rāwandī, preserved through al-Khayyāṭ, and answered in the orbit of Ibn Qutayba’s hadith-apologetic project.

## Why this matters for hadith criticism today

Ḍirār is not doing modern source criticism, and he should not be turned into a modern academic before his time. He is not drawing isnād diagrams, identifying common links, or reconstructing regional transmission layers. Yet his work anticipates the central question behind those methods: when a report serves a factional need too perfectly, how do we distinguish memory from argument?

His answer is not the answer of modern historians, but it is historically revealing. He retreats to consensus because consensus is the only form of post-prophetic proof he thinks can escape factional capture. Modern historians need not accept his solution to recognize the problem he saw. Reports can be true, false, reshaped, selectively quoted, or contextually redeployed. In all cases, they do social work.

That is the real power of *Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh*. It shows that the anxiety over hadith proliferation was not invented by Orientalists, modern reformers, or anti-traditional polemicists. A second/eighth-century Muslim theologian already saw that rival communities could place their own doctrines on the Prophet’s tongue and then use those attributions to tear the community apart.

<VerdictBox title="Conclusion">
*Kitāb al-Taḥrīsh* is best read as an early Muslim critique of sectarian hadith-production, not as a simple rejection of every prophetic report. Ḍirār’s target is the disputed report that appears inside a factional controversy and then claims to settle that controversy by prophetic authority. His solution is consensus; his diagnosis is more enduring: when each group has its own prophetic archive, transmission becomes a battlefield.
</VerdictBox>
