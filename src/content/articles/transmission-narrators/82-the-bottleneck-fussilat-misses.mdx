The report is preserved with this core wording in the canonical collections:[^1]

<HadithBlock translation={'This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two people remain.'} source={'Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820'}  arabic={"لا يَزَالُ هَذَا الأَمْرُ فِي قُرَيْشٍ مَا بَقِيَ مِنْهُمُ اثْنَانِ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/61/2"} />

In September 2026, Fussilat published a defense of this report under the title *Who Fabricated the Hadith? | Leadership of Quraish*.[^2] Its basic argument revolves around a report that is transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar, through his grandson Muḥammad b. Zayd, through Muḥammad’s son ʿĀṣim, and from ʿĀṣim through numerous students. Since ʿĀṣim and the men above him do not look like dishonest political propagandists, Fussilat concludes that there is effectively “no room” for a fabricator.
The article agrees insofar the report really does become securely visible at ʿĀṣim, making him the common link for the tradition. Numerous pupils independently transmit it from him directly. This observation is correct, but Fussilat then treats the independent strands below ʿĀṣim as verifying the single family strand above him. It does not. The evidence we possess is much more specific.

The question is therefore not, “*Can we prove that ʿĀṣim was a liar?*” The historical question is what independent evidence shows that this particular hadith existed before ʿĀṣim? That is the question Fussilat never really answers.

## What Fussilat Actually Proves


Fussilat begins with the chains and rightly rejects the possibility that ʿĀṣim’s many pupils independently invented the same saying and falsely attributed it to him. Its conclusion is that “*Aasim narrated the hadith as reported.*”[^2] That is compatible with the source-critical analysis. The report is transmitted from ʿĀṣim through multiple pupils. The six principal direct pupils collated for the core Ibn ʿUmar recension are the following:[^1]

- Muʿādh b. Muʿādh;
- Abū al-Naḍr Hāshim b. al-Qāsim;
- Muḥammad b. Yazīd al-Kalāʿī al-Wāsiṭī;
- Abū al-Walīd Hishām al-Ṭayālisī;
- Aḥmad b. ʿAbd Allāh b. Yūnus;
- Bishr b. al-Mufaḍḍal.

The wording varies slightly between them, but the core of the tradition is stable:[^1]

<HadithBlock translation={'This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two remain.'} source={'Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820 (core wording across recensions)'}  arabic={"لا يَزَالُ هَذَا الأَمْرُ فِي قُرَيْشٍ مَا بَقِيَ مِنْهُمُ اثْنَانِ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/61/2"} />

This is exactly what a genuine common link looks like.  Above ʿĀṣim there is one stem; below him the report suddenly fans into multiple students. That gives us the solid conclusion of ʿĀṣim historically teaching the report to those individuals. It does not give us the next set of conclusions of Muḥammad b. Zayd teaching it to ʿĀṣim, then Ibn ʿUmar teaching it to Muḥammad, and then the Prophet having taught it to Ibn ʿUmar. Those are additional historical claims and each layer would require additional independent verification.
This distinction is basic to isnād-cum-matn analysis. In a previous HadithCritic study on the “*angels cursing women*” traditions, I put the principle this way: the fact that a transmitter could have met a teacher is only a background condition; the real question is whether the report is independently controlled above the common link.[^3] A plausible upper chain is not the same thing as an independently evidenced upper chain.[^3] An isnād is itself a historical claim. It cannot function as its own verification. The surviving evidence looks asymmetrical:

The cautious historical statement is not, “*Ibn ʿUmar said this.*”

<ClaimBox title="Central transmission finding">

**The report is securely datable to ʿĀṣim’s transmission level. Its attribution above him is not independently established.**

</ClaimBox>

This is simply the difference between what the chain claims and what the extant transmission independently demonstrates. Fussilat collapsed those two categories.

## The Family Test Fussilat Accidentally Points Toward


The most important development in this investigation is the “sibling test.” Fussilat himself emphasizes that ʿĀṣim sometimes transmitted through his brothers. He then uses this as evidence of his honesty. In one famous case, ʿĀṣim says that he had heard a report from his father, but had not retained it properly, so his brother Wāqid reminded him of it.[^2] That evidence is indeed important—but not for the reason Fussilat thinks. It tells us that the family transmission network leaves visible traces. Muḥammad b. Zayd did not have only one transmitting son. The relevant family included:

This family structure is independently attested in al-Mizzī’s *Tahdhīb al-Kamāl*, Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s  *al-Jarḥ wa-l-Taʿdīl*, and Ibn Saʿd’s *al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā*.[^4] The question is therefore, do these brothers preserve other Muḥammad b. Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar material? They do. And that changes the evidentiary value of their silence on the Quraysh report.  Wāqid independently transmits the father’s Ibn ʿUmar material Aḥmad preserves the Farewell-Pilgrimage warning through Wāqid:

<HadithBlock translation={'Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar narrated to us; Shuʿba narrated to us, from Wāqid b. Muḥammad b. Zayd, who heard his father narrate from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar, from the Prophet, that he said during the Farewell Pilgrimage: ‘Alas for you’—or he said, ‘Woe to you—do not revert to disbelief after me, with some of you striking the necks of others.’'} source={'Aḥmad b. The crucial part is the chain: Wāqid → Muḥammad b. Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar. Wāqid therefore possessed independent access[^5] to the same paternal Ibn ʿUmar channel that the Quraysh hadith claims to use. He also preserves another family report[^6] through the same father-route; an Abū Bakr hadith recorded by Ibn Abī Shayba.[^6] Thus the explanation, “*Perhaps only ʿĀṣim preserved his father’s Ibn ʿUmar material*” is no longer justifiable. The family really did preserve paternal Ibn ʿUmar material through more than one son. Yet there is no independent sibling that transmits this report alongside ʿĀṣim. Every located witness to this matn still converges on him.

## Al-Dāraquṭnī Gives Us the Control Case


A revealing comparison comes from a completely different report: the “*Islam is built on five*” tradition. Al-Dāraquṭnī records that Aḥmad b. Yūnus transmitted it as:[^7]

<HadithBlock translation={'From ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad, from his brother Wāqid, from his father, from Ibn ʿUmar.'} source={'Al-Dāraquṭnī, al-ʿIlal, 13:211, no. 3102 (Aḥmad b. Yūnus route)'}  arabic={"عن عاصم بن محمد عن أخيه واقد عن أبيه عن ابن عمر"} url={"https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-2/h/11657/service/8"} />

while Bishr b. al-Mufaḍḍal, Muʿādh b. Muʿādh, and Abū al-Naḍr transmitted:[^8]

<HadithBlock translation={'From ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad, from his father, from Ibn ʿUmar.'} source={'Al-Dāraquṭnī, al-ʿIlal, 13:211, no. 3102 (Bishr, Muʿādh, and Abū al-Naḍr routes)'}  arabic={"عن عاصم بن محمد عن أبيه عن ابن عمر"} url={"https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-2/h/11657/service/8"} />

and their brother ʿUmar transmitted a mawqūf form:

<HadithBlock translation={'From his father, from Ibn ʿUmar.'} source={'Al-Dāraquṭnī, al-ʿIlal, 13:211, no. 3102 (ʿUmar’s mawqūf route)'}  arabic={"عن أبيه عن ابن عمر"} url={"https://hdith.com/encyclopedia/book/b-2/h/11657/service/8"} />

Al-Dāraquṭnī then proposes:

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Dāraquṭnī'} source={'al-ʿIlal, 13:211, no. 3102'}>

“It appears that ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad heard it from his father and then verified it with his brother Wāqid.”

<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">ويشبه أن يكون عاصم بن محمد سمعه من أبيه، واستثبته من أخيه واقد</p>

</QuoteBlock>

This is extremely important. It demonstrates that within this exact family:

- the same paternal material can survive through multiple brothers;
- ʿĀṣim’s own route can sometimes preserve his brother Wāqid as an intermediate verifier;
- another brother, ʿUmar, can preserve an independent version from the same father.

In other words, the family can leave precisely the sort of cross-sibling traces that historical criticism would expect if a report really circulated among multiple sons of Muḥammad b. Zayd. But for the hadith of Quraysh’s authority, there is:

- No Wāqid route.
- No ʿUmar route.
- No Zayd route.
- No Abū Bakr route.
- No Nāfiʿ route.
- No Sālim route.
- No ʿAbd Allāh b. Dīnār route.

Every located witness converges on ʿĀṣim. Fussilat is correct that the students below ʿĀṣim are too numerous to dismiss as coordinated invention. Below ʿĀṣim the report spreads widely. Above him it contracts completely.

## “ʿĀṣim Was Honest” Does Not Solve This Problem

A large portion of Fussilat’s defense is devoted to ʿĀṣim’s character. He looks at a brief glimpse of ʿĀṣim’s transmission history:

- he uses longer isnāds elsewhere
- he sometimes narrates through brothers
- he admits when he did not memorize something
- he transmits material critical of rulers.

Therefore, he concludes ʿĀṣim does not fit the profile of a fabricator.[^2] This is much weaker than it sounds. The question is not whether ʿĀṣim was a habitual liar. The question is whether this particular report is independently attested before him. Those are completely different claims. A narrator may preserve a hundred genuine reports and still be the point at which one report becomes propheticized. By propheticization, I mean the elevation of an existing maxim, legal or political principle, family report, or interpretive formulation into a saying attributed to the Prophet. Secondary formation does not require the transmitter to be a career forger. It can result from:

- deliberate invention;
- mistaken attribution;
- raising an inherited maxim to the Prophet;
- memory conflation;
- political or legal interpretation becoming quotation;
- retrojection to an earlier family authority;
-  pious legitimation.

Hadith criticism often turns this into a moral drama where a narrator is either upright or he is a liar. Historical criticism does not need that binary. This is why the epistemological issue matters. This was argued in a prior HadithCritic study.[^9]

<QuoteBlock author={'HadithCritic'} source={'The Quranic Epistemology of Verification (Q. 49:6)'} sourceUrl={'https://hadithcriticblog.com/blogs/origins-early-history/74-the-quranic-epistemology-of-verification/'}>

“Truth and falsehood are properties of claims. They are not properties of people.”

</QuoteBlock>

A trustworthy person can be wrong. A morally compromised person can report something true. Reputation changes how carefully we examine a claim; it does not substitute for verification of the claim itself. Even if every positive rijāl judgment about ʿĀṣim is accepted, the upper transmission still requires independent evidence.

## The “Longer Isnād” Argument Is a Non Sequitur

Fussilat places considerable weight on the fact that ʿĀṣim sometimes used longer routes to Ibn ʿUmar even though he allegedly possessed a shorter family route through his father. It argues that a dishonest narrator would have simply used the short route every time.[^2] That only works if the accusation is:  “ʿ*Āṣim fabricated his entire corpus and fabricated all his isnāds*.”
No serious source-critical argument requires that. Suppose ʿĀṣim genuinely heard one report through: one of his brothers → Nāfiʿ → Ibn ʿUmar and another through: brother → Sālim → Ibn ʿUmar.  There is no reason those genuine reports would suddenly be shortened merely because another politically distinctive report entered circulation through a family attribution. The existence of real long chains elsewhere proves only that ʿĀṣim preserved real long chains elsewhere. It does not authenticate this specific short chain.  The logic is a non sequitur. He preserved longer isnāds elsewhere therefore this short upper isnād cannot be secondary. The conclusion does not follow from the premise.

## Fussilat Reconstructs ʿĀṣim’s Politics Selectively

Fussilat also tries to reconstruct ʿĀṣim’s political beliefs from the reports he transmitted. When a report portrays hypocrisy before rulers, Fussilat treats the matn as evidence that ʿĀṣim himself was no friend of rulers. When a report discourages rebellion against Yazīd, Fussilat explains that ʿĀṣim could simply have transmitted different material from different teachers and need not personally endorse all of it.[^2] Those two moves pull in opposite directions.
If transmitted content is reliable evidence for ʿĀṣim’s personal politics, then contradictory political material must be reconciled. If transmitted content is not necessarily his personal politics because he transmitted whatever he received, then the anti-ruler reports cannot be used as biographical proof that he personally rejected ruler-friendly ideas.
There is an even clearer example. Fussilat admits that ʿĀṣim’s shortened report about hypocrisy does not mention Marwān. He nevertheless argues that his audience would have known the context was Umayyad.[^2] That is a contextual historical inference. It may even be a reasonable one, but the same method cannot then be dismissed when applied to the Quraysh report. If we are permitted to interpret a vague political saying in light of its historical environment when that helps the defense, then we are also permitted to ask what Quraysh report would have meant in a world where Khārijites and Ibāḍīs explicitly denied that Quraysh possessed an intrinsic monopoly over rulership (which we actually end up doing later).

## The Real Political Question Was Not “*Umayyads or Abbasids?*”

This is where Fussilat targets the wrong theory. The article repeatedly asks whether the hadith was fabricated for the Umayyads or Abbasids. It then argues that ʿĀṣim was not sufficiently pro Umayyad or pro-Abbasid to fit either profile, but the report does not say “*the Umayyads shall rule*” or “*the Abbasids shall rule.*” It says, “*this authority will remain in Quraysh.*” The most historically relevant opposition is therefore not Umayyad vs. Abbasid, but Qurashī monopoly vs. non-Qurashī eligibility. That dispute is explicitly attested during the time ʿĀṣim’s report becomes visible.

## The Counter-Doctrine Is Explicit by 77 AH

Under the events of 77 AH, al-Ṭabarī preserves a remarkable exchange between Shabīb’s camp and Muṭarrif b. al-Mughīra. The reported Khārijite position stated the following.[^10]

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Ṭabarī'} source={'Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, 6:287–290, esp. 288'} sourceUrl={'https://ar.wikisource.org/wiki/تاريخ_الطبري/الجزء_السادس'}>

“We [Khārijites] have chosen for ourselves the one among us who is most acceptable to us and most capable of bearing what has been entrusted to him. So long as he does not alter or change, he is the holder of our authority … We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this affair than the rest of the Arabs … Had they not known that the best people before God are the most pious, and that those most entitled to this affair are the most pious, the best among them, and the most capable of carrying their affairs, they would not have governed the people.”

<p lang="ar" dir="rtl"><span class="hc-quote__excerpt-label" lang="en" dir="ltr">Arabic excerpt for the highlighted clause</span>لأنا لا نرى أن قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر من غيرها من العرب</p>

</QuoteBlock>

The most important clause is: “*We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this authority than the rest of the Arabs.*” Compare this with ʿĀṣim’s report which states, “*this authority will remain in Quraysh.*” The wording is almost an ideological mirror image. It refutes the suggestion that there was no obvious historical purpose for a hadith about leadership being permanently Qurashī. Qurashī leadership was exactly what was being disputed. A closely related recension in al-Balādhurī has Shabīb’s answer as well.[^11]

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Balādhurī'} source={'Jumal min Ansāb al-Ashrāf, 7:399–400'} sourceUrl={'https://shamela.org/gen/191eb8ae8222339c91429efb3d68a664_gen.pdf'}>

“We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this authority than we are. Just as Quraysh chose its best after the death of the Messenger of God, so we may choose our best.”

<p lang="ar" dir="rtl">إنا لا نرى قريشًا أحق بهذا الأمر منا، فمَن اختارت قريش أفضلها بعد وفاة رسول الله فذلك لك لنا أن نختار أفضلنا.</p>

</QuoteBlock>



## The Ibāḍī Material Preserves the Same Constitutional Logic


Ann Lambton summarizes the broader Khārijite doctrine[^12] by arguing the Khārijites rejected the Sunnī restriction of the imamate to Quraysh; the imam need not belong to any particular family or tribe and could even be a slave, provided he was the best-qualified Muslim of his time and a good ruler.[^12] Sālim b. Dhakwān’s early Ibāḍī epistle supplies a more formative control. In §128, allegiance is explicitly conditional:[^12]

<QuoteBlock author={'Sālim b. Dhakwān'} source={'The Epistle of Sālim Ibn Dhakwān, 141–142, §128'}>

“We hold that a band of Muslims should pay allegiance to their imam only on [condition that he will wage] *jihād* in the path of God and that they will obey him in what is approved … Let them therefore appoint to the command their most excellent men and those among them who understand [God’s religion].”

</QuoteBlock>

The passage lays out the positive alternative logic—merit, religious competence, approved conduct, and conditional allegiance. The received Ibāḍī hadith corpus is especially revealing because it preserves both sides of the tension. Report 45 gives a conditional Quraysh formulation:[^13]

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Rabīʿ b. Ḥabīb'} source={'Al-Rabīʿ b. Ḥabīb, Musnad, Bāb fī l-wilāya wa-l-imāra, no. 45'}>

“This authority will not cease to be among you, with you as its rulers, so long as you do not introduce innovations; but if you do, God will set over you the worst of His creation, who will strip you as this branch is stripped.”

</QuoteBlock>

Later in the received collection, report 818 again says Qurashī power remains only so long as they do not introduce innovations contrary to religion, while report 819 commands obedience to a mutilated Abyssinian slave so long as he rules according to God’s Book.[^14] This is important because the Ibāḍī corpus is not simply deleting or suppressing pro-Quraysh material. Gaiser therefore describes these traditions[^15] as a “*straightforward rejection of Qurayshī claims to authority in favor of piety as the final legitimating criterion of leadership.*” He further notes that Ibāḍī jurisprudence rejected Qurashī membership, kinship with the Prophet, and designation (naṣṣ) as necessary criteria of legitimate authority.[^15] The later legal tradition becomes even more explicit. Al-Kudamī writes under the chapter[^16] called, “*The Chapter on the Imamate and the Discussion Concerning It*”—

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Kudamī'} source={'Al-Kudamī, al-Istiqāma, 2:119, Bāb al-imāma wa-l-qawl fīhā'}>

“The people of rectitude among the imams agreed on obedience to the just imam so long as he obeys God and His Messenger and acts according to the Book of God and the Sunna of His Prophet… If a mutilated Abyssinian were appointed over you and established among you the Book of God and my Sunna, hear him and obey him.”

</QuoteBlock>

This is not a chapter about subordinate military officers. It’s explicitly about the imamate. Al-Kindī’s Muṣannaf gives a precise formulation:[^17]

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Kindī'} source={'al-Muṣannaf, vol. 7, pt. 10, 332–333, 349'}>

“That is: after the Prophet, the imamate is not contracted for a successor—or an imam removed—except through the consultation of the learned and their consent, sincerely for God.”

</QuoteBlock>

The same work states that if an imam abandons the conditions on which he was given allegiance:

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Kindī'} source={'al-Muṣannaf, vol. 7, pt. 10, 332–333, 349'}>

“if he departs from those conditions on which allegiance was pledged to him, his imamate ceases.”

</QuoteBlock>

The Ibāḍī logic is:

- the imam is selected;
- merit and religious competence matter;
- obedience is conditional;
- justice matters;
- consultation and consent matter;
- the office can be forfeited;
- non-Qurashī ancestry does not invalidate rule.

ʿĀṣim’s Qurashī hadith has no piety condition, justice condition, consultation condition or forfeiture clause of any sort. The authority remains in Quraysh as long as human society itself remains. That is precisely why the hadith works so well as an unconditional counter-formulation.

## The 129–130 AH Crisis Makes the Dispute Concrete


The constitutional dispute did not remain theoretical. By 129–130 AH, the Ibāḍī movement was operating under a non-Qurashī imam, ʿAbd Allāh b. Yaḥyā al-Kindī, while Abū Ḥamza al-Shārī’s forces seized Mecca, defeated the Medinans at Qudayd, and occupied Medina, where ʿĀṣim himself resided.[^18]  The 129–130 AH movement put a non-Qurashī model of supreme leadership into practice in the Ḥijāz during ʿĀṣim’s lifetime.

Three principal branches are necessarily post-130[^19] because the pupils had not yet been born. Muʿādh was only a child. The visible multi-pupil proliferation of the report therefore belongs overwhelmingly to ʿĀṣim’s later teaching career.[^19] Bishr is the important control. He was already an active student by 130/131 and therefore could have heard ʿĀṣim earlier.[^19]

## Fussilat’s Own Chronology Does Not Refute the Theory

Fussilat argues that ʿĀṣim continued transmitting the report after the Umayyads fell, and therefore an Umayyad motive is unlikely.[^2][^2] That objection works only against an Umayyad specific theory.

- The hadith is not Umayyad-specific.
- It does not say Banū Umayya.
- It does not say Banū al-ʿAbbās.
- It does not say Banū Hāshim.


It establishes a wider constitutional category: Quraysh. That makes the report remarkably portable

- An Umayyad can use it.
- An Abbasid can use it.
- An ʿAlid can use it.
- A Zubayrid can use it.
- An ʿUmarid can use it

The report excludes one category above all others, a non-Qurashī claimant to supreme authority. Thus the fact that the saying survived the Umayyad-Abbasid transition is not a problem for the counter-tradition theory. It is exactly what we should expect from a branch-neutral doctrine of Qurashī monopoly.

## Criticizing Rulers Does Not Mean Rejecting Qurashī Exclusivity

Fussilat repeatedly points to reports in which ʿĀṣim transmits criticism of rulers. It uses these to argue that he was not politically motivated to create a pro-Qurashī hadith.[^2] That confuses two entirely different questions. A person can believe a ruler being corrupt while also believing members of a hyper-specific constitutional class may legitimately hold the office. There is no contradiction. Someone can oppose an Umayyad and still believe the caliph must be Qurashī. Someone can oppose an Abbasid and still believe the caliph must be Qurashī. The Khārijite/Ibāḍī doctrine ties legitimacy to moral performance as opposed to established genealogy. The whole debate is about whether bad conduct can defeat inherited or tribal claims to authority. So “anti-ruler material” tells us little about whether ʿĀṣim accepted or rejected Qurashī eligibility.

## “He Had No Chance of Becoming Caliph” Is Not a Historical Rebuttal

Fussilat argues that ʿĀṣim and his family were not close to achieving political power, so generic pro-Qurashī motivation becomes meaningless.[^2] This assumes that ideology must generate immediate personal material gain, which isn’t necessarily true. People defend:

- tribal privilege;
- class privilege;
- dynastic principles;
- legal schools;
- ethnic hierarchies;
- institutional authority;
-  constitutional restrictions

—without personally expecting to occupy the highest office protected by that system. ʿĀṣim was not simply “*some random man from Quraysh.*” Al-Mizzī identifies him as the following:[^20]

<QuoteBlock author={'Al-Mizzī'} source={'Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, 13:542–543, no. 3027'}>

“ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad b. Zayd b. ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb, al-ʿUmarī, al-Madanī.”

</QuoteBlock>

He belonged to a prestigious Qurashī-ʿUmarid family. The fact that he personally had little chance of becoming caliph does not make tribal constitutional ideology meaningless. Fussilat then asks a revealing question. If ʿĀṣim were simply biased in favor of Quraysh, why fabricate something about leadership rather than knowledge, piety, or virtue?[^2] As mentioned previously, leadership was the point under dispute during his time. Shabīb’s position does not say Quraysh are not more knowledgeable. He stated, “*we do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this authority.*” The Ibāḍī sources are about who may be imam, how the imam is selected, what conditions legitimate him, and when he can lose office. So if an opposing tradition were to answer that constitutional doctrine, leadership is exactly what we would expect it to address. Fussilat presents the specificity of the matn as a problem for the political theory. Historically, it is one of the strongest reasons to take the political setting seriously.

## The Lexical Mirror: الأمر هذا

The Khārijite-associated position states, “*we do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this authority,*” while ʿĀṣim’s recension states, “*this authority will remain in Quraysh.*” One denies an intrinsic genealogical right. The other makes genealogical possession effectively permanent. When the same phrase occurs inside opposing constitutional formulations, and one of those formulations is transmitted only through a second-century common link living in the aftermath of that exact controversy, the parallel becomes historically relevant and unlikely to be coincidental

## The Qurʾān Section Does Not Solve the Provenance Problem

<QuranVerse verse="49:13" />
Fussilat ends by arguing that Qurashī political leadership does not necessarily contradict Q49:13. It then points to Qurʾānic examples in which God selects particular families for prophethood or grants special favor to Israel.[^2] Even if this entire theological argument is granted, it does not answer the historical question. A report can be theologically possible and still be historically secondary. The logic (since God ***can*** choose a lineage for a particular role, therefore, God ***has*** chosen Quraysh for perpetual supreme political authority) is invalid without independent evidence for the second proposition. The examples of Adam, Noah, Abraham’s family, ʿImrān’s family, and Israel demonstrate that God **can** and **has** explicitly choose families. They do not demonstrate that **He did** establish an eternal Qurashī monopoly over the caliphate. That is a modal fallacy. Just because it is possible, doesn’t mean it’s the case. The hadith itself remains the evidence for the specific claim and therefore its provenance still has to be established.

## The “Magical Fabricator”  Straw Man

Fussilat concludes that critics need a mysterious or “magical” fabricator because no one in the chain fits the profile it has constructed,1 but no magical figure is required. The transmission diagram gives us an obvious historical location. If we were to ask:

- Where does the report first become multiply recoverable? The answer would be ʿĀṣim.
- Where does it first fan into numerous independent pupils?  The answer would be ʿĀṣim.
- Does another son of Muḥammad b. Zayd independently preserve this saying? No.
- Do those same brothers independently preserve *other* paternal Ibn ʿUmar material? Yes.

None of this requires magical thinking. It’s the sort of asymmetry common-link analysis is designed to identify. We can say with strong confidence:

- this Quraysh report is securely attested at ʿĀṣim’s level;
- it proliferates through multiple pupils below him;
- above him, all located versions collapse into the same family stem;
- other brothers demonstrably preserve Muḥammad b. Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar material;
- no sibling bypass has been found for this saying;
- the visible fan-out is largely post-130 AH;
- a directly contrary Khārijite doctrine about Qurashi leadership is explicitly attested during ʿĀṣim’s lifetime;
- Ibāḍī sources preserve the same broader non-genealogical, conditional theory of legitimacy;
- non-Qurashī supreme leadership was put into practice in the Ḥijāz in 129–130 AH.

The strongest historical reconstruction is that ʿĀṣim constructed this tradition in response to Kharijite doctrine during the Kharijite conquest of Medina and Mecca—a period in which ʿĀṣim was alive, actively transmitting hadith, and able to witness these events firsthand. Above ʿĀṣim, the chain is uncorroborated. The family itself supplies a control group because Wāqid and ʿUmar really do preserve other material from Muḥammad b. Zayd through Ibn ʿUmar. For this politically distinctive saying, however, only ʿĀṣim’s route survives. Its broad dissemination belongs predominantly of the time of his career.
At the same time, Khārijite and Ibāḍī political discourse had already made the relevant constitutional issue explicit. Quraysh **did not** possess an intrinsic superior right to leadership. Virtues like piety, competence, consultation, and justice were the operative criteria. The 129–130 revolt then placed that alternative model into practice in the Ḥijāz. The ʿĀṣim recension attempts to undercut that precise constitutional ideal:

<HadithBlock translation={'This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two people remain.'} source={'Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820'}  arabic={"لا يَزَالُ هَذَا الأَمْرُ فِي قُرَيْشٍ مَا بَقِيَ مِنْهُمُ اثْنَانِ"} url={"https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/61/2"} />

ʿĀṣim’s Qurayshi narrative was intended to counter this Ibadi/Kharijite doctrine and preserve the office as an exclusively Qurayshi institution. That is why ʿĀṣim remains the earliest securely recoverable point of broad dissemination and the strongest candidate for the report’s formulation.

## Summary

Fussilat’s argument depends on a series of assumptions that do not survive a source-critical analysis.

- First, it treats the isnād’s upper half as established history before independently testing it.
- Second, it takes multiple students below ʿĀṣim and incorrectly lets them authenticate the single strand above him.
- Third, it turns ʿĀṣim’s general character into proof for a particular report.
- Fourth, it mistakes anti-ruler sentiment for evidence against Qurashī constitutional exclusivity.
- Fifth, it restricts political motive to pro-Umayyad or pro-Abbasid partisanship and therefore misses the more basic constitutional dispute over whether Quraysh possessed an intrinsic monopoly on supreme authority.
- Sixth, it asks why anyone would fabricate something specifically about leadership when leadership is exactly what the opposing Khārijite/Ibāḍī doctrine was about.


<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">

The evidence we have established suggests ʿĀṣim is responsible for the formulation of the hadith of Quraysh’s genealogical authority, in a time where that established model was under threat under an emerging Kharajite/Ibadi rule that threatened that established Qurashi model; all taking place where ʿĀṣim lived, and during his time of proliferating hadith.

</VerdictBox>
