A response to Fussilat’s “Who Fabricated the Hadith? | Leadership of Quraish.”

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two people remain.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش ما بقي من الناس اثنان</Arabic>**

</div>

In September 2026, Fussilat published a defense of this report under the title *Who Fabricated the Hadith? | Leadership of Quraish*.[^1] Its basic argument revolves around a report that is transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar, through his grandson Muḥammad b. Zayd, through Muḥammad’s son ʿĀṣim, and from ʿĀṣim through numerous students. Since ʿĀṣim and the men above him do not look like dishonest political propagandists, Fussilat concludes that there is effectively “no room” for a fabricator.

The article contains one important insight: **the report really does become securely visible at ʿĀṣim.** Numerous pupils independently transmit it from him. That observation is correct, but the argument then makes the decisive mistake of treating the independent strands **below ʿĀṣim** as verifying the single family strand **above ʿĀṣim**. It does not. The evidence we possess is much more specific. In the extant transmission, the report begins to proliferate at ʿĀṣim. Above him, every located recension in the corpus searched collapses into one family route:

The question is therefore not, “Can we prove that ʿĀṣim was a liar?” The historical question is what independent evidence shows that this particular saying existed before ʿĀṣim? That is the question Fussilat never really answers.

## I. What Fussilat Actually Proves

Fussilat begins with the chains and rightly rejects the possibility that ʿĀṣim’s many pupils independently invented the same saying and falsely attributed it to him. Its conclusion is that “Aasim narrated the hadith as reported.”[^1] That is compatible with the source-critical analysis. The report is transmitted from ʿĀṣim through multiple pupils. The six principal direct pupils collated for the core Ibn ʿUmar recension are:[^18]

- Muʿādh b. Muʿādh;
- Abū al-Naḍr Hāshim b. al-Qāsim;
- Muḥammad b. Yazīd al-Kalāʿī al-Wāsiṭī;
- Abū al-Walīd Hishām al-Ṭayālisī;
- Aḥmad b. ʿAbd Allāh b. Yūnus;
- Bishr b. al-Mufaḍḍal.

The wording varies slightly—**<Arabic>من الناس</Arabic>**, **<Arabic>منهم</Arabic>**, **<Arabic>في الناس</Arabic>**—but the core is stable:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820 (core wording across recensions)">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as … two remain.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش ما بقي … اثنان</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

*Musnad Abī Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī*, no. 2068 (Dār Hajar ed.), does **not** supply an independent route above ʿĀṣim. The printed/transcribed witness reads:

<HadithBlock source="Musnad Abī Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī, 3:462, no. 2068 (Dār Hajar ed.)">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“Abū Dāwūd narrated to us, saying: al-ʿUmarī narrated to us, from ʿĀṣim, from his father, from Ibn ʿUmar, who said: I heard the Messenger of God (peace and blessings be upon him) say: ‘This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two men remain among the people.’”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>حدثنا أبو داود قال حدثنا العمري عن عاصم عن أبيه عن ابن عمر قال سمعت رسول الله صلى الله عليه وسلم يقول لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش ما بقي في الناس رجلان</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

It is therefore another lower branch into the same **ʿĀṣim → father → Ibn ʿUmar** stem, not a bypass through ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb.[^18] This is exactly what a genuine common link looks like: above ʿĀṣim there is one stem; below him the report suddenly fans into multiple students. That gives us the conclusion of ʿĀṣim historically teaching the report. It does not give us the next conclusion of Muḥammad b. Zayd teaching it to ʿĀṣim, Ibn ʿUmar teaching it to Muḥammad, and the Prophet having taught it to Ibn ʿUmar. Those are additional historical claims and require additional control.

This distinction is basic to isnād-cum-matn analysis. In a previous HadithCritic study on the “angels cursing women” traditions, I put the principle this way: the fact that a transmitter could have met a teacher is only a background condition; the real question is whether the report is independently controlled **above the common link**.[^2] A plausible upper chain is not the same thing as an independently evidenced upper chain.

## II. Fussilat Begs the Question at the Beginning

The article says that “the hadith originated with Ibn Umar,” then describes how Ibn ʿUmar passed it to Muḥammad b. Zayd and Muḥammad passed it to ʿĀṣim.[^1] But that is not a conclusion reached from independent evidence. It is simply the isnād rewritten as narrative history. An isnād is itself a historical claim. It cannot function as its own verification. The surviving evidence looks asymmetrical:

Thus the cautious historical statement is not “Ibn ʿUmar said this.” It is:

<ClaimBox title="Central thesis">

**The report is securely datable to ʿĀṣim’s transmission level. Its attribution above him is not independently established.**

</ClaimBox>

This is not proof that the upper isnād is false. It is simply the difference between **what the chain claims** and **what the extant transmission independently demonstrates**.

Fussilat collapsed those two categories from the start.

## III. The Family Test Fussilat Accidentally Points Toward

The most important development in this investigation is the sibling test. Fussilat himself emphasizes that ʿĀṣim sometimes transmitted through his brothers. It uses this as evidence of his honesty. In one famous case, ʿĀṣim says that he had heard a report from his father but had not retained it properly, so his brother Wāqid reminded him of it.[^1] That evidence is indeed important—but not for the reason Fussilat thinks. It tells us that the family transmission network leaves visible traces. Muḥammad b. Zayd did not have only one transmitting son. The relevant family included:

Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar material? They do. And that changes the evidentiary value of their silence on the Quraysh report.

### Wāqid independently transmits the father’s Ibn ʿUmar material

Aḥmad preserves the Farewell-Pilgrimage warning through Wāqid:

<HadithBlock source="Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal, Musnad, 5:123, no. 5578; 5:247, no. 5810">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“Muḥammad b. Jaʿfar narrated to us; Shuʿba narrated to us, from Wāqid b. Muḥammad b. Zayd, who heard his father narrate from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar, from the Prophet, that he said during the Farewell Pilgrimage: ‘Alas for you’—or he said, ‘Woe to you—do not revert to disbelief after me, with some of you striking the necks of others.’”[^4]

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>حدثنا محمد بن جعفر حدثنا شعبة عن واقد بن محمد بن زيد أنه سمع أباه يحدث عن عبد الله بن عمر عن النبي ﷺ أنه قال في حجة الوداع</Arabic>: “<Arabic>ويحكم</Arabic>” <Arabic>أو قال</Arabic>: “<Arabic>ويلكم، لا ترجعوا بعدي كفارا، يضرب بعضكم رقاب بعض</Arabic>”.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The crucial part is not the matn. It is the chain: **Wāqid → Muḥammad b. Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar.**

Wāqid therefore demonstrably possessed independent access to the same paternal Ibn ʿUmar channel that the Quraysh hadith claims to use. He also preserves another family report through the same father-route, an Abū Bakr saying recorded by Ibn Abī Shayba.[^5]

### ʿUmar does the same

ʿUmar b. Muḥammad likewise preserves multiple reports through: **ʿUmar → father → Ibn ʿUmar**

including the report about death being slaughtered between Paradise and Hell, the neighbor report, the “five unseen matters,” and a version of the five-pillars tradition.[^6]

Thus the explanation, "Perhaps only ʿĀṣim preserved his father’s Ibn ʿUmar material” is no longer justifiable. The family really did preserve paternal Ibn ʿUmar material through more than one son. Yet no sibling bypass for the Quraysh report has been located in the corpus; every located witness to this matn still converges on ʿĀṣim.

- the same paternal material can survive through multiple brothers;
- ʿĀṣim’s own route can sometimes preserve his brother Wāqid as an intermediate verifier;

In other words, the family can leave precisely the sort of cross-sibling traces that historical criticism would expect if a report really circulated among multiple sons of Muḥammad b. Zayd. But for:

There is:
- No Wāqid route.
- No ʿUmar route.
- No Zayd route.
- No Abū Bakr route.
- No Nāfiʿ route.
- No Sālim route.
- No ʿAbd Allāh b. Dīnār route.

Every located witness converges on ʿĀṣim. That is a bottleneck Fussilat misses out on. Fussilat is correct that the students below ʿĀṣim are too numerous to dismiss as coordinated invention. But that makes the picture more interesting. Below ʿĀṣim the report spreads widely. Above him it contracts completely.

## V. “ʿĀṣim Was Honest” Does Not Solve This Problem

A large portion of Fussilat’s defense is devoted to ʿĀṣim’s character. He uses longer isnāds elsewhere. He sometimes narrates through brothers. He admits when he did not memorize something. He transmits material critical of rulers. Therefore, the article concludes, he does not fit the profile of a fabricator.[^1] This is much weaker than it sounds. The question is not whether ʿĀṣim was a habitual liar. The question is whether **this particular report** is independently attested before him. Those are completely different claims.

A narrator may preserve a hundred genuine reports and still be the point at which one report becomes propheticized. By **propheticization**, I mean the elevation of an existing maxim, legal or political principle, family report, or interpretive formulation into a saying attributed to the Prophet. Secondary formation does not require the transmitter to be a career forger. It can result from:

- deliberate invention;
- mistaken attribution;
- raising an inherited maxim to the Prophet;
- memory conflation;
- political or legal interpretation becoming quotation;
- retrojection to an earlier family authority;
- pious legitimation.

Hadith criticism often turns this into a moral drama where a narrator is either upright or he is a liar. Historical criticism does not need that binary. This is why the epistemological issue matters. In a previous HadithCritic study I argued:

<QuoteBlock author="HadithCritic" source="The Quranic Epistemology of Verification (Q. 49:6)">

**“Truth and falsehood are properties of claims. They are not properties of people.”**[^8]

</QuoteBlock>

A trustworthy person can be wrong. A morally compromised person can report something true. Reputation changes how carefully we examine a claim; it does not substitute for verification of the claim itself. Even if every positive rijāl judgment about ʿĀṣim is accepted, the upper transmission still requires independent evidence.

## VI. The “Longer Isnād” Argument Is a Non Sequitur

Fussilat places considerable weight on the fact that ʿĀṣim sometimes used longer routes to Ibn ʿUmar even though he allegedly possessed a shorter family route through his father. It argues that a dishonest narrator would have simply used the short route every time.[^1] That only works if the accusation is:

> “ʿĀṣim fabricated his entire corpus and fabricated all his isnāds.”

No serious source-critical argument requires that. Suppose ʿĀṣim genuinely heard one report through: **brother → Nāfiʿ → Ibn ʿUmar**
and another through: **brother → Sālim → Ibn ʿUmar.**

There is no reason those genuine reports would suddenly be shortened merely because another politically distinctive report entered circulation through a family attribution. The existence of real long chains elsewhere proves only that ʿĀṣim preserved real long chains elsewhere. It does not authenticate this specific short chain.

The logic is a non sequitur:
> **He preserved longer isnāds elsewhere**
> therefore
> **this short upper isnād cannot be secondary.**

The conclusion does not follow from the premise.

## VII. Fussilat Reconstructs ʿĀṣim’s Politics Selectively

The article also tries to reconstruct ʿĀṣim’s political beliefs from the reports he transmitted. When a report portrays hypocrisy before rulers, Fussilat treats the matn as evidence that ʿĀṣim himself was no friend of rulers. When a report discourages rebellion against Yazīd, Fussilat explains that ʿĀṣim could simply have transmitted different material from different teachers and need not personally endorse all of it.[^1] Those two moves pull in opposite directions.

If transmitted content is reliable evidence for ʿĀṣim’s personal politics, then contradictory political material must be reconciled. If transmitted content is not necessarily his personal politics because he transmitted whatever he received, then the anti-ruler reports cannot be used as biographical proof that he personally rejected ruler-friendly ideas.

There is an even clearer example. Fussilat admits that ʿĀṣim’s shortened report about hypocrisy does **not** mention Marwān. It nevertheless argues that his audience would have known the context was Umayyad.[^1] That is a contextual historical inference. It may even be a reasonable one. But the same method cannot then be dismissed when applied to the Quraysh report. If we are permitted to interpret a terse political saying in light of its historical environment when that helps the defense, then we are also permitted to ask what **<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش</Arabic>** meant in a world where Khārijites and Ibāḍīs explicitly denied that Quraysh possessed an intrinsic monopoly over **<Arabic>هذا الأمر</Arabic>**.

## VIII. The Real Political Question Was Not “Umayyads or Abbasids?”

This is where Fussilat targets the wrong theory. The article repeatedly asks whether the hadith was fabricated for the Umayyads or Abbasids. It then argues that ʿĀṣim was not sufficiently pro-Umayyad or pro-Abbasid to fit either profile.[^1] But the report does not say “the Umayyads shall rule” or “the Abbasids shall rule.” 

It says:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820 (excerpt)">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will remain in Quraysh.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The most historically relevant opposition is therefore not **Umayyad vs. Abbasid** but **Qurashī monopoly vs. non-Qurashī eligibility.** That dispute is explicitly attested during the time ʿĀṣim’s report becomes visible.

## IX. The Counter-Doctrine Is Explicit by 77 AH

Under the events of 77 AH, al-Ṭabarī preserves a remarkable exchange between Shabīb’s camp and Muṭarrif b. al-Mughīra. The reported Khārijite position states:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Ṭabarī" source="Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, 6:287–290, esp. 288">

“We have chosen for ourselves the one among us who is most acceptable to us and most capable of bearing what has been entrusted to him. So long as he does not alter or change, he is the holder of our authority … We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this affair than the rest of the Arabs … Had they not known that the best people before God are the most pious, and that those most entitled to this affair are the most pious, the best among them, and the most capable of carrying their affairs, they would not have governed the people.”[^9]

**<Arabic>فإنا قد اخترنا لأنفسنا أرضانا فينا، وأشدنا اضطلاعا لما حمل، فما لم يغير ولم يبدل فهو ولي أمرنا ... لأنا لا نرى أن قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر من غيرها من العرب ... ولولا أنهم علموا أن خير الناس عند الله أتقاهم، وأن أولاهم بهذا الأمر أتقاهم وأفضلهم فيهم، وأشدهم اضطلاعا بحمل أمورهم ما تولوا أمور الناس</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

The most important clause is:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Ṭabarī" source="Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, 6:287–290, esp. 288">

“We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to **this authority** than the rest of the Arabs.”

**<Arabic>لا نرى أن قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر من غيرها من العرب</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

Now compare the ʿĀṣim recension:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820 (excerpt)">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“**This authority** will remain in Quraysh.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The wording is almost an ideological mirror image. It refutes the suggestion that there was no obvious historical purpose for a hadith about leadership being permanently Qurashī. Leadership was exactly what was being disputed.

### Al-Balādhurī preserves the same dispute

A closely related recension in al-Balādhurī has Shabīb’s answer:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Balādhurī" source="Jumal min Ansāb al-Ashrāf, 7:399–400">

“We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to this authority than we are. Just as Quraysh chose its best after the death of the Messenger of God, so we may choose our best.”[^10]

**<Arabic>إنّا لا نرى قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر منا، فكما اختارت قريش أفضلها بعد وفاة رسول الله فكذلك لنا أن نختار أفضلنا</Arabic>.**

</QuoteBlock>

The two chronicles should not be counted as two independent eyewitness testimonies; both belong to a related Iraqi historiographical stream. But they preserve the same constitutional argument in two recensions. The key point is that the doctrine is historically intelligible and explicitly articulated.

## X. The Ibāḍī Material Preserves the Same Constitutional Logic

Ann Lambton summarizes the broader Khārijite doctrine: the Khārijites rejected the Sunnī restriction of the imamate to Quraysh; the imam need not belong to any particular family or tribe and could even be a slave, provided he was the best-qualified Muslim of his time and a good ruler.[^11]

Sālim b. Dhakwān’s early Ibāḍī epistle supplies a more formative control. In §128, allegiance is explicitly conditional:

<QuoteBlock author="Sālim b. Dhakwān" source="The Epistle of Sālim Ibn Dhakwān, 141–142, §128">

“We hold that a band of Muslims should pay allegiance to their imam only on [condition that he will wage] jihād in the path of God and that they will obey him in what is approved … Let them therefore appoint to the command their most excellent men and those among them who understand [God’s religion].”[^12]

<div lang="ar" dir="rtl">

ولا نرى النفر من المسلمين أن يبايعوا إمامهم إلا على الجهاد في سبيل الله والطاعة بالمعروف … فيولوا الأمر أفاضلهم وفقهاءهم أنفسهم …

</div>

</QuoteBlock>

The passage does not explicitly say “Quraysh is unnecessary.” It instead lays out the positive alternative logic—merit, religious competence, approved conduct, and conditional allegiance. The received Ibāḍī hadith corpus is especially revealing because it preserves **both sides** of the tension.

Report 45 gives a conditional Quraysh formulation:

<HadithBlock source="Al-Rabīʿ b. Ḥabīb, Musnad, Bāb fī l-wilāya wa-l-imāra, no. 45">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will not cease to be among you, with you as its rulers, so long as you do not introduce innovations; but if you do, God will set over you the worst of His creation, who will strip you as this branch is stripped.”[^13]

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لن يزال هذا الأمر فيكم وأنتم ولاته ما لم تحدثوا، فإذا فعلتم سلط الله عليكم شرار خلقه فيلحونكم كما يلحى هذا القضيب</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

Later in the received collection, report 818 again says Qurashī power remains only so long as they do not introduce innovations contrary to religion, while report 819 commands obedience to a mutilated Abyssinian slave so long as he rules according to God’s Book.[^13] This is important because the Ibāḍī corpus is not simply deleting or suppressing pro-Quraysh material. It preserves it while **qualifying** it.

Gaiser therefore describes these traditions as a “straightforward rejection of Qurayshī claims to authority in favor of piety as the final legitimating criterion of leadership.” He further notes that Ibāḍī jurisprudence rejected Qurashī membership, kinship with the Prophet, and designation (*naṣṣ*) as necessary criteria of legitimate authority.[^14] The later legal tradition becomes even more explicit.

### Al-Kudamī: the Abyssinian ruler in a chapter on the imamate

In *al-Istiqāma*, under **<Arabic>باب الإمامة والقول فيها</Arabic>**—“The Chapter on the Imamate and the Discussion Concerning It”—al-Kudamī writes:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Kudamī" source="al-Istiqāma, 2:119">

“The people of rectitude among the imams agreed on obedience to the just imam so long as he obeys God and His Messenger and acts according to the Book of God and the Sunna of His Prophet…”[^15]

**<Arabic>وأجمع أهل الاستقامة من الأئمة على طاعة إمام العدل، ما أطاع الله ورسوله، وعمل بكتاب الله وسنة نبيه</Arabic> ...**

</QuoteBlock>

He then cites:

<HadithBlock source="Al-Kudamī, al-Istiqāma, 2:119, Bāb al-imāma wa-l-qawl fīhā">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“If a mutilated Abyssinian were appointed over you and established among you the Book of God and my Sunna, hear him and obey him.”[^15]

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لو وليكم حبشي جدع فأقام فيكم كتاب الله وسنتي فاسمعوا له وأطيعوا</Arabic>.**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

The context matters. This is not a chapter about subordinate military officers. It is explicitly about the **imamate**.

### Al-Kindī’s *Muṣannaf*: consultation, consent, and forfeiture

Al-Kindī’s *Muṣannaf*, internally attributed to the Omani jurist Abū ʿAbd Allāh Aḥmad b. ʿAbd Allāh b. Mūsā al-Kindī, gives a more precise formulation than the paraphrase previously used in this article. It states:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Kindī" source="al-Muṣannaf, vol. 7, pt. 10, 332–333, 349">

That is: after the Prophet, the imamate is not contracted for a successor—or an imam removed—except through the consultation of the learned and their consent, sincerely for God.[^16]

**<Arabic>وقد أجمع رأي المسلمين من بعد رسول الله ﷺ: أن الإمامة لا تجب لإمام بعد إمام، أو عزله إلّا عن مشورة أهل العلم، ورضًى منهم على النصح لله</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

The same work states that if an imam abandons the conditions on which he was given allegiance:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Kindī" source="al-Muṣannaf, vol. 7, pt. 10, 332–333, 349">

“if he departs from those conditions on which allegiance was pledged to him, his imamate ceases.”[^16]

**<Arabic>فإذا زايل تلك الشروط التي بويع عليها، زالت إمامته</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

The Ibāḍī logic is:

- the imam is selected;
- merit and religious competence matter;
- obedience is conditional;
- justice matters;
- consultation and consent matter;
- the office can be forfeited;
- non-Qurashī ancestry does not invalidate rule.

The ʿĀṣim recension says:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two people remain.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش ما بقي من الناس اثنان</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

- There is no piety condition.
- No justice condition.
- No consultation condition.
- No forfeiture clause.

The authority remains in Quraysh as long as human society itself remains. That is precisely why the hadith works so well as an unconditional counter-formulation.

## XI. The 129–130 AH Crisis Makes the Dispute Concrete

The constitutional dispute did not remain theoretical. By 129–130 AH, the Ibāḍī movement was operating under a non-Qurashī imam, ʿAbd Allāh b. Yaḥyā al-Kindī, while Abū Ḥamza al-Shārī’s forces seized Mecca, defeated the Medinans at Qudayd, and occupied Medina, where ʿĀṣim himself resided.[^19]

> **The 129–130 AH movement put a non-Qurashī model of supreme leadership into practice in the Ḥijāz during ʿĀṣim’s lifetime.**

The direct-pupil chronology sharpens the picture further.

Three principal branches are necessarily post-130 because the pupils had not yet been born. Muʿādh was only a child. The visible multi-pupil proliferation of the report therefore belongs overwhelmingly to ʿĀṣim’s later teaching career.[^20]

## XIII. Fussilat’s Own Chronology Does Not Refute the Theory

Fussilat argues that ʿĀṣim continued transmitting the report after the Umayyads fell, and therefore an Umayyad motive is unlikely.[^1] That objection works only against an Umayyad-specific theory.

- The hadith is not Umayyad-specific.
- It does not say Banū Umayya.
- It does not say Banū al-ʿAbbās.
- It does not say Banū Hāshim.
- It establishes a wider constitutional category: **Quraysh**.

That makes the report remarkably portable.
- An Umayyad can use it.
- An Abbasid can use it.
- An ʿAlid can use it.
- A Zubayrid can use it.
- An ʿUmarid can use it.

The report excludes one category above all others, **a non-Qurashī claimant to supreme authority**. Thus the fact that the saying survived the Umayyad-Abbasid transition is not a problem for the counter-tradition theory. It is exactly what we should expect from a branch-neutral doctrine of Qurashī monopoly.

## XIV. Criticizing Rulers Does Not Mean Rejecting Qurashī Exclusivity

Fussilat repeatedly points to reports in which ʿĀṣim transmits criticism of rulers. It uses these to argue that he was not politically motivated to create a pro-Qurashī hadith.[^1]

That confuses two entirely different questions.

A person can believe:

> “This ruler is corrupt.”

while also believing:

> “Only members of this constitutional class may legitimately hold the office.”

There is no contradiction. Someone can criticize a king and still support monarchy. Someone can oppose an Umayyad and still believe the caliph must be Qurashī. The Khārijite/Ibāḍī doctrine itself shows why the distinction matters. Their theory ties legitimacy to moral performance rather than genealogy. The whole debate is about whether bad conduct can defeat inherited or tribal claims to authority. So "anti-ruler material" tells us little about whether ʿĀṣim accepted or rejected Qurashī eligibility.

## XV. The Ibn Masʿūd Report Does Even Less Work

Fussilat cites another political report attributed through ʿĀṣim in which resisting rulers with hand, tongue, and heart is praised. It immediately concedes that there is a break in the chain.[^1] Yet that same broken report is then used to reconstruct ʿĀṣim’s political psychology. This is methodologically unstable. If a broken isnād makes a report weak evidence for the historical speech of Ibn Masʿūd or the Prophet, then its matn is also weak evidence for a precise reconstruction of ʿĀṣim’s personal ideology. And even if we grant that ʿĀṣim taught it, the point remains irrelevant. Resistance to an unjust ruler does not logically imply rejection of a Qurashī requirement for the office itself.

## XVI. Muḥammad b. Zayd’s Supposed Anti-Umayyad Profile Does Not Answer the Question

Fussilat next attempts to clear Muḥammad b. Zayd by reconstructing him as anti-Umayyad. It points to his transmission of criticism of rulers, a pro-Ahl al-Bayt report, his knowledge of Ibn al-Zubayr, and his presence in the era of the Umayyad attack on Mecca.[^1] None of those facts entails:

> “Muḥammad b. Zayd could not have believed in Qurashī political exclusivity.”

A person could oppose Umayyad conduct, respect Ahl al-Bayt, admire Ibn al-Zubayr, and still believe that the supreme office belonged only to Quraysh.

These are different axes: **dynastic preference** ≠ **constitutional eligibility**.

This is one of the most persistent false connections in the Fussilat article. It repeatedly treats hostility toward a particular ruler or dynasty as if it were evidence against a tribal rule of eligibility. It is not.

## XVII. Ibn ʿUmar’s Piety Is a Red Herring Until the Report Reaches Him

The article then turns to Ibn ʿUmar’s sincerity, devotion, criticism of al-Ḥajjāj, opposition to the killing of al-Ḥusayn, and lack of personal political ambition.[^1] Those facts may be relevant to Ibn ʿUmar’s biography. They are not independent evidence that this hadith reaches him.

Before asking:

> “Would Ibn ʿUmar lie?”

we first have to establish:

> **“Is this report independently recoverable from Ibn ʿUmar?”**

At present, it is not. The only route to Ibn ʿUmar is the same family stem that becomes visible at ʿĀṣim.

Thus the argument:

> Ibn ʿUmar was sincere → Ibn ʿUmar would not lie → therefore this report is authentic to Ibn ʿUmar

contains a hidden premise:

> **the report has already been established as Ibn ʿUmar’s.**

That is the very premise under investigation.

## XVIII. “He Had No Chance of Becoming Caliph” Is Not a Historical Rebuttal

Fussilat argues that ʿĀṣim and his family were not close to achieving political power, so generic pro-Qurashī motivation becomes meaningless.[^1] This assumes that ideology must generate immediate personal material gain. That's not necesarily true.

People defend:

- tribal privilege;
- class privilege;
- dynastic principles;
- legal schools;
- ethnic hierarchies;
- institutional authority;
- constitutional restrictions

without personally expecting to occupy the highest office protected by that system. ʿĀṣim was not simply “some random man from Quraysh.” Al-Mizzī identifies him as:

<QuoteBlock author="Al-Mizzī" source="Tahdhīb al-Kamāl, 13:542–543, no. 3027">

ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad b. Zayd b. ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb, al-ʿUmarī, al-Madanī.[^17]

**<Arabic>عاصم بن محمد بن زيد بن عبد الله بن عمر بن الخطاب، العمري المدني</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

He belonged to a prestigious Qurashī-ʿUmarid family. The fact that he personally had little chance of becoming caliph does not make tribal constitutional ideology meaningless.

## XIX. Why Leadership? Because Leadership Was the Dispute

Fussilat asks a revealing question. If ʿĀṣim were simply biased in favor of Quraysh, why fabricate something about **leadership** rather than knowledge, piety, or virtue?[^1]

The answer is contained in the historical sources:

> **Because leadership was the point under dispute.**

Shabīb’s position does not say Quraysh are not more knowledgeable.

It says:

<QuoteBlock author="Shabīb" source="Quoted in al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, 6:288">

“We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to **this authority**.”

**<Arabic>لا نرى أن قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

The Ibāḍī sources are about who may be imam, how the imam is selected, what conditions legitimate him, and when he can lose office. So if an opposing tradition were to answer that constitutional doctrine, **leadership is exactly what we would expect it to address.** Fussilat presents the specificity of the matn as a problem for the political theory. Historically, it is one of the strongest reasons to take the political setting seriously.

## XX. The Lexical Mirror: **<Arabic>هذا الأمر</Arabic>**

The most striking verbal correspondence should not be overstated, but it should not be ignored either. The Khārijite-associated position says:

<QuoteBlock author="Shabīb" source="Quoted in al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk, 6:288">

“We do not consider Quraysh more entitled to **this authority**.”

**<Arabic>لا نرى أن قريشا أحق بهذا الأمر</Arabic>**

</QuoteBlock>

The ʿĀṣim recension says:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820 (excerpt)">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“**This authority** will remain in Quraysh.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

One denies an intrinsic genealogical right. The other makes genealogical possession effectively permanent. Lexical overlap by itself proves nothing. **<Arabic>هذا الأمر</Arabic>** is ordinary political vocabulary. But when the same phrase occurs inside opposing constitutional formulations, and one of those formulations is transmitted only through a second-century common link living in the aftermath of that controversy, the parallel becomes historically relevant.

## XXI. The Qurʾān Section Does Not Solve the Provenance Problem

Fussilat ends by arguing that Qurashī political leadership does not necessarily contradict Q49:13. It then points to Qurʾānic examples in which God selects particular families for prophethood or grants special favor to Israel.[^1] Even if this entire theological argument is granted, it does not answer the historical question. A report can be **theologically possible** and still be **historically secondary**.

The logic:

> God can choose a lineage for a particular role

therefore

> God chose Quraysh for perpetual supreme political authority

is invalid without independent evidence for the second proposition.

The examples of Adam, Noah, Abraham’s family, ʿImrān’s family, and Israel demonstrate that God **can** explicitly choose families. They do not demonstrate that He **did** establish an eternal Qurashī monopoly over the caliphate.

That is a modal fallacy:

**possible ≠ actual.**

The hadith itself remains the evidence for the specific claim—and therefore its provenance still has to be established.

## XXII. The “Magical Fabricator” Is a Straw Man

Fussilat concludes that critics need a mysterious or “magical” fabricator because no one in the chain fits the profile it has constructed.[^1] But no magical figure is required. The transmission diagram gives us an obvious historical location:

**ʿĀṣim b. Muḥammad.**

If we ask:

**Where does the report first become multiply recoverable?**

ʿĀṣim.

**Where does it first fan into numerous independent pupils?**

ʿĀṣim.

**Does another son of Muḥammad b. Zayd independently preserve this saying?**

No.

**Do those same brothers independently preserve other paternal Ibn ʿUmar material?**

Yes.

None of this requires "magical thinking." It is the sort of asymmetry common-link analysis is designed to identify.

## XXIII. What the Evidence Allows Us to Say—and What It Does Not

We can say with strong confidence:

- the Quraysh “two remain” report is securely attested at ʿĀṣim’s level;
- it proliferates through multiple pupils below him;
- above him, all located versions collapse into the same family stem;
- other brothers demonstrably preserve Muḥammad b. Zayd → Ibn ʿUmar material;
- no sibling bypass has been found for this saying;
- the visible fan-out is largely post-130 AH;
- a directly contrary Khārijite doctrine about **<Arabic>هذا الأمر</Arabic>** is explicitly attested during ʿĀṣim’s lifetime;
- Ibāḍī sources preserve the same broader non-genealogical, conditional theory of legitimacy;
- non-Qurashī supreme leadership was put into practice in the Ḥijāz in 129–130 AH.

The strongest historical reconstruction is that ʿĀṣim constructed this tradition in response to Kharijite doctrine during the Kharijite conquest of Medina and Mecca—a period in which ʿĀṣim was alive, actively transmitting hadith, and able to witness these events firsthand.

## The Strongest Historical Reconstruction

The report is historically secure at ʿĀṣim. Above him the chain is uncorroborated. The family itself supplies a control group because Wāqid and ʿUmar really do preserve other material from Muḥammad b. Zayd through Ibn ʿUmar. For this politically distinctive saying, however, only ʿĀṣim’s route survives. Its broad dissemination belongs predominantly of the time of his career. At the same time, Khārijite and Ibāḍī political discourse had already made the relevant constitutional issue explicit: Quraysh did not possess an intrinsic superior right to **al-amr**; piety, competence, consultation, and justice were the operative criteria. The 129–130 revolt then placed that alternative model into practice in the Ḥijāz.

The ʿĀṣim recension answers that world with extraordinary economy:

<HadithBlock source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, nos. 3501 and 7140; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, no. 1820">
<div class="hadith-block__translation">

“This authority will remain in Quraysh as long as two people remain.”

</div>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">

**<Arabic>لا يزال هذا الأمر في قريش ما بقي من الناس اثنان</Arabic>**

</div>
</HadithBlock>

<VerdictBox title="Historical reconstruction">

ʿĀṣim’s Qurayshi narrative was intended to counter this Ibadi/Kharijite doctrine and preserve the office as an exclusively Qurayshi institution. That is why ʿĀṣim remains the earliest securely recoverable point of broad dissemination and the strongest candidate for the report’s formulation.

</VerdictBox>

## XXIV. Summary

Fussilat’s argument depends on a series of assumptions that do not survive a source-critical analysis.

First, it treats the isnād’s upper half as established history before independently testing it. Second, it takes multiple students below ʿĀṣim and incorrectly lets them authenticate the single strand above him. Third, it turns ʿĀṣim’s general character into proof for a particular report. Fourth, it mistakes anti-ruler sentiment for evidence against Qurashī constitutional exclusivity. Fifth, it restricts political motive to pro-Umayyad or pro-Abbasid partisanship and therefore misses the more basic constitutional dispute over whether Quraysh possessed an intrinsic monopoly on supreme authority. Sixth, it asks why anyone would fabricate something specifically about leadership when leadership is exactly what the opposing Khārijite/Ibāḍī doctrine was about.
