This article is a case study in how a single disputed transmission can become a moral weapon. The report under review tells a woman to answer her husband’s sexual summons even if she is occupied at the oven. Later polemical and legal uses of the report often treat it as a clean prophetic command, but the transmission does not behave like a broad communal memory. It behaves like a narrow Yamāmī family report that becomes stronger in social effect as it passes through later collectors and jurists.

<ClaimBox title="Central Claim">
The tannūr report should not be treated as an independent prophetic rule about marital access. Its recoverable lines converge on Qays b. Ṭalq from his father, and the strongest branch places the coercive wording in a path whose decisive lower link, Qays, was disputed by major critics. The matn also changes in a revealing direction: one branch says the husband should go to his wife, while the more cited branch makes the wife come or respond to him.
</ClaimBox>

## The report in question

  arabic="حَدَّثَنَا هَنَّادٌ، حَدَّثَنَا مُلَازِمُ بْنُ عَمْرٍو، قَالَ حَدَّثَنِي عَبْدُ اللهِ بْنُ بَدْرٍ، عَنْ قَيْسِ بْنِ طَلْقٍ، عَنْ أَبِيهِ طَلْقِ بْنِ عَلِيٍّ، قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللهِ ﷺ: إِذَا الرَّجُلُ دَعَا زَوْجَتَهُ لِحَاجَتِهِ فَلْتَأْتِهِ، وَإِنْ كَانَتْ عَلَى التَّنُّورِ. قَالَ أَبُو عِيسَى: هَذَا حَدِيثٌ حَسَنٌ غَرِيبٌ."
  translation="Hannād narrated to us: Mulāzim b. ʿAmr narrated to us: ʿAbd Allāh b. Badr narrated to me from Qays b. Ṭalq, from his father Ṭalq b. ʿAlī, who said: The Messenger of God, peace and blessings be upon him, said: ‘When a man calls his wife for his need, let her come to him, even if she is at the oven.’ Abū ʿĪsā al-Tirmidhī said: ‘This is a ḥasan gharīb report.’"
/>

The phrase “ḥasan gharīb” already tells the reader that this is not a mass-transmitted marital ethic. It is a report with a narrow route that later received legal and moral weight far beyond the strength of its historical base. The issue is not that every transmitter above Qays is automatically rejected. The issue is that the report’s moral burden rests on a disputed individual link, and the matn itself shows pressure toward a more coercive reading.

The table matters because a list of six citations can look like six independent witnesses. The transmission map shows something more limited. The major printed sources are mostly different exits from the same narrow corridor. Several collectors preserve the report, but the report keeps returning to Qays b. Ṭalq from his father. A repeated report is not automatically a multiplied report.

<ContextNote title="Why the wording matters">
The report has two social forms. In the Muḥammad b. Jābir branch, the husband is the grammatical subject: if he wants something from his wife, “let him go to her.” In the Mulāzim branch, the wife becomes the obligated subject: if he calls her, “let her come” or “let her respond.” Even if one does not accuse a specific narrator of intentional fabrication, the variant movement shows how a private desire can be transformed into a command placed upon the woman.
</ContextNote>

## Qays b. Ṭalq is the pressure point

The criticism should focus where the chain actually narrows. Mulāzim b. ʿAmr and ʿAbd Allāh b. Badr are generally treated positively in the rijāl material. The problem is Qays b. Ṭalq. He is not a companion, and the report’s authority depends on accepting his claim from his father. Sunni critics themselves disagreed over whether he could carry this kind of legal burden.

This is where the popular apologetic response often becomes circular. The report is treated as acceptable because later compilers cited it, and those citations are then treated as proof that the report should shape marriage ethics. The actual rijāl profile is mixed. Once the report is placed beside the Qurʾān’s standard for marriage, the burden of proof becomes even heavier.

## The Qurʾānic marital standard is not coercion

The Qurʾān speaks about marriage in terms of kindness, mutual protection, and affection. It does not frame a wife as an instrument obligated to abandon her immediate circumstance whenever the husband expresses desire.

A report that can be used to suspend a woman’s agency in the most intimate part of marriage needs extremely strong evidence. This report has the opposite profile: a narrow transmission, a disputed common link, variant wording, and a social afterlife that presses the wife into unilateral availability.

<ContextNote title="What this case actually proves">
This case does not require the claim that Qays sat down and consciously invented the report for personal lust. The more careful conclusion is that the earliest recoverable transmission already sits in a narrow patriarchal corridor, and the stronger later wording is the one that intensifies the wife’s obligation. Hadith criticism should be able to notice that pattern instead of hiding it behind the prestige of later compilation.
</ContextNote>

## Why this became useful

A short, memorable report like this is convenient because it turns a social expectation into a prophetic sentence. It removes negotiation from marriage, treats male desire as legally urgent, and makes refusal appear religiously suspect. That is why its transmission history matters. The report did not merely preserve a sentence; it helped manufacture a moral atmosphere in which one spouse’s demand could be sacralized over the other spouse’s dignity.

The Qurʾān warns that false religious speech becomes attractive precisely because it is useful to people who want authority. That warning applies especially when a report gives one group power over another.

<QuranVerse verse="6:112-113" />

<VerdictBox title="Verdict">
The tannūr report should be rejected as a basis for coercive marital doctrine. Its chains do not provide broad independent attestation, its decisive link is disputed, and its matn variants show a movement toward placing the burden on the wife. Whatever one makes of Qays b. Ṭalq personally, the report is too narrow and too morally dangerous to override the Qurʾān’s language of kindness, mercy, and mutual garment-like protection.
</VerdictBox>
