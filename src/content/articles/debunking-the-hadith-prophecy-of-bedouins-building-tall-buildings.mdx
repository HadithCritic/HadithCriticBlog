Among the reports that hadith apologists offer as proof of the corpus's "miraculous" foreknowledge, one of the most popular is the sign that "barefoot, naked shepherds will compete in raising tall buildings." The argument writes itself: impoverished Bedouins of the desert could never have been expected to build skyscrapers, so the rise of Dubai and Riyadh fulfills a prediction no human could have made. The conclusion is that the Prophet knew the future, and that the corpus carrying his words is therefore trustworthy.

The reading inverts the report. The line is not a forecast of a glorious future. It is a complaint about a present, and the present it complains about is the wealth that the Arab conquests dumped into the garrison cities of Iraq. Its natural home is the literature of *zuhd*, the ascetic preaching that warned the new Muslim rich against pouring their conquest spoils into stone and mortar. The Qurʾān itself frames building-rivalry not as a marvel to be predicted but as a vice to be condemned:

<QuranVerse verse="26:128-129" />

When the prophet Hūd rebukes the people of ʿĀd, the charge is precisely that they "build on every hill a mansion for vanity's sake" and "set up buildings as if they will last forever." Lavish construction is the scriptural emblem of worldly heedlessness. A saying that lists "competing in tall buildings" among the omens of moral decline is not predicting the Burj Khalifa; it is preaching the same sermon as Sūrat al-Takāthur. Read in that light, four things become clear, and this article takes them in order: the report fails the apologists' own test for a genuine prophecy; its wording will not hold still; its two supposedly independent Companion chains collapse into a single Iraqi source; and what it actually targets is the documented building-and-luxury boom of Umayyad Iraq that the Kūfan ascetics were already lamenting in the mouth of Khabbāb b. al-Aratt.

# The Faulty Logic and Fallacies

Before reaching the real origin of the report, it is worth meeting the apologetic on its own ground. The defenders of the prophecy supply four criteria a prediction must satisfy to count as genuine:

1. It must be unintuitive, not something easily expected to happen.
2. It must carry risk, meaning it is falsifiable and could have turned out false.
3. It must be specific, not so vague that any later event can be read into it.
4. A true prophet of God cannot get a prophecy wrong.

The "tall buildings" report fails the first three on its own terms.

**It is not unintuitive.** Tall and elaborate building was never alien to the Arabs. The Nabataeans, desert Arabs themselves, carved al-Khazneh at Petra and the monumental tombs of al-Ḥijr (Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ) centuries before Islam, the latter named in the Qurʾān. The Quraysh ran caravans into Byzantine Syria, Ghassānid and Lakhmid courts, Ḥimyarite Yemen, and Sasanian Iraq, and the tradition itself has the Prophet travelling these routes as a merchant. A man who had seen the palaces of Hira and the churches of Syria predicting that newly enriched Arabs would build big is not exercising supernatural insight; he is stating the obvious consequence of sudden wealth.

**It carries no real risk, because it is not falsifiable.** The report fixes no date, no place, no tribe, and no mechanism. Any group of Arabs that grows wealthy and builds at any point in fourteen centuries can be slotted in after the fact. Had the Gulf stayed poor, the "prophecy" would simply have been attached to some other moment of Arab prosperity.

**It is not specific, and its own commentators read it as already fulfilled.** Far from pointing to the twenty-first century, the classical tradition understood the sign to describe something close to the Prophet's own era. Ibn Ḥajar al-ʿAsqalānī says so plainly in *Fatḥ al-Bārī*:

The tradition's own master commentator does not reach for a future Arabia. He says the sign already happened, near the Prophet's own time, and the only thing it describes is people building competitively higher and more ornately than one another. That is not a window into 2024. It is a description of the building boom of the first Islamic century. Why a first-century Muslim moralist would single that out, and project it onto the lips of the Prophet, is the question the rest of this article answers.

The herd cannot decide whether it is camels, black beasts, or sheep. The destitute alternately become "heads of the people," "kings of the earth," or are described as "deaf and dumb." The building clause itself sometimes drops out, replaced by "kings of the earth." Even the verb shifts, from "compete in height" (yataṭāwalūn) to "vie" (yatabārawn). This is the behavior of a circulating homiletic motif that each narrator paraphrased to taste, not the behavior of a fixed prophetic sentence preserved word for word. ## Two "independent" Companions, one Iraqi source

The apologetic leans on the claim that this sign reaches us through two separate Companions, Abū Hurayra and ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb, which would make it multiply attested. It does carry two Companion names. But isnād analysis shows that the two are not independent witnesses to anything. Each "family" of chains narrows to a single Iraqi successor, and the two successors were exact contemporaries working the same material.

### Family one: Abū Hurayra, through the Kūfan Abū Zurʿa

Every prophetic chain in the Abū Hurayra family passes through one man, **Abū Zurʿa b. ʿAmr b. Jarīr al-Bajalī**, a Kūfan successor and grandson of the Companion Jarīr b. ʿAbd Allāh al-Bajalī. Below him the report fans out into Bukhārī, Muslim, Ibn Mājah, Nasāʾī, Aḥmad, and Ibn Ḥibbān through three of his Kūfan students. Above him there is a single strand to Abū Hurayra.

### Family two: ʿUmar, through the Baṣran Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar

The ʿUmar family looks independent on paper, with a chain climbing through Ibn ʿUmar to ʿUmar himself. But it too narrows to one man, **Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar**, a Baṣran who became the judge of Merv. And his version is not a bare report. In every single recension it is welded to a frame that gives the game away: the famous story of Yaḥyā setting out with Ḥumayd b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān to confront Ibn ʿUmar over the *qadar* heresy of **Maʿbad al-Juhanī**, "the first to speak about qadar in Baṣra."

That frame is decisive for dating. Maʿbad al-Juhanī's qadar agitation, and his execution after the revolt of Ibn al-Ashʿath, belong to the 80s AH. A report whose narrative casing is a Baṣran heresy dispute of the 80s cannot be an untouched dictation from the Prophet's majlis. It is a composition of that generation, in that city, attached to the Gabriel scene.

### The rijāl: contemporaries, not corroborators

Both men sit in the third ṭabaqa, the middle successors. They are not a Companion and an unrelated Companion separated by chance; they are two contemporaries of the same generation, in the two neighboring garrison cities, both circulating the identical sign-cluster inside the identical "stranger questions the Prophet about Islam, Īmān, Iḥsān, and the Hour" frame. Yaḥyā in particular is flagged by Ibn Ḥajar as a transmitter who narrated *mursal*, that is, comfortable supplying a chain across a gap. He is also, in his own report, the protagonist who personally manufactures the ascent: it is he who "set out and met Ibn ʿUmar" and drew the saying up to ʿUmar.

<ContextNote title="What the two diagrams mean together">
This is not two independent attestations of a prediction. It is a single Iraqi tradition with two faces. The Kūfan face (Abū Zurʿa, attributed to Abū Hurayra) carries the sign in the bare Gabriel frame. The Baṣran face (Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar, attributed to ʿUmar) carries the same sign in the same frame, but bolted onto an anti-qadarī polemic of the 80s AH and routed to a second, prestigious Companion. The most economical reading is the one the matn forces: the floating Kūfan sign-material reached Baṣra, where Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar gave it a fresh isnād up to ʿUmar through Ibn ʿUmar, producing the appearance of independent corroboration where there was only one source. Two names, one origin, no prophecy.
</ContextNote>

## What the saying was actually fighting

If the sign is not a forecast, what was the moralist who first told it reacting to? The answer is sitting in the canon, in the mouth of a Companion who settled in the same Kūfa that produced Abū Zurʿa: **Khabbāb b. al-Aratt**, an early Muslim, a Badr veteran, and a man who lived to see the conquest wealth arrive. His lament is the prose key to the "tall buildings" sign.

<HadithBlock label="Khabbāb: 'we found nowhere to put it but the dust'" source="Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5672 (Ādam ← Shuʿba ← Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid ← Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim ← Khabbāb)">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    دَخَلْنَا عَلَى خَبَّابٍ نَعُودُهُ وَقَدِ اكْتَوَى سَبْعَ كَيَّاتٍ، فَقَالَ: إِنَّ أَصْحَابَنَا الَّذِينَ سَلَفُوا مَضَوْا وَلَمْ تَنْقُصْهُمُ الدُّنْيَا، وَإِنَّا أَصَبْنَا مَا لَا نَجِدُ لَهُ مَوْضِعًا إِلَّا التُّرَابَ، وَلَوْلَا أَنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ نَهَانَا أَنْ نَدْعُوَ بِالْمَوْتِ لَدَعَوْتُ بِهِ. ثُمَّ أَتَيْنَاهُ مَرَّةً أُخْرَى وَهُوَ يَبْنِي حَائِطًا لَهُ، فَقَالَ: إِنَّ الْمُسْلِمَ لَيُؤْجَرُ فِي كُلِّ شَيْءٍ يُنْفِقُهُ إِلَّا فِي شَيْءٍ يَجْعَلُهُ فِي هَذَا التُّرَابِ.
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "We came to Khabbāb to visit him, and he had been cauterized seven times. He said: 'Our companions who went before us departed, and the world did not diminish their reward; but we have acquired wealth we find nowhere to put except the dust. Were it not that the Prophet forbade us to pray for death, I would pray for it.' Then we came to him another time while he was building a wall of his, and he said: 'The Muslim is rewarded for everything he spends, except for something he puts into this dust.'"
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

This is the whole thesis in one report. The conquests have made the early Muslims wealthy past the point of knowing what to do with it ("we have acquired wealth we find nowhere to put except the dust"), and the one outlet is building, which Khabbāb is shown literally doing ("while he was building a wall of his") even as he condemns it as the one expenditure that earns no reward. The "dust" (turāb) is mortar and foundation. Building is figured as the sink into which surplus conquest wealth is poured, and figured as spiritually worthless. The barefoot shepherds "competing in tall buildings" are the same complaint dramatized as an omen.

And the chain is the giveaway. Trace it: **Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid ← Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim ← Khabbāb**. Every link is Kūfan, and two of the three carry the same tribal nisba, al-Bajalī, as Abū Zurʿa, the common link of the building-sign itself. The Khabbāb wealth-lament and the building-sign were circulating in the same Kūfan ascetic milieu, in the same families.

There is even a tell that the building-condemnation was an editorial layer added to the Kūfan tradition rather than its original core. In Muslim's recension of the very same chain, only the "do not wish for death" portion survives; the building clause is absent:

<HadithBlock label="The same chain, without the building clause" source="Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2681a (Abū Bakr b. Abī Shayba ← ʿAbd Allāh b. Idrīs ← Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid ← Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim ← Khabbāb)">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    دَخَلْنَا عَلَى خَبَّابٍ وَقَدِ اكْتَوَى سَبْعَ كَيَّاتٍ فِي بَطْنِهِ، فَقَالَ: لَوْلَا أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ نَهَانَا أَنْ نَدْعُوَ بِالْمَوْتِ لَدَعَوْتُ بِهِ.
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "We came to Khabbāb, and he had been cauterized seven times on his belly. He said: 'Were it not that the Messenger of God forbade us to pray for death, I would pray for it.'"
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

So even within the Kūfan transmission, the anti-building moral was a clause that some narrators raised and others did not. The Kūfan tradents took a Companion's deathbed remark and built onto it the lesson that worldly construction is the one valueless expenditure. That is the workshop in which the "tall buildings" sign was made.

### The wider ascetic chorus

Khabbāb is not alone. The same anti-building sentiment runs through a cluster of *zuhd* reports, where the Prophet is made to frown on construction itself:

<HadithBlock label="'Every building is a burden'" source="Sunan Ibn Mājah 4161 (ḥasan); cf. Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5237 (← Anas b. Mālik)">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    مَرَّ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ بِقُبَّةٍ عَلَى بَابِ رَجُلٍ مِنَ الْأَنْصَارِ، فَقَالَ: «مَا هَذِهِ؟» قَالُوا: قُبَّةٌ بَنَاهَا فُلَانٌ. فَقَالَ: «كُلُّ مَالٍ يَكُونُ هَكَذَا فَهُوَ وَبَالٌ عَلَى صَاحِبِهِ يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ». فَبَلَغَ الْأَنْصَارِيَّ ذَلِكَ فَوَضَعَهَا … وَفِي رواية: «أَمَا إِنَّ كُلَّ بِنَاءٍ وَبَالٌ عَلَى صَاحِبِهِ إِلَّا مَا لَا بُدَّ مِنْهُ».
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    The Messenger of God passed a dome over the door of a man of the Anṣār and asked, "What is this?" They said, "A dome so-and-so built." He said, "Every wealth spent like this is a burden on its owner on the Day of Resurrection." When the Anṣārī heard, he tore it down … In another wording: "Every building is a burden on its owner, except what is indispensable."
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

The pattern is unmistakable. A first-century ascetic current, strongest in Iraq, taught that building beyond bare need was a spiritual liability. It generated the dome story, the "every building is a burden" maxim, the Khabbāb wealth-lament, and, dressed as an omen of the Hour, the "barefoot shepherds competing in tall buildings." These are not separate facts about the future; they are one moral campaign against one target.

## The target was real: the building boom of Umayyad Iraq

That target is not hypothetical. The conquests really did pour the treasure of two empires into Iraq, and the new Muslim elite really did spend it on monumental building, exactly in the cities where these reports were forged. The ascetics were not predicting a building craze; they were watching one.

**The spoils.** When the Muslims took Ctesiphon (al-Madāʾin), they stood before the white palace and arch of Khusraw and saw wealth on a scale they had no category for. The chroniclers record the carpet and crown of Khusraw carried to ʿUmar in Medina, and treasuries counted in the billions of dirhams. This is the windfall Khabbāb meant by "wealth we find nowhere to put except the dust."

<HadithBlock label="'This is the White Palace of Khusraw'" source="Ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī, Masālik al-Abṣār; cf. al-Ṭabarī and al-Balādhurī on the spoils of al-Madāʾin">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    وَلَمَّا شَاهَدَ الْمُسْلِمُونَ إِيوَانَ كِسْرَى كَبَّرُوا، وَقَالُوا: هَذَا أَبْيَضُ كِسْرَى … وَوُجِدَ فِي بُيُوتِ أَمْوَالِ كِسْرَى مَا لَا يُحْصَى مِنَ الْأَمْوَالِ وَالْجَوَاهِرِ وَالتُّحَفِ.
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "When the Muslims beheld the arch of Khusraw they cried 'God is greatest' and said, 'This is the White Palace of Khusraw' … and in the treasuries of Khusraw was found wealth, jewels, and treasures beyond counting."
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

**The garrison cities turn to stone.** Baṣra and Kūfa began as encampments of reed and clay. Within a generation the conquest money rebuilt them in baked brick. al-Balādhurī records that Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī first built the mosque and governor's residence (Dār al-Imāra) of Baṣra in mud-brick, clay, and thatch, and that **Ziyād b. Abīhi** then rebuilt them in gypsum and fired brick, monumental enough that al-Ḥajjāj later wished to strike Ziyād's name off the building.

<HadithBlock label="From reed and clay to gypsum and fired brick" source="al-Balādhurī, Futūḥ al-Buldān, on the building of Baṣra and its Dār al-Imāra">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    وَبَنَى أَبُو مُوسَى الْأَشْعَرِيُّ الْمَسْجِدَ وَدَارَ الْإِمَارَةِ بِاللَّبِنِ وَالطِّينِ وَسَقَفَهَا بِالْعُشْبِ … [ثُمَّ] بَنَى زِيَادٌ دَارَ الْإِمَارَةِ بِالْبَصْرَةِ … فَلَمَّا قَدِمَ الْحَجَّاجُ بْنُ يُوسُفَ الْعِرَاقَ أُخْبِرَ أَنَّ زِيَادًا ابْتَنَى دَارَ الْإِمَارَةِ، فَأَرَادَ أَنْ يُزِيلَ اسْمَهُ عَنْهَا، فَهَمَّ بِبِنَائِهَا بِجِصٍّ وَآجُرٍّ.
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī built the mosque and the governor's residence of mud-brick and clay, and roofed them with thatch … Then Ziyād built the governor's residence of Baṣra [anew] … and when al-Ḥajjāj b. Yūsuf came to Iraq and was told that Ziyād had built the residence, he wished to erase his name from it, and resolved to rebuild it in gypsum and fired brick."
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

**The palaces of the governors.** The most spectacular case is al-Ḥajjāj's green-domed palace at the new city of Wāsiṭ, which delighted him "as no building ever had." The episode is preserved with a punchline that no commentary could improve: a captured Khārijite, brought before al-Ḥajjāj and gazing at the construction, throws the Qurʾān's own anti-building verse in his face.

<HadithBlock label="A Khārijite recites Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ at al-Ḥajjāj's green dome" source="Ibn ʿAsākir, Tārīkh Dimashq; cf. al-Tadhkira al-Ḥamdūniyya, on al-Ḥajjāj's Khaḍrāʾ at Wāsiṭ">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    أُتِيَ الْحَجَّاجُ بِرَجُلٍ مِنَ الْخَوَارِجِ وَهُوَ فِي خَضْرَاءِ وَاسِطٍ، فَلَمَّا مَثُلَ بَيْنَ يَدَيْهِ وَنَظَرَ إِلَى بُنْيَانِهِ فَقَالَ: ﴿أَتَبْنُونَ بِكُلِّ رِيعٍ آيَةً تَعْبَثُونَ وَتَتَّخِذُونَ مَصَانِعَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَخْلُدُونَ﴾ … وَكَانَ الْحَجَّاجُ قَدْ بَنَى خَضْرَاءَ وَاسِطٍ فَأَعْجَبَتْهُ مَا لَمْ يُعْجِبْهُ بِنَاءٌ قَطُّ، فَقَالَ لِمَنْ حَوْلَهُ: كَيْفَ تَرَوْنَ قُبَّتِي هَذِهِ؟
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    A man of the Khārijites was brought before al-Ḥajjāj while he was in the Green Palace of Wāsiṭ. When he stood before him and looked at his building, he recited: "Do you build on every hill a monument for vanity's sake, and take for yourselves strongholds, as if you will last forever?" … al-Ḥajjāj had built the Green Palace of Wāsiṭ, and it pleased him as no building ever had, so he asked those around him: "How do you see this dome of mine?"
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

This single anecdote ties the whole argument into a knot. Here is an Iraqi governor of exactly the era and the cities in question, gazing with pride at the most lavish building of his life, and here is a pious opponent answering him with the very Qurʾānic verse (26:128 to 129) that condemns monumental building as the mark of the doomed. The "barefoot shepherds competing in tall buildings" report is the hadith form of that Khārijite's recitation. It is the ascetic and pietist reaction to the Umayyad-Iraqi building boom, turned into an omen and projected back onto the Prophet.

**The motif had deep roots in Iraq.** The Arabs of Iraq did not even need to invent the idea that monumental building is vanity doomed to ruin. It was the most famous local legend they had. Beside Kūfa stood the Lakhmid palaces of al-Khawarnaq and al-Sadīr, and the proverbial tale of their builder-king, al-Nuʿmān, who looked out from his tower over all his wealth and dominion and was struck by its transience, then renounced the world. The poet ʿAdī b. Zayd fixed it as a maxim:

<HadithBlock label="al-Khawarnaq: the king who renounced his palace" source="ʿAdī b. Zayd, in Ibn ʿAsākir, Tārīkh Dimashq; the proverb of al-Khawarnaq and al-Sadīr">
  <div class="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    وَتَذَكَّرَ رَبَّ الْخَوَرْنَقِ إِذْ أَشْـ ـرَفَ يَوْمًا وَلِلْهُدَى تَفْكِيرُ
    <br/>
    سَرَّهُ مَالُهُ وَكَثْرَةُ مَا يَمْـ ـلِكُ وَالْبَحْرُ مُعْرِضٌ وَالسَّدِيرُ
  </div>
  <div class="hadith-block__translation">
    "And remember the lord of al-Khawarnaq, when he looked out one day, and was moved to think on guidance: his wealth had delighted him, and the abundance of his possessions, with the sea stretched before him and al-Sadīr."
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

The lesson that the proud builder's monument is vanity was, quite literally, the view from the edge of Kūfa. When a Kūfan or Baṣran moralist of the first century watched the conquest fortunes rise into brick and dome around him, he had both a live target and a ready cultural script. The "tall buildings" sign is what he produced.

## Verdict

<VerdictBox title="Historical Verdict">
The report that "barefoot, naked shepherds will compete in tall buildings" is not a prophecy of Gulf skyscrapers. It is a piece of first-century Iraqi ascetic preaching, condemning the wealth and monumental building that the conquests poured into Kūfa and Baṣra, and projected back onto the Prophet in the Gabriel scene. It fails the apologists' own criteria: building was never beyond the Arabs (Petra, al-Ḥijr), the claim fixes no testable detail, and the tradition's own commentator, Ibn Ḥajar, says the sign already occurred near the Prophet's time. Its matn will not hold still, sliding between camels, black beasts, and sheep, and between "heads of the people" and "kings of the earth," which is the signature of a paraphrased homiletic motif, not a fixed dictum. Its two "independent" Companion attestations collapse into a single source: the Kūfan common link Abū Zurʿa al-Bajalī (in the name of Abū Hurayra), paralleled by the Baṣran common link Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar (in the name of ʿUmar), an exact contemporary who supplied a fresh chain to ʿUmar and wrapped it in the anti-qadarī Maʿbad al-Juhanī frame of the 80s AH, manufacturing the look of corroboration. What the saying actually condemns is documented history: the spoils of Ctesiphon, Ziyād rebuilding Baṣra's residence in fired brick, and al-Ḥajjāj's green dome at Wāsiṭ, against which a Khārijite recited the Qurʾān's own rebuke of vain building (26:128 to 129). The same Kūfan circle that produced the omen also preserved Khabbāb b. al-Aratt's lament, through the Kūfan chain Ismāʿīl b. Abī Khālid from Qays b. Abī Ḥāzim, that the believers had acquired wealth they could only sink "into the dust." The hadith is the sermon of the ascetics, not the foresight of a prophet.
</VerdictBox>

Idrīs ← Ismāʿīl ← Qays) preserves only the death-wish portion without the building clause, showing the anti-building moral to be a clause that some Kūfan narrators raised and others did not. al-Bazzār notes that the building-condemnation wording was raised from the Ismāʿīl-Qays-Khabbāb chain only by Abū Muʿāwiya.

[^zuhd]: The wider zuhd-against-building cluster: Sunan Ibn Mājah 4161 (ḥasan), the Anṣārī's dome which he demolishes after the Prophet's rebuke, "every wealth like this is a burden on its owner"; Sunan Abī Dāwūd 5237 (← Anas), the raised dome (qubba mushrifa) the Prophet shuns until it is leveled, ending "every building is a burden on its owner except what is indispensable (illā mā lā budda minhu)"; with parallels in Abū Yaʿlā, al-Ṭaḥāwī's Sharḥ Mushkil al-Āthār, and al-Bayhaqī's Shuʿab al-Īmān. The Qurʾānic frame is Sūrat al-Takāthur 102:1 to 2 and the rebuke of ʿĀd in Sūrat al-Shuʿarāʾ 26:128 to 129.

[^iraq]: On the conquest wealth and the Iraqi building boom: the spoils of al-Madāʾin (Ctesiphon), the carpet and crown of Khusraw, and the "White Palace," in al-Ṭabarī, Tārīkh, al-Balādhurī, Futūḥ al-Buldān, and Ibn Faḍl Allāh al-ʿUmarī, Masālik al-Abṣār; the rebuilding of Baṣra's Dār al-Imāra by Ziyād b. Abīhi in gypsum and fired brick, al-Balādhurī, Futūḥ al-Buldān; al-Ḥajjāj's Green Palace (al-Khaḍrāʾ) at Wāsiṭ and the Khārijite's recitation of Q 26:128 to 129, Ibn ʿAsākir, Tārīkh Dimashq, and al-Tadhkira al-Ḥamdūniyya; the legend of al-Khawarnaq and al-Sadīr and al-Nuʿmān's renunciation, in the verse of ʿAdī b. Zayd preserved by Ibn ʿAsākir and others.
