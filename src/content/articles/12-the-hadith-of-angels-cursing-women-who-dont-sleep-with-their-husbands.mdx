The report is famous because of what it says, but its transmission history is more revealing than the line usually quoted in sermons. In its most familiar form, the Prophet is made to say that when a man calls his wife to his bed and she refuses, the angels curse her until morning. That is how many readers know the hadith from Bukhārī and Muslim. Yet when all the early variants are placed beside one another, the report does not look like a single Prophetic teaching preserved through two independent routes from Abū Hurayrah. It looks like a **matn cluster**: one marital-obedience idea, formulated through two common links in two different centers, then attached to Abū Hurayrah through plausible but uncontrolled Successor links.

The question is not whether one can simply point to Abū Hurayrah, Abū Ḥāzim, Qatādah, or al-Aʿmash and declare the report false. The case has to be structural. The report becomes visible only at the level of **Qatādah b. Diʿāmah** in Baṣra and **al-Aʿmash** in Kūfa. Above them, the chains narrow into single strands. Below them, the reports proliferate. That is exactly the zone where isnād-cum-matn analysis has to do its work.

## The two canonical forms

The first form is the one most people recognize. The husband is the grammatical subject. He calls. She refuses. He sleeps angry. The angels curse until morning.

  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    إِذَا دَعَا الرَّجُلُ امْرَأَتَهُ إِلَى فِرَاشِهِ فَأَبَتْ، فَبَاتَ غَضْبَانَ عَلَيْهَا، لَعَنَتْهَا الْمَلَائِكَةُ حَتَّى تُصْبِحَ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    “When a man calls his wife to his bed and she refuses, so he spends the night angry with her, the angels curse her until morning.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

The second form is less famous but earlier in physical attestation and simpler in shape. The woman is the grammatical subject. She abandons the husband’s bed. The angels curse until she returns.

<HadithBlock label="Cluster B — Qatādah form" source="Musnad al-Ṭayālisī, 1456:2580; Aḥmad, Musnad, 7471/8579/9013/10045; Dārimī, Sunan, 2274; Bukhārī, Ṣaḥīḥ, 5194; Muslim, Ṣaḥīḥ, 120.">
  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    إِذَا بَاتَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ هَاجِرَةً لِفِرَاشِ زَوْجِهَا، لَعَنَتْهَا الْمَلَائِكَةُ حَتَّى تَرْجِعَ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    “When a woman spends the night abandoning her husband’s bed, the angels curse her until she returns.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

There are variations inside each family. Some Qatādah lines say “until morning,” some say “until she returns,” and one preserves a bare maxim with no summons, no anger, and no time-limit: “A woman does not abandon her husband’s bed except that the angels of God curse her.” But the two basic forms are stable enough to analyze. The Qatādah form is broad, aphoristic, and woman-focused. The al-Aʿmash form is narrower, legal-casuistic, and husband-focused.

## What ICMA can and cannot prove

Isnād-cum-matn analysis does not begin by asking whether a transmitter could have met another transmitter. That is only a background condition. A man may have met his teacher and still not have heard a particular report from him. The real question is whether the report is independently controlled above the common link.

A common link is the transmitter at whose level the report begins to spread through multiple students. If several independent students transmit a report from Qatādah, then Qatādah is recoverable as the point from which that version proliferated. But if the chain above Qatādah is only Qatādah → Zurārah → Abū Hurayrah, with no other student of Zurārah transmitting the same report, then Zurārah is not independently controlled for this report. His general reliability and his general possibility of hearing from Abū Hurayrah do not prove that this specific hadith came from him.

The same rule applies to al-Aʿmash. Abū Ḥāzim al-Ashjaʿī was a real transmitter from Abū Hurayrah. al-Aʿmash really did transmit from Abū Ḥāzim. But if every early Abū Ḥāzim version of this report passes through al-Aʿmash, then Abū Ḥāzim is not an independent control for this report. The report is recoverable at al-Aʿmash, not securely above him.

This is the central point. The issue is not whether the upper links are **possible**. They are possible. The issue is whether they are **proved for this specific report**. They are not.

  <strong>Method note:</strong> Classical hadith criticism often treats the general possibility of hearing, narrator reliability, and canonical acceptance as sufficient for accepting a report. ICMA asks a different historical question. It asks where the report first becomes visible in a branching transmission network and whether the pre-branching strand is independently controlled.
</div>

## The variant corpus in brief

A corpus search of 1,221 hadith collections, about 1.33 million records, and twenty rijāl and ʿilal works produced 194 raw matches for the curse motif, using normalized searches around “angels,” “curse,” “bed,” “abandoning,” and “a man calls his wife.” The analysis below is based on the early and primary witnesses, especially collectors up to Bayhaqī.[^1]

The Qatādah bundle is the earlier recoverable family. Its earliest physical witness is al-Ṭayālisī, who gives the chain Abū Dāwūd al-Ṭayālisī → Shuʿbah → Qatādah → Zurārah → Abū Hurayrah. Aḥmad preserves multiple lines through Shuʿbah and Hammām. Dārimī places it under the husband’s right over the wife. Bukhārī and Muslim also preserve the Qatādah form, though Muslim contains a line with “until morning” beside the more native “until she returns.”[^2]

The al-Aʿmash bundle appears in Ibn Abī Shaybah, Ibn Rāhawayh, Aḥmad, Bukhārī, Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, Nasāʾī, Ibn Ḥibbān, and Bayhaqī. Its partial common links include Abū Muʿāwiyah, Wakīʿ, Jarīr, Abū ʿAwānah, Ibn Numayr, ʿAlī b. Mushir, and Shuʿbah through Sulaymān, which is al-Aʿmash. This is a strong common-link bundle below al-Aʿmash, but above al-Aʿmash the report is only al-Aʿmash → Abū Ḥāzim → Abū Hurayrah.[^3]

The distribution is therefore not:

<div className="bad-model">
  <span>Abū Hurayrah taught the same hadith independently to Zurārah and Abū Ḥāzim.</span>
</div>

It is more accurately:

<div className="good-model">
  <span>Qatādah and al-Aʿmash each produced recoverable common-link formulations of the same marital-obedience threat motif and each attached it to Abū Hurayrah through a plausible but uncontrolled Successor.</span>
</div>

## The matn tells the story

The two clusters are not the same wording randomly paraphrased. They are two different legal shapes.

| Feature | Qatādah / Zurārah bundle | al-Aʿmash / Abū Ḥāzim bundle |
|---|---:|---:|
| Woman is the grammatical subject | Yes | No |
| Husband is the grammatical subject | No | Yes |
| “Abandoning / migrating from the husband’s bed” | Yes | No |
| Husband summons the wife to bed | No | Yes |
| Wife explicitly refuses | No | Yes |
| Husband sleeps angry or displeased | No | Yes |
| Curse ends “until she returns” | Dominant | No |
| Curse ends “until morning” | Minority / contamination | Dominant |
| Bare maxim form | Yes, through Hammām | No |

The Qatādah form sounds like a moral warning: if she abandons the bed, the angels curse until she returns. The al-Aʿmash form sounds like a legal case: if he summons, she refuses, and he sleeps angry, then the curse lasts until morning. The direction of development is not difficult to see. A broad warning can easily be narrowed into a legal scenario. A fully casuistic scenario is less likely to generate the shorter maxim.

The most primitive-looking form is the Hammām line from Qatādah:

<HadithBlock label="Primitive-looking maxim" source="Aḥādīth ʿAffān, 30305:277; Aḥmad, Musnad, 25794:8579.">
  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    لاَ تَهْجُرُ امْرَأَةٌ فِرَاشَ زَوْجِهَا إِلاَّ لَعَنَتْهَا مَلاَئِكَةُ اللهِ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    “No woman abandons her husband’s bed except that the angels of God curse her.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

This is not yet the fully developed Bukhārī/Muslim form of the husband calling and the wife refusing. It is a short threat formula. That matters because short legal-moral maxims are exactly the kind of material that could be “propheticized” in early legal discourse.

## The upper links fail the control test

This is the decisive point.

Zurārah b. Awfā is not an unknown man. He is a Baṣran qāḍī and a plausible transmitter from Abū Hurayrah. But for this report, a scan of the corpus found no non-Qatādah student of Zurārah transmitting the bed-curse hadith. In the report corpus, the Zurārah-route matches are overwhelmingly through Qatādah, and the apparent exceptions are late compilations re-citing Bukhārī and Muslim rather than independent early lines.[^4]

Abū Ḥāzim al-Ashjaʿī is also not an unknown man. He is a real Kūfan transmitter from Abū Hurayrah, and al-Aʿmash is known to transmit from him. But for this report, every early Abū Ḥāzim line passes through al-Aʿmash. No non-al-Aʿmash student of Abū Ḥāzim preserves the report in an early source.[^5]

That silence is not a small detail. Both Successors had other students.

| Successor | Other well-attested students | Who carries this report? |
|---|---|---|
| Zurārah b. Awfā | Bahz b. Ḥakīm, ʿAwf al-Aʿrābī, ʿAbdallāh b. ʿUbayd, al-Mulaykī, others | Qatādah only |
| Abū Ḥāzim al-Ashjaʿī | Yazīd b. Kaysān, Manṣūr b. al-Muʿtamir, Abū Mālik al-Ashjaʿī, others | al-Aʿmash only |

If the report had genuinely belonged to Zurārah as a known Abū Hurayrah teaching, we would expect at least one other Zurārah student to preserve it. If it had genuinely belonged to Abū Ḥāzim as a known Abū Hurayrah teaching, we would expect at least one other Abū Ḥāzim student to preserve it. Instead, each report appears only through the common link who made it proliferate.

This is what I mean by **false independence at the Companion level**. The two reports both end at Abū Hurayrah, but that does not prove two independent Abū Hurayrah transmissions. It proves two common-link formations attached to Abū Hurayrah.

## Why Qatādah is the likeliest origin point

The strongest scenario is that the Qatādah version is the earliest recoverable form. That does not mean we can prove Qatādah sat down and consciously forged it. The historical claim should be more careful. The report cannot be shown to predate Qatādah, and Qatādah’s legal-exegetical environment gives us the most natural reason for the report to exist.

The key background is Q. 4:34.

<QuranVerse verse="4:34" label="Quran 4:34" />

This verse supplied early exegetes with a legal grammar of male authority, female obedience, nushūz, admonition, bed-separation, and discipline. Qatādah’s preserved tafsīr of the verse is especially important. In ʿAbd al-Razzāq’s Tafsīr, Maʿmar transmits from Qatādah that a man struck his wife. She came to the Prophet. The Prophet wanted retaliation from him, but then the verse “Men are qawwāmūn over women” was revealed.[^6]

<HadithBlock label="Qatādah on Q. 4:34" source="ʿAbd al-Razzāq, Tafsīr, Q. 4:34, PageV01P157.">
  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    عَبْدُ الرَّزَّاقِ قَالَ: أَنَا مَعْمَرٌ، عَنْ قَتَادَةَ، قَالَ: صَكَّ رَجُلٌ امْرَأَتَهُ، فَأَتَتِ النَّبِيَّ، فَأَرَادَ أَنْ يُقِيدَهَا مِنْهُ، فَأَنْزَلَ اللهُ تَعَالَى: الرِّجَالُ قَوَّامُونَ عَلَى النِّسَاءِ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    ʿAbd al-Razzāq said: Maʿmar informed us from Qatādah: A man struck his wife, so she came to the Prophet. He wanted to exact retaliation for her against him, but God revealed: “Men are qawwāmūn over women.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

This is not a neutral explanation of marriage. In Qatādah’s version, the verse enters the story precisely to block retaliation against a husband and to establish male authority as the controlling rule. In the same passage, Qatādah explains “qānitāt” as “obedient.” Ṭabarī preserves the fuller wording: “obedient to God and to their husbands.”[^7]

<HadithBlock label="Qatādah: qānitāt means obedient" source="ʿAbd al-Razzāq, Tafsīr, PageV01P157; Ṭabarī, Jāmiʿ al-Bayān, PageV06P689.">
  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    عَنْ قَتَادَةَ فِي قَوْلِهِ تَعَالَى: قَانِتَاتٌ، قَالَ: مُطِيعَاتٌ.
    <br />
    وَفِي رِوَايَةِ الطَّبَرِيِّ: أَيْ مُطِيعَاتٌ لِلَّهِ وَلِأَزْوَاجِهِنَّ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    From Qatādah concerning “qānitāt”: he said, “obedient.” In Ṭabarī’s version: “obedient to God and to their husbands.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

Then Qatādah explains the disciplinary sequence. If the husband fears nushūz, he admonishes her. If she accepts, that ends the matter. If not, he abandons her bed. If she accepts, that ends the matter. If not, he strikes her with a non-severe beating.[^8]

  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">

    <br />
    وَفِي رِوَايَةٍ أُخْرَى عَنْ قَتَادَةَ: تَبْدَأُ يَا ابْنَ آدَمَ فَتَعِظُهَا، فَإِنْ أَبَتْ عَلَيْكَ فَاهْجُرْهَا، يَعْنِي بِهِ: فِرَاشَهَا.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    “If he fears her nushūz, he admonishes her. If she accepts, then fine; otherwise he abandons her bed. Now compare that exegetical logic to the Qatādah hadith form:

<div className="parallel-box">
  <div>
    <strong>Q. 4:34 in Qatādah’s tafsīr</strong>
    <span>female nushūz → admonition → bed/firāsh discipline → return/obedience</span>
  </div>
  <div>
    <strong>Qatādah’s hadith form</strong>
    <span>woman abandons husband’s bed → angels curse her → until she returns</span>

The conceptual fit is exact. The hadith does not need to begin as a remembered saying from the Prophet. It reads naturally as a propheticized warning built from the Q. 4:34 exegetical-legal world. Qatādah’s tafsīr gives the motive profile: male authority, female obedience, bed-abandonment, and return to compliance.

There is also an important non-Qatādah control. Mujāhid, in Ibn Abī Ḥātim’s Tafsīr, explains Q. 4:34 in terms very close to the hadith’s logic: if a woman commits nushūz away from her husband’s bed, he tells her, “Fear God and return to your bed; this is the admonition.”[^9]

  <div className="hadith-block__arabic" dir="rtl" lang="ar">
    عَنْ مُجَاهِدٍ: فَعِظُوهُنَّ، قَالَ: إِذَا نَشَزَتِ الْمَرْأَةُ عَنْ فِرَاشِ زَوْجِهَا، فَإِنَّهُ يَقُولُ لَهَا: اتَّقِي اللهَ وَارْجِعِي إِلَى فِرَاشِكِ، وَهَذِهِ الْمَوْعِظَةُ.
  </div>
  <div className="hadith-block__translation">
    From Mujāhid concerning “admonish them”: “If the woman commits nushūz away from her husband’s bed, he says to her: Fear God and return to your bed. This is the admonition.”
  </div>
</HadithBlock>

This is extremely important. It shows that the idea did not need the hadith in order to exist. The exegetical maxim was already there: wife, nushūz, husband’s bed, admonition, return. The hadith intensifies the same idea by adding angels and curse.

That is why the Qatādah form is best explained as a **propheticized tarhīb**: a warning formula that attaches divine threat to a legal-exegetical norm.

## Why Zurārah is probably not the motive source

Zurārah b. Awfā should not be made the villain of the argument. A scan of his wider transmission profile does not show him as a specialist in reports that elevate men over women. His preserved material is dominated by prayer, Qurʾān, night worship, moral reports, legal cases, and devotional material. The gender-control theme does not appear as his clear personal signature.

That matters because the best historical explanation should locate motive where the evidence locates it. For this report, the motive does not appear clearly at Zurārah’s level. It appears at Qatādah’s level. Qatādah is the recoverable common link, and Qatādah’s tafsīr of Q. 4:34 supplies the full legal grammar of the report.

So the argument should not be:

<div className="bad-model">
  <span>Zurārah invented an anti-woman report.</span>
</div>

It should be:

<div className="good-model">
  <span>Qatādah, or the Basran legal-exegetical environment visible through Qatādah, propheticized a Q. 4:34 marital-obedience maxim and routed it through a plausible Zurārah → Abū Hurayrah strand.</span>
</div>

This distinction keeps the case precise. It does not overstate what we can prove.

## al-Aʿmash and the problem of false independence

The al-Aʿmash bundle is not the earliest form. It is more developed. It supplies the details that a jurist would want: the husband initiates, the wife refuses, the husband sleeps angry, and the curse lasts until morning. It transforms a broad warning into a more actionable legal scenario.

One could argue that al-Aʿmash independently formulated a Kūfan version of the same Q. 4:34 idea. That is possible. But al-Aʿmash should not be treated as a neutral transmitter in this dossier. There is a prior pattern problem.

In the bewitchment dossier, al-Aʿmash appears in the same kind of structural role. A report centered elsewhere around Hishām b. ʿUrwah appears through a Kūfan al-Aʿmash route that lacks Hishām in the isnād while preserving the same narrative world. The earlier analysis argued that the most plausible explanation is not independent origin, but source-obscuring or route substitution in a politically charged Kufan environment.[^10]

In an unpublished Ṣaḥīfat ʿAlī study, the same structural tendency appears again. The study argues that al-Aʿmash produced a Kūfan cluster that reproduces the rhetorical architecture of an earlier al-Shaʿbī construction while changing the authority structure, contents, and polemical function. The point is not that the Ṣaḥīfat ʿAlī case proves this hadith by itself. The point is that al-Aʿmash repeatedly appears as a common-link reformulator whose versions can look independent in isnād while being structurally dependent in matn architecture.[^11]

That prior pattern changes how we judge the al-Aʿmash form here. The standalone evidence proves only that al-Aʿmash is the common link of the Kūfan version. The wider pattern makes genuine independence less attractive. The al-Aʿmash version preserves the same core threat as Qatādah’s earlier Baṣran form but recasts it into a sharper legal case and attaches it to a different plausible Abū Hurayrah Successor, Abū Ḥāzim.

This is exactly how false independence works. The route is believable because Abū Ḥāzim really can transmit from Abū Hurayrah. But believability is not proof. A successful rerouted isnād normally uses plausible links.

<div className="method-note">
  <strong>Careful claim:</strong> I am not claiming that this dossier alone proves al-Aʿmash stole Qatādah’s wording. The stronger claim is that al-Aʿmash’s bundle should be treated as suspect independence. It is a second common-link formulation, not independent proof that the report reaches Abū Hurayrah.
</div>

## What about al-Aʿmash hearing from Abū Ḥāzim?

This point needs correction from some earlier polemical treatments. al-Aʿmash did transmit from Abū Ḥāzim in general, and direct-hearing language exists in other reports. Therefore, the argument should not rest on the claim that al-Aʿmash could not have heard from Abū Ḥāzim at all.[^12]

The weak link is more specific. For **this report**, Abū Ḥāzim is uncontrolled. His other students do not preserve it. The only early route through him is al-Aʿmash. So the chain does not prove Abū Ḥāzim taught this report from Abū Hurayrah. It proves al-Aʿmash taught a report and placed it through Abū Ḥāzim to Abū Hurayrah.

This is an important difference. Classical isnād criticism often asks: could al-Aʿmash have heard from Abū Ḥāzim? ICMA asks: does any independent Abū Ḥāzim student transmit this report? The answer, so far, is no.

## The legal setting: husband-rights literature

By the time the report enters legal collections, it is already being read as a husband-rights text. Dārimī places the Qatādah form under a chapter on the husband’s right over the woman. Abū Dāwūd places the al-Aʿmash form under the same kind of chapter. Ibn Abī Shaybah places the al-Aʿmash report in a dense legal-ethical environment with reports about the wife being commanded to observe the husband’s right, women hypothetically prostrating to husbands, a wife coming to the husband even if at the oven, and extreme statements about never fully repaying the husband’s right.[^13]

That environment does not prove where the report began, but it shows why it survived. It was useful. It gave jurists and preachers a Prophetic threat formula for enforcing a one-sided marital duty. The Q. 4:34 exegetical logic supplied the legal grammar. The hadith supplied the divine terror.

Later tafsīr also recognizes the fit. Suyūṭī places the al-Aʿmash/Bukhārī-Muslim version directly inside discussion of Q. 4:34.[^14] That is exactly where the report belongs conceptually.

## The false companion-level support

Bazzār notes that similar wording was also narrated from Zayd b. Arqam and Ibn Abī Awfā.[^15] This sounds, at first, like broader Companion support. But in the corpus search, those non-Abū Hurayrah versions do not appear in the major early core collections as independent controls. They surface in peripheral or later works. They are better understood as **deep dives**: later Companion-level strands that emerge after the report is already circulating.

Deep dives do not independently prove an early Companion origin. They often show the opposite. Once a report becomes useful, it can acquire additional Companion routes. That creates the appearance of wider attestation while leaving the earliest recoverable common links unchanged.

## So who fabricated it?

The clean answer is: we cannot prove a personal confession, so we should not pretend to have one. The better historical language is **propheticization**, **common-link formulation**, and **false independence**.

Still, if we ask where the likeliest origin lies, the answer is Qatādah’s Basran environment. The Qatādah form is earlier, simpler, closer to the non-prophetic Q. 4:34 exegetical idea, and recoverable before the al-Aʿmash form. It is therefore the best candidate for the first propheticized version.

al-Aʿmash then represents a second-stage problem. His version may be an independent Kūfan propheticization of the same Q. 4:34 motif, but his prior pattern makes route-rerouting or common-link reformulation especially plausible. The al-Aʿmash form is not proof of a second Abū Hurayrah transmission. It is proof of a second common-link formulation.

## The final reconstruction

The report is therefore classically strong but historically weak above the common links. That distinction is essential. A classical scholar may say the report is ṣaḥīḥ because Bukhārī and Muslim transmit it through acceptable men. An ICMA analysis asks a different question: can the report be shown to predate Qatādah or al-Aʿmash? At present, the answer is no.

## Verdict

The hadith is best understood as a first-/second-century legal-exegetical warning projected onto the Prophet.

The earliest recoverable version is Qatādah’s Baṣran form. Its content fits his Q. 4:34 tafsīr almost too well: men’s authority, women’s obedience, nushūz, bed-abandonment, and return. The report turns that framework into a divine threat.

The al-Aʿmash version is a later or secondary Kūfan formulation. It is not independent proof of Abū Hurayrah’s teaching. It is a second common-link bundle through an uncontrolled Abū Ḥāzim strand, produced by a transmitter who elsewhere appears capable of creating independent-looking routes for inherited report-architecture.

So the strongest conclusion is not that one can name the exact moment of forgery. It is that the report **cannot be shown to come from the Prophet**, cannot be shown to come from Abū Hurayrah, and is better explained as a propheticized marital-obedience maxim from the early legal-exegetical tradition.

The angels in this hadith are not evidence of heaven speaking. They are evidence of law learning how to speak with heaven’s voice.
