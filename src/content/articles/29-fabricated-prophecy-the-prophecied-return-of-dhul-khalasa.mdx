## The claim

The apologetic claim is simple. A pre-Islamic idol called **Dhū al-Khalaṣa** was destroyed in the Prophet’s lifetime. Centuries later, the shrine allegedly reappeared in southern Arabia and was destroyed again in the modern period. Therefore, the report in which the Prophet predicts that the women of Daws will one day circle Dhū al-Khalaṣa is presented as a fulfilled prophecy.

The argument sounds impressive only if the report is treated as an isolated sentence. Once the sentence is placed back into its transmission history, its local geography, and its shrine-memory context, it looks less like supernatural foresight and more like a later apocalyptic interpretation of a sacred site that had never fully disappeared from memory.

<ClaimBox>
The Dhū al-Khalaṣa report is best read as an **al-Zuhrī-shaped apocalyptic reworking of a persistent southern sanctuary memory**. The exact prophetic form runs through al-Zuhrī from Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib from Abū Hurayra, while the destruction report is a separate Jarīr b. ʿAbd Allāh family. The motive is exposed by Maʿmar’s aside: someone in his world knew that a house already stood over the old stone.
</ClaimBox>

That means the modern “fulfillment” argument starts at the wrong end of the problem. The more important question is not whether some later building at the site can be found. The more important question is why a second/eighth-century transmitter already preserved a note that the old stone had a building over it.

## The report in question

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَضْطَرِبَ أَلَيَاتُ نِسَاءِ دَوْسٍ عَلَى ذِي الْخَلَصَةِ، وَذُو الْخَلَصَةِ طَاغِيَةُ دَوْسٍ الَّتِي كَانُوا يَعْبُدُونَ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until the buttocks of the women of Daws move around Dhū al-Khalaṣa. Dhū al-Khalaṣa was the idol of Daws which they used to worship in the Jāhiliyya.”
>
> **Source:** al-Bukhārī, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 7116, through Abū al-Yamān → Shuʿayb → al-Zuhrī → Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Muslim’s form has the same core, but adds a useful geographical gloss:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَضْطَرِبَ أَلَيَاتُ نِسَاءِ دَوْسٍ حَوْلَ ذِي الْخَلَصَةِ، وَكَانَتْ صَنَمًا تَعْبُدُهَا دَوْسٌ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ بِتَبَالَةَ.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until the buttocks of the women of Daws move around Dhū al-Khalaṣa. It was an idol worshiped by Daws in the Jāhiliyya at Tabāla.”
>
> **Source:** Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 2906, through ʿAbd al-Razzāq → Maʿmar → al-Zuhrī → Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

The language is deliberately humiliating. It does not merely say that people will worship an idol. It says that **the women of Daws** will move their bodies around it. This is not neutral ethnography. It is tribal shame language placed into an end-times frame.

<ContextNote>
The important point is not that al-Zuhrī, Saʿīd, or Abū Hurayra are “weak” in the ordinary Sunni rijāl sense. They are not. The point is that the exact prophecy has a narrow recoverable shape, and the most revealing variant comes through Maʿmar from al-Zuhrī.
</ContextNote>

The branches after al-Zuhrī are not independent origins. Bukhārī has Shuʿayb from al-Zuhrī. Muslim and ʿAbd al-Razzāq have Maʿmar from al-Zuhrī. Al-Bazzār has ʿUqayl from al-Zuhrī. Ibn Abī ʿĀṣim has Muḥammad b. Abī ʿAtīq from Ibn Shihāb. The spread begins **after** al-Zuhrī, not before him.

That does not prove by itself that al-Zuhrī invented the report. It does mean that the famous prophetic form is recoverable to an al-Zuhrī-centered transmission environment, not to a public, multiply witnessed Prophetic statement.

## The smoking gun: the house over the stone

The most important version is the Maʿmar route because it preserves an aside that Bukhārī’s shorter form does not foreground.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
أَخْبَرَنَا عَبْدُ الرَّزَّاقِ، عَنْ مَعْمَرٍ، عَنِ الزُّهْرِيِّ، عَنِ ابْنِ الْمُسَيَّبِ، عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ، قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَضْطَرِبَ آلِيَاتُ نِسَاءِ دَوْسٍ حَوْلَ ذِي الْخَلَصَةِ، وَكَانَتْ صَنَمًا تَعْبُدُهَا دَوْسٌ فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ بِتَبَالَةَ. قَالَ مَعْمَرٌ: وَسَمِعْتُ غَيْرَ الزُّهْرِيِّ يَقُولُ: عَلَى ذَلِكَ الْحَجَرِ بَيْتٌ بُنِيَ الْيَوْمَ.
</div>

> ʿAbd al-Razzāq reported from Maʿmar, from al-Zuhrī, from Ibn al-Musayyib, from Abū Hurayra that the Messenger of God said: “The Hour will not be established until the buttocks of the women of Daws move around Dhū al-Khalaṣa. It was an idol worshiped by Daws in the Jāhiliyya at Tabāla.” Maʿmar said: “I heard someone other than al-Zuhrī say: over that stone there is a house built today.”
>
> **Source:** ʿAbd al-Razzāq, *al-Jāmiʿ*, no. 20795.
</HadithBlock>

Ibn Ḥibbān preserves the same clue in an even sharper form:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
قَالَ مَعْمَرٌ: إِنَّ عَلَيْهِ الْآنَ بَيْتًا مَبْنِيًّا مُغْلَقًا.
</div>

> Maʿmar said: “There is now a built, closed house over it.”
>
> **Source:** Ibn Ḥibbān, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 6749.
</HadithBlock>

This is the line that explains the report’s motive. The old idol had supposedly been destroyed. Yet Maʿmar’s world still knew of a **stone** and a **built house over it**. That is not a clean fulfilled prophecy appearing out of nowhere centuries later. It is a living site-memory problem already visible in the transmission itself.

The hadith answers that problem by turning the site into an apocalyptic sign. The old sanctuary is not simply a ruin. It is a future relapse waiting to happen.

## The destruction report is separate

The destruction story is often cited as if it supports the prophecy. It supports the existence and importance of the sanctuary, but it does not independently support the future prediction. It is a separate report family.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
قَالَ لِي رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: أَلَا تُرِيحُنِي مِنْ ذِي الْخَلَصَةِ؟ وَكَانَ بَيْتًا فِي خَثْعَمَ يُسَمَّى كَعْبَةَ الْيَمَانِيَةِ.
</div>

> Jarīr b. ʿAbd Allāh said: The Messenger of God said to me, “Will you not relieve me of Dhū al-Khalaṣa?” It was a house in Khathʿam called the Yemeni Kaʿba.
>
> **Source:** al-Bukhārī, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 3020.
</HadithBlock>

Other versions say that Jarīr set out with cavalry from Aḥmas, demolished it, burned it, and killed those found around it. Muslim’s versions also call it the Yemeni Kaʿba and place it in the Khathʿam/Bajīla environment. Ibn Abī Shayba likewise preserves the form, “a house that belonged to Khathʿam in the Jāhiliyya, called the Yemeni Kaʿba.” Al-Ṭabarānī and Ibn Ḥibbān preserve an even more useful line in which the Prophet is made to say that nothing remained of the pre-Islamic idols except the house of Dhū al-Khalaṣa.

That last idea is important because it matches the later shrine-survival problem. The narrative world itself remembers Dhū al-Khalaṣa as the idol that remained when the others had been demolished.

## Dhū al-Khalaṣa was not simply “the idol of Daws”

This is where the apologetic reading becomes too flat. The prophecy version says Daws. But the broader historical memory does not restrict Dhū al-Khalaṣa to Daws.

Ibn Hishām says that Dhū al-Khalaṣa belonged to **Daws, Khathʿam, Bajīla, and those Arabs who lived in their lands at Tabāla**. Al-Balādhurī describes it as **a white stone at Tabāla** and as an idol of **Bajīla, Khathʿam, and Azd al-Sarāt**. Ibn Ḥazm expands the associated groups even further, listing Bajīla, Khathʿam, al-Ḥārith b. Kaʿb, Jurm, Zubayd, al-Ghawth b. Murr, and Banū Hilāl b. ʿĀmir, and locating it between Mecca and Yemen.

Ibn ʿAsākir preserves later legendary material in which the Arabs as a whole are said to have revered the site. Al-Hamdānī lists Dhū al-Khalaṣa among the sacred geographies of Arabia, alongside Mecca, Īliyāʾ, al-Lāt near Nakhlah, and the Kaʿba of Najrān.

So why does the prophecy focus on Daws?

The answer is almost certainly **Abū Hurayra**.

The report becomes more rhetorically powerful when a Dawsi Companion reports the future relapse of his own tribal world. It is not an outsider saying, “Those southern tribes will return to paganism.” It is an internal tribal witness saying, in effect, “My own people’s women will one day circle the old idol.”

That is why the wording is so humiliating. It does not say “Daws will worship.” It says “the buttocks of the women of Daws will move around it.” The report makes the future relapse bodily, tribal, and shameful.

## The Daws conversion template

The selection of Daws also fits a pre-existing conversion story. Daws is remembered in the sīra and hadith material as a tribe whose guidance was difficult and required prophetic prayer.

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
جَاءَ الطُّفَيْلُ بْنُ عَمْرٍو الدَّوْسِيُّ إِلَى رَسُولِ اللَّهِ ﷺ فَقَالَ: يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، إِنَّ دَوْسًا قَدْ عَصَتْ وَأَبَتْ، فَادْعُ اللَّهَ عَلَيْهَا. فَقَالَ النَّاسُ: هَلَكَتْ دَوْسٌ. فَقَالَ: اللَّهُمَّ اهْدِ دَوْسًا وَأْتِ بِهِمْ.
</div>

> Al-Ṭufayl b. ʿAmr al-Dawsī came to the Messenger of God and said: “Messenger of God, Daws has disobeyed and refused, so pray to God against them.” The people said, “Daws is destroyed.” But he said: “O God, guide Daws and bring them.”
>
> **Sources:** al-Shāfiʿī, *Musnad*; also preserved through related routes in Ibn Rāhawayh and the sīra material.
</HadithBlock>

That memory gives the Dhū al-Khalaṣa prophecy a narrative arc. Daws was once resistant, then guided by prophetic mercy, and now the eschatological report imagines Daws relapsing back into the cultic world from which it had been saved.

Whether one treats that conversion story as historical or literary, it shows that Daws already carried a recognizable symbolic role in early Islamic memory. It was not random.

## What would push Saʿīd or al-Zuhrī to transmit such a report?

The question of motive should not be flattened into one sentence: “al-Zuhrī fabricated it for the Umayyads.” That is too crude. The report’s motive becomes clearer when Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib and al-Zuhrī are placed in their own worlds.

Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib is not a clean candidate for simple Umayyad propaganda. Historical reports remember him refusing the Umayyad demand to pledge allegiance to al-Walīd and Sulaymān while ʿAbd al-Malik was still alive. He was beaten, dressed in rough garments, paraded, and imprisoned for that refusal. That memory matters. If the report entered at Saʿīd’s layer, it is better explained as **Medinan moral-apocalyptic commentary** than as a court-sponsored anti-Yemeni slogan.

Saʿīd’s world had witnessed the collapse of the old order into civil war, coercive bayʿa politics, hereditary succession, and the transformation of religion into state control. In such an atmosphere, a report about Arabia eventually reverting to the religion of its fathers makes sense. It is a warning about post-Prophetic decline.

Al-Zuhrī’s position is different. He is the perfect figure for the report’s polished circulation because he sits between **Saʿīd’s Medinan authority** and **Umayyad-era writing and diffusion**. Ibn Saʿd and al-Balādhurī preserve the famous report in which al-Zuhrī says that scholars disliked writing knowledge until the rulers compelled them, after which they thought it wrong to prevent Muslims from it. They also preserve the memory that after al-Walīd b. Yazīd was killed, written materials from al-Zuhrī’s knowledge were found in such quantity that they were carried on pack animals. Al-Zuhrī is also remembered as having sat with Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib for years.

That makes al-Zuhrī the natural common-link for a report like this. He could attach a southern shrine-memory to Saʿīd, Saʿīd to Abū Hurayra, and Abū Hurayra to Daws. The result is an elegant prophetic form: a famous Dawsi Companion predicts a future Dawsi relapse around a site whose physical afterlife was already known.

<ContextNote>
The report does not require a special Umayyad grudge against Daws. The stronger motive is sacred-site polemic. The old southern “Yemeni Kaʿba” is demoted into an end-times sign of pagan relapse, while Abū Hurayra’s Dawsi identity makes the warning sound like an internal tribal confession rather than outside slander.
</ContextNote>

## The “Yemeni Kaʿba” angle

The phrase “Yemeni Kaʿba” is not just a geographical curiosity. It matters because it places Dhū al-Khalaṣa inside a sacred-political hierarchy.

In the Islamic order, Mecca is the sanctuary. The Kaʿba is the center. A rival or quasi-rival sanctuary remembered as the **Yemeni Kaʿba** is not merely a local idol; it is a rival sacred memory. A report predicting the shameful return of people to this Yemeni sanctuary implicitly subordinates it to the Islamic Meccan order. It says: the true Kaʿba remains the center of worship, while the “Yemeni Kaʿba” becomes a sign of end-times degeneration.

This is why the report’s southern geography, Dawsi framing, and Abū Hurayra attribution work so well together. The story is not simply “idols will return.” It is: a famous Dawsi authority predicts that his own tribal world will regress to a southern pagan sanctuary remembered as a rival Kaʿba.

## The collateral variants: a wider return-to-idols motif

The exact Daws report is al-Zuhrī-shaped, but the motif is wider. Other reports imagine the end of time as a return to ancestral cults after Islam has declined.

Muslim preserves a report from ʿĀʾisha in which the Prophet says that night and day will not pass away until al-Lāt and al-ʿUzzā are worshiped. When ʿĀʾisha objects that she thought the Qurʾānic promise had ended such worship, the response is that God will send a good wind to take the believers, and those who remain will return to the religion of their fathers.

Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād preserves a report from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
إِذَا عُبِدَتْ ذُو الْخَلَصَةِ كَانَ ظُهُورُ الرُّومِ عَلَى الشَّامِ.
</div>

> “When Dhū al-Khalaṣa is worshiped, the Romans will dominate al-Shām.”
>
> **Source:** Nuʿaym b. Ḥammād, *Kitāb al-Fitan*, no. 1333.
</HadithBlock>

This is not the same report. It is not even phrased as a Prophetic hadith here. But it shows how Dhū al-Khalaṣa could function inside a wider Syrian apocalyptic imagination: Arabia returns to paganism, Syria falls to Rome, and the final age begins.

Nuʿaym also preserves a form in which, after Jesus and after a wind takes the believers, people return to the religion of their fathers. Then Abū Hurayra’s line appears almost as a vivid comment: “As if I can see the buttocks of the women of Daws...” That form is valuable because it suggests that the Daws phrase could circulate as an illustrative apocalyptic image inside a broader return-to-idols framework.

There is also a striking variant in al-Ḥākim’s *Mustadrak*. In a report from ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr, a man is asked his tribe and says he is from Banū ʿĀmir b. Ṣaʿṣaʿa. The reply then says:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَتَدَافَعَ نِسَاءُ بَنِي عَامِرٍ عَلَى ذِي الْخَلَصَةِ، وَثَنٍ كَانَ يُسَمَّى فِي الْجَاهِلِيَّةِ.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until the women of Banū ʿĀmir push against one another at Dhū al-Khalaṣa, an idol that was named in the Jāhiliyya.”
>
> **Source:** al-Ḥākim, *al-Mustadrak*, no. 8653.
</HadithBlock>

This is extremely important. It shows that the formula “women of [tribe] around Dhū al-Khalaṣa” could be adapted. In Abū Hurayra’s version the tribe is Daws because Abū Hurayra is Dawsi. In the ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿAmr variant, the target can become Banū ʿĀmir in response to a tribal exchange.

That does not prove the Abū Hurayra version was copied from the Banū ʿĀmir version. It proves something more general: the report belongs to a flexible apocalyptic shame motif, not a single cleanly preserved public prophecy.

The development looks like this:

| Layer | Evidence | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Shrine memory | Dhū al-Khalaṣa as a white stone at Tabāla, linked to Khathʿam, Bajīla, Azd, Daws, and others | Establishes a real southern sacred site |
| Destruction memory | Jarīr destroys the house called the Yemeni Kaʿba | Explains Islamic triumph over the old cult |
| Survival problem | Reports that it remained, or that a house stood over the stone “today” | Creates the need for explanation |
| Dawsi framing | Abū Hurayra is Dawsi, and Daws has a conversion-story template | Makes the prophecy an internal tribal warning |
| Apocalyptic upgrade | The women of Daws return to Dhū al-Khalaṣa before the Hour | Converts site survival into prophecy |
| Later fulfillment claim | Modern stories of a revived shrine | Misread the report as a miracle instead of recognizing the earlier site afterlife |

## The rijāl question

Sunni rijāl literature does not give a simple “weak transmitter” solution here. Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib is one of the most prestigious Medinan authorities. Al-Zuhrī is central to early Sunni transmission. Abū Hurayra is one of the most frequently used Companion authorities. That is precisely why the report is effective.

The critique is not:

> “The chain contains an obviously rejected liar.”

The critique is:

> “The exact prophetic form has a narrow al-Zuhrī stem, the matn selects Daws for a reason, and the Maʿmar version preserves the contemporary shrine-survival clue that explains why the prophecy was useful.”

This is an isnād-cum-matn argument, not a one-line rijāl dismissal.

## Why the modern fulfillment claim fails

The modern fulfillment claim usually says: Dhū al-Khalaṣa returned, people worshiped around it, and it was destroyed again. But the Maʿmar note already says there was a built house over the stone in his own period or in the memory available to him. That means the old cultic site had a visible afterlife long before the modern event.

So the modern episode cannot function as a clean miracle. It is part of a longer pattern of shrine memory, local sacred geography, and recurrent attempts to interpret or suppress the site.

The correct question is not:

> “How could the Prophet know this shrine would return?”

The correct question is:

> “Why did a second/eighth-century transmission environment already know of a house over the old stone, and why did it preserve a prophecy saying people would one day return to it?”

Once that question is asked, the report becomes intelligible without miracle.

## Conclusion

The Dhū al-Khalaṣa report is not a straightforward fulfilled prophecy. The exact prophetic wording is narrow and al-Zuhrī-centered. The destruction report is separate. The site is not consistently Dawsi in the historical memory. The old sanctuary is described as belonging to a wider southern tribal landscape: Khathʿam, Bajīla, Azd, Daws, and others. It is remembered as a white stone at Tabāla and as the Yemeni Kaʿba.

The most important clue is Maʿmar’s note: there was already a built house over the stone. That makes the motive visible. The prophecy was not produced because the shrine unexpectedly reappeared centuries later. It was produced because the shrine, stone, or sacred memory had not disappeared cleanly in the first place.

Daws and Abū Hurayra were selected because they made the report rhetorically stronger. Abū Hurayra was Dawsi, so the report becomes an internal tribal confession. A famous Muslim authority from Daws is made to predict that his own tribal world will return to an old pagan sanctuary. That is far more powerful than a generic warning that Arabs will worship idols again.

<VerdictBox>
The Dhū al-Khalaṣa prophecy is best explained as a second/eighth-century apocalyptic reinterpretation of a persistent southern sacred site. The report demotes the old “Yemeni Kaʿba” into an end-times sign, uses Abū Hurayra’s Dawsi identity to create an internal tribal witness, and turns the survival of a shrine memory into a prophecy. It is not a clean miracle claim. It is a shrine-survival problem dressed as foresight.
</VerdictBox>
