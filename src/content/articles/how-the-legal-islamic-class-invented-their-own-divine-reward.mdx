## The Hadith That Pays the Judge Even When He Is Wrong

One of the most influential reports in Islamic legal theory says:

<HadithBlock>
إذا حكم الحاكم فاجتهد ثم أصاب فله أجران، وإذا حكم فاجتهد ثم أخطأ فله أجر
</HadithBlock>

“If a judge gives judgment, strives, and reaches the correct ruling, he receives two rewards. If he gives judgment, strives, and gets it wrong, he still receives one reward.”

That is not a small statement. It does not merely say that God forgives sincere mistakes. It does not merely say that intention matters. It says something far more useful for a professional class: the judge is rewarded even when his ruling is wrong.

That is the whole issue.

A judge is not just a private believer trying his best. A judge binds other people. He can take property from one person and give it to another. He can validate a marriage, dissolve a dispute, punish an accused person, or give legal force to a claim. When he is wrong, someone else lives under the cost of that mistake.

Yet the hadith does not center the person harmed by the wrong judgment. It centers the judge. It tells him that even his error is spiritually profitable.

So before we ask whether the hadith is “useful,” we should ask the better question.

Useful for whom?

<ClaimBox>
This report gives divine reward to the legal official even when his ruling is wrong. A hadith like that should never be accepted casually, especially when its transmission runs through the legal class that benefited from it.
</ClaimBox>

This is not an argument against justice. It is not an argument against sincere reasoning. It is not an argument that every judge who makes a mistake is evil. That would be childish.

The issue is more precise. This hadith gives a divine reward structure to the profession of legal judgment itself. It protects the judge’s status even when the judgment fails. It turns juristic error into a rewarded act. That is exactly the kind of doctrine a legal class would want once it had begun to build a system around ijtihād, disagreement, analogy, and judicial discretion.

The report does not float above history. It enters the record through transmitters, jurists, judges, and legal theorists. That is where the problem begins.

## What the Hadith Does for the Legal Class

Every legal system has a problem. Judges disagree. Scholars disagree. One jurist says something is lawful. Another says it is forbidden. One court rules for a man. Another court would have ruled against him. One school treats a ruling as valid. Another school rejects it.

If the law is supposed to represent God’s will, disagreement becomes dangerous. If two scholars contradict each other, did one of them speak falsely about God? If a judge issues a wrong ruling, did he sin by binding people to error? If a school builds law through human reasoning, how does it protect itself from the accusation that it is inventing religion?

The “two rewards or one reward” hadith solves that problem beautifully.

It says the correct judge gets two rewards, and the mistaken judge still gets one. The legal class wins either way. Right or wrong, the jurist remains spiritually safe. Right or wrong, the system of ijtihād remains protected. Right or wrong, the public is told that the scholar’s process is rewarded by God.

That is why this report became so useful. It makes disagreement less embarrassing. It makes error less dangerous. It gives the professional class a religious guarantee.

But usefulness is not authenticity. In fact, when a report is this useful to the people transmitting and citing it, usefulness becomes a reason for suspicion.

## The First Bundle: The Yazīd Route

The major canonical route runs through **Yazīd ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Hād**. The chain is usually presented through ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ:

**The Prophet → ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ → Abū Qays → Busr ibn Saʿīd → Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Taymī → Yazīd ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Hād → later transmitters → collectors**

That is the route behind the impressive spread into Bukhārī, Muslim, Abū Dāwūd, al-Nasāʾī, Aḥmad, and Ibn Ḥibbān. But the spread is not the same as independence. The branches fan out after Yazīd. They do not independently establish the statement before him.

This is why diagrams matter. A person who only sees collector names may think, “Bukhārī has it, Muslim has it, Abū Dāwūd has it, al-Nasāʾī has it, Aḥmad has it. Therefore, it must be widely transmitted.”

But that is not how transmission works.

If several collectors receive a report through later branches that all pass through one earlier transmitter, then the report is not multiplied at the earliest point. It is multiplied after that transmitter. The real historical question is not how many books eventually cite it. The question is where the branches converge.

Here they converge on Yazīd.

That does not automatically prove fabrication. A common link may preserve something earlier. But it means the report cannot simply be treated as a public prophetic teaching with broad early circulation. The earliest recoverable form of this canonical bundle sits at Yazīd’s layer.

That alone should make us cautious.

## The Verification Note Is Not Neutral

The most suspicious feature of the Yazīd route is the verification note.

After transmitting the report, Yazīd says that he related it to **Abū Bakr ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm**. Abū Bakr then allegedly replied that Abū Salamah had narrated the same report to him from Abū Hurayrah.

At first, this looks powerful. It seems to give the hadith another Companion route. The report is not only from ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ. It is also from Abū Hurayrah. It appears confirmed by Abū Bakr ibn Ḥazm, a major Medinan legal figure.

But look carefully at what is happening.

The Abū Hurayrah confirmation appears **inside Yazīd’s own report**. It is not yet an independent bundle. It is Yazīd telling us that someone else confirmed his report. That may be true. But it cannot do the same work as a separate early chain standing outside Yazīd.

A transmitter saying, “I checked this with another authority, and he confirmed it,” is not the same thing as independent transmission. It may be a real memory. It may also be a literary authentication device. Either way, it must be treated as part of the report’s rhetoric, not as automatic proof.

The content of the note is also too convenient. It does three things at once.

It gives the report a second Companion. It connects the report to Abū Bakr ibn Ḥazm, a Medinan judge and legal authority. And it makes the legal class itself appear as the body that verifies the doctrine.

That is exactly what a self-authenticating legal hadith would look like.

## The Second Bundle: The ʿAbd al-Razzāq Route

The second diagram shows the Abū Hurayrah line as a separate route:

**The Prophet → Abū Hurayrah → Abū Salamah → Abū Bakr ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm → Yaḥyā ibn Saʿīd al-Anṣārī → Sufyān al-Thawrī → Maʿmar → ʿAbd al-Razzāq → later collectors**

This is the route that appears through al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Ḥibbān, and al-Bayhaqī.

This route is important, but it does not rescue the report.

The reason is simple. The route corresponds to the very confirmation line already named in Yazīd’s report. Yazīd says Abū Bakr ibn Ḥazm confirmed the report from Abū Salamah from Abū Hurayrah. Later, we find a route through Abū Bakr, Abū Salamah, and Abū Hurayrah, but it reaches the collections through a late bottleneck at ʿAbd al-Razzāq.

That means the two bundles are not as independent as they first appear. They are linked by the verification note. The second route may represent the later circulation of the same confirmation line, not an independent public memory of the Prophet’s words.

This is the crucial distinction.

Formal chain multiplication is not the same as genuine historical independence. A report can gain routes over time. A corroboration note can become a full isnād. A legal doctrine can be backfilled with supporting chains. The fact that a second line exists does not automatically prove that the doctrine goes back to the Prophet.

It may only prove that the doctrine became important enough to receive additional support.

## The Real Shape of the Evidence

When the two diagrams are read together, the structure is not “many independent chains.” It is more complicated and more suspicious.

One route goes through Yazīd and includes an internal claim of confirmation by Abū Bakr ibn Ḥazm. Another route later appears through that same Abū Bakr line and reaches the collectors through ʿAbd al-Razzāq. Both routes are located in the same legal and Medinan scholarly world. Both serve the same professional doctrine. Both end up strengthening the same claim: the judge is rewarded even when wrong.

This is what the apologist usually misses. He sees multiple collectors and thinks the report is secure. But the diagram forces a different question.

Where does the report become historically visible?

Which transmitters make it useful?

Which professional class benefits from it?

Why does the report contain its own verification note?

Why does the alleged Medinan doctrine not appear where we would most expect it?

The hadith starts to look less like a preserved prophetic statement and more like a legal doctrine acquiring prophetic clothing.

## The Legal Class Behind the Report

The transmitters matter because this is not a random devotional statement. It is a doctrine about legal authority.

Abū Bakr ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿAmr ibn Ḥazm was not just a narrator. He was a Medinan judge and legal authority. Yaḥyā ibn Saʿīd al-Anṣārī was a major Medinan jurist. Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm al-Taymī belonged to the same Medinan scholarly environment. Yazīd ibn al-Hād is transmitting through that same legal world.

So when a hadith says judges are rewarded even when wrong, and the report moves through judges and jurists, we are allowed to ask the obvious question.

Is this a prophetic teaching preserved by a neutral class?

Or is this a professional doctrine produced by the very class that benefited from it?

The question is not disrespectful. It is basic historical criticism.

A report that rewards warriors and circulates through warriors would be suspicious. A report that rewards rulers and circulates through court transmitters would be suspicious. A report that rewards merchants and circulates through merchant networks would be suspicious. So a report that rewards judges and circulates through judges and jurists must also be suspicious.

The legal class had a problem. It needed to defend human reasoning as religion. It needed to explain why jurists could disagree and still remain authoritative. It needed to protect judges from the moral consequences of wrong rulings.

This hadith solves that problem too perfectly.

## The Mālik Test

Now comes the most serious silence.

If this hadith was a known Medinan legal teaching in the early second century, where is it in Mālik’s *Muwaṭṭaʾ*?

Mālik ibn Anas died in 179 AH. His *Muwaṭṭaʾ* is one of the earliest and most important monuments of Medinan legal tradition. It preserves hadith, Companion practice, Successor opinion, and legal reasoning. It is exactly the kind of work where a report about judicial ijtihād should appear if it were already circulating as a major Medinan proof.

But it is absent.

That silence is not a mathematical disproof. Silence rarely proves anything by itself. But this is not ordinary silence. The report allegedly moves through the Medinan legal world that Mālik knew best. It involves figures and themes that belong to that world. It addresses judicial reasoning, legal error, and the authority of the judge. Mālik’s book is not uninterested in law. It is a legal work.

So the absence matters.

If a doctrine this useful, this legal, and this Medinan was already widely known in Mālik’s environment, why does Mālik not preserve it?

<ClaimBox>
The problem is not merely that Mālik omits the report. The problem is that the report allegedly belongs to Mālik’s own legal world, through Medinan legal authorities, on a question that his legal project had every reason to preserve.
</ClaimBox>

This is why the Mālik test is so damaging. The hadith looks later than the world it claims to represent. It becomes prominent when jurists are defending legal method more explicitly. It is absent from the earlier Medinan legal monument where we would expect it to appear.

That does not prove every detail of its formation. But it weakens the claim that this was a settled prophetic doctrine in early Medina.

## Why al-Shāfiʿī Needed This Hadith

The hadith becomes especially useful in the period when Islamic legal theory becomes more self-conscious.

By the late second and early third Islamic centuries, jurists were not merely collecting isolated rulings. They were defending methods. They were arguing about analogy, reports, disagreement, authority, and the role of scholars when the Qur’an did not give a direct answer. This is the world in which a hadith rewarding ijtihād becomes invaluable.

Al-Shāfiʿī is important because his project required a disciplined theory of legal authority. He needed prophetic reports to stabilize law. He needed legal reasoning to operate where explicit texts were absent. He needed a way to defend the scholar’s effort without making every disagreement look like rebellion against God.

The judge-reward hadith does exactly that.

It says that legal striving itself is rewarded. It says that error does not destroy the judge’s status. It turns the jurist’s uncertainty into worship.

That is why the timing matters. The report becomes useful at precisely the moment when legal theorists needed a prophetic charter for their own method.

The doctrine develops.

Then the proof-text appears.

That pattern is suspicious.

## The Hadith Is Not Just About Sincerity

A defender may say, “The hadith only means that sincere effort is rewarded.”

But that is not what the wording is limited to.

The hadith speaks about the **ḥākim**, the one who judges or rules. It speaks about **ḥukm**, judgment. It speaks about **ijtihād**, striving to reach a ruling. This is not a general statement about a believer trying his best. It is a legal statement about an official act of judgment.

That difference matters.

A private believer who misunderstands something harms mostly himself. A judge who misunderstands something can harm other people. He can impose his mistake with force. He can make his error into a ruling.

The hadith still gives him reward.

This is why the report is so convenient. It does not merely console the sincere. It protects the institution.

## The Qur’anic Problem

The Qur’an does not teach people to follow a class of rewarded error-makers.

It repeatedly commands justice. It warns against following what one has no knowledge of. It condemns false attribution to God. It attacks religious authorities who consume people’s wealth and block the way of God. It tells people to bring proof. It does not give a blank spiritual guarantee to officials who bind others with mistaken rulings.

A Qur’anic moral instinct would ask different questions.

Was the ruling just?

Was there evidence?

Was anyone wronged?

Was the judge following truth or desire?

Did the ruling serve power?

Did the judgment attribute to God what God did not authorize?

The hadith avoids those questions. It moves the focus from the harmed person to the rewarded judge. It makes the judge’s effort the center of the moral universe.

That is not how justice should work.

## The Circularity Problem

The hadith is used to justify ijtihād.

But the hadith itself appears inside the very legal culture that needed ijtihād justified.

That is circular.

The jurist says: “Our legal reasoning is valid because the Prophet said the judge who strives is rewarded even when wrong.”

But when we ask where this prophetic statement comes from, we find it moving through jurists, judges, Medinan legal authorities, and later theorists whose authority depended on such reasoning being valid.

The class produces the doctrine. The doctrine protects the class. The class then cites the doctrine as divine authorization.

That is not evidence.

That is institutional self-sanctification.

<ContextNote>
This does not mean every act of legal reasoning is insincere. It means this particular report functions as a professional guarantee, and its transmission history gives us reason to suspect that the guarantee was produced by the profession itself.
</ContextNote>

## What Most Likely Happened

The most economical explanation is not that the Prophet publicly taught a doctrine rewarding mistaken judges, that the doctrine circulated through Medinan jurists, that Mālik somehow omitted it despite its relevance, that the report then surfaced through narrow legal bundles, and that later legal theorists just happened to find in it the perfect proof for their own authority.

That is possible, but it is not the best explanation.

The simpler explanation is that the report emerged from the legal culture that needed it.

Judges and jurists needed a theological account of disagreement and error. They needed to defend the legitimacy of ijtihād. They needed to protect the scholar’s authority when rulings differed. A report promising divine reward for judicial striving solved the problem perfectly. It made the legal profession’s weakness into its virtue.

If the judge is right, two rewards.

If he is wrong, one reward.

Either way, the judge wins.

That is exactly the kind of hadith a legal class would invent.

## Final Verdict

The “judge receives two rewards or one reward” hadith should not be treated as a neutral prophetic foundation for Islamic legal theory.

Its canonical spread narrows through Yazīd ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn al-Hād. Its Abū Hurayrah corroboration first appears as a verification story inside Yazīd’s own report. Its later Abū Hurayrah route follows that same Abū Bakr ibn Ḥazm → Abū Salamah → Abū Hurayrah line and reaches the collectors through ʿAbd al-Razzāq. The relevant transmitters belong to the legal world that benefited from the doctrine. Mālik’s *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, the great early monument of Medinan legal practice, does not preserve it despite its obvious relevance. Later legal theorists then use it exactly as one would expect: to sanctify ijtihād and protect juristic authority.

This is not a hadith about humility before God.

It is a hadith about institutional protection.

The legal class created a system in which its own reasoning could bind the community. Then it produced a prophetic reward structure for that system. The judge could be right and rewarded. The judge could be wrong and rewarded. The only person not protected by the doctrine was the person living under the wrong ruling.

That is why this report matters.

It shows how human legal authority becomes divine speech. It shows how a professional class can place its own interests into prophetic form. And it shows why hadith criticism cannot stop at whether a later scholar graded a chain authentic.

We have to ask what the report does.

This one rewards the judges.

That is the clue.
