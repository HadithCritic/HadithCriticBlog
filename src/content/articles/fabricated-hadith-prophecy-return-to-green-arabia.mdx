## The claim

The modern miracle claim usually begins with a climate headline. Arabia was once greener in the distant past, parts of it are greener today after rain, and the report says the land of the Arabs will “return” to meadows and rivers. The claim then treats the sentence as a scientifically precise prediction of modern climate change.

That framing skips the older historical problem. The report is not a free-floating climate statement. It is a small eschatological clause embedded in changing bundles about wealth, zakāt, safe travel, killing, and the signs of the Hour. Its transmission does not spread from many independent Companions. It converges on one family line: **Abū Hurayra → Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān → Suhayl b. Abī Ṣāliḥ**. The point is not merely that Suhayl appears in the chain. The point is that every recoverable line containing the distinctive “meadows and rivers” clause runs through Suhayl, and the earliest technical notice we have, from al-Bazzār, already says so.

<ClaimBox>
The “Green Arabia” report is best explained as a late Umayyad agrarian-apocalyptic tradition. Suhayl b. Abī Ṣāliḥ is the demonstrable common link and disseminator. The better motive, however, points one generation earlier to his father, Abū Ṣāliḥ Dhakwān al-Sammān al-Zayyāt: a Medinan client-scholar, a Medina-to-Kufa trader in ghee and oil, and an ʿUthmānī transmitter whose world was materially tied to pasture, irrigation, and the political imagination of land reclamation.
</ClaimBox>

That is more accurate than the older version of this blog. The old argument treated Suhayl as if the entire case depended on proving that he personally fabricated the report. The stronger argument is subtler. In isnād-cum-matn analysis, the common link is the point from which the report becomes visible and organized. He is not automatically the inventor. Here the matn, the family biography, and the historical setting make Abū Ṣāliḥ’s world the more plausible place to locate the kernel.

## The report in its best-known form

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لاَ تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى يَكْثُرَ الْمَالُ وَيَفِيضَ، حَتَّى يَخْرُجَ الرَّجُلُ بِزَكَاةِ مَالِهِ فَلاَ يَجِدُ أَحَدًا يَقْبَلُهَا مِنْهُ، وَحَتَّى تَعُودَ أَرْضُ الْعَرَبِ مُرُوجًا وَأَنْهَارًا.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until wealth becomes abundant and overflows, so that a man will go out with the zakāt of his wealth and find no one to accept it from him, and until the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers.”
>
> **Source:** Muslim, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 157c, through Qutayba b. Saʿīd → Yaʿqūb b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Qārī → Suhayl b. Abī Ṣāliḥ → his father → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Aḥmad preserves a near-identical form through the same Qutayba–Yaʿqūb route, but with the added clause that killing will become widespread:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى يَكْثُرَ الْمَالُ وَيَفِيضَ ... وَحَتَّى تَعُودَ أَرْضُ الْعَرَبِ مُرُوجًا وَأَنْهَارًا، وَحَتَّى يَكْثُرَ الْهَرْجُ. قَالُوا: وَمَا الْهَرْجُ يَا رَسُولَ اللهِ؟ قَالَ: الْقَتْلُ، الْقَتْلُ.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until wealth becomes abundant and overflows ... and until the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers, and until killing becomes widespread.” They said, “What is killing, Messenger of God?” He said, “Killing, killing.”
>
> **Source:** Aḥmad, *Musnad*, no. 9395, through Qutayba b. Saʿīd → Yaʿqūb → Suhayl → his father → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Another Aḥmad version changes the surrounding material. The greening clause comes first, then safe travel between Iraq and Mecca, then *harj*:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَعُودَ أَرْضُ الْعَرَبِ مُرُوجًا وَأَنْهَارًا، وَحَتَّى يَسِيرَ الرَّاكِبُ بَيْنَ الْعِرَاقِ وَمَكَّةَ لَا يَخَافُ إِلَّا ضَلَالَ الطَّرِيقِ، وَحَتَّى يَكْثُرَ الْهَرْجُ.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers, until a rider travels between Iraq and Mecca fearing nothing except losing the road, and until killing becomes widespread.”
>
> **Source:** Aḥmad, *Musnad*, no. 8833, through Muḥammad b. al-Ṣabbāḥ → Ismāʿīl b. Zakariyyā → Suhayl → his father → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Ibn Ḥibbān has a shorter form with only *harj* and greening:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى يَكْثُرَ الْهَرْجُ، وَحَتَّى تَعُودَ أَرْضُ الْعَرَبِ مُرُوجًا وَأَنْهَارًا.
</div>

> “The Hour will not be established until killing becomes widespread and until the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers.”
>
> **Source:** Ibn Ḥibbān, *Ṣaḥīḥ*, no. 6700, through Muḥammad b. Isḥāq b. Ibrāhīm → Qutayba b. Saʿīd → Yaʿqūb b. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān → Suhayl → his father → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Al-Ḥākim preserves the bare clause through Sufyān al-Thawrī, but his form contains an important hesitation:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
قَالَ سُفْيَانُ: لَا أَعْلَمُ إِلَّا قَدْ رَفَعَهُ. قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: «لَا تَقُومُ السَّاعَةُ حَتَّى تَعُودَ أَرْضُ الْعَرَبِ مُرُوجًا وَأَنْهَارًا».
</div>

> Sufyān said: “I only know that he raised it.” He said: the Messenger of God said, “The Hour will not be established until the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers.”
>
> **Source:** al-Ḥākim, *al-Mustadrak*, no. 8472, through al-Ḥusayn b. Ḥafṣ → Sufyān al-Thawrī → Suhayl → his father → Abū Hurayra.
</HadithBlock>

Sufyān’s hesitation matters. It does not destroy the report by itself, but it does show that the attribution was not as effortless as later apologetic citation makes it sound. The transmitter himself pauses over whether he knows it as a raised Prophetic statement.

The diagram corrects two common mistakes.

The first mistake is the apologetic one: treating the existence of multiple later citations as if they were independent prophetic witnesses. They are not. Muslim, Aḥmad, Ibn Ḥibbān, al-Ḥākim, al-Bazzār, and Abū ʿAwāna preserve different recensions, but the distinctive clause does not escape the line **Suhayl → Abū Ṣāliḥ → Abū Hurayra**.

The second mistake is the crude skeptical one: treating this as if there were no real transmission bundle at all. There is a bundle. After Suhayl, we have several students in different regional settings: Yaʿqūb al-Qārī in the Medinan line, Ismāʿīl b. Zakariyyā and Sufyān al-Thawrī in Kufan lines, and ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz b. al-Mukhtār in the Basran line. The problem is not that the whole thing is a single late citation. The problem is that the bundle begins only at Suhayl.

That distinction is important. The correct conclusion is not “one weak liar invented it.” The correct conclusion is that the report becomes visible as a structured tradition in Suhayl’s lifetime, while the content and family context point one generation earlier.

## al-Bazzār already noticed the bottleneck

The most important early critical note comes from al-Bazzār. After transmitting the fullest form through ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz b. al-Mukhtār from Suhayl, he says:

<HadithBlock>
<div class="hadith-block__arabic">
وَهَذَا الْحَدِيثُ قَدْ رُوِيَ بَعْضُهُ عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ مِنْ غَيْرِ وَجْهٍ، وَلَا نَعْلَمُ أَخَذَهُ إِلَّا سُهَيْلٌ عَنْ أَبِيهِ عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ.
</div>

> “Part of this ḥadīth has been narrated from Abū Hurayra by more than one route, but we do not know anyone who took it except Suhayl from his father from Abū Hurayra.”
>
> **Source:** al-Bazzār, *Musnad*, no. 9071.
</HadithBlock>

This is the key to the whole case. al-Bazzār is not saying that every surrounding theme is unique to Suhayl. The motifs of overflowing wealth, refused zakāt, *harj*, and secure travel have other lives in the hadith corpus. What is distinctive here is the way those motifs are joined to the clause that **the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers**. That clause is the family’s contribution.

The apologetic argument often points to Muslim or al-Ḥākim and stops there. But al-Bazzār’s note shows that the bottleneck was visible to early hadith critics themselves. This is not a modern hostile invention.

## Suhayl is the disseminator, not necessarily the author

Suhayl b. Abī Ṣāliḥ was not an unknown nobody. He was a known Medinan transmitter, and several authorities accepted him. That has to be stated clearly because the older blog’s rijāl section leaned too hard on selective negative judgments.

At the same time, he was not above criticism. Ibn Ḥajar summarizes him as truthful, but says his memory changed late. Critics also report that his Iraqi transmission was affected by forgetfulness. Abū Ḥātim’s formula is especially useful: his ḥadīth may be written, but is not used as proof. This is not a total rejection. It is a warning against building a miracle claim on a unique Suhayl-family clause.

The route profile matches that warning. The “Green Arabia” clause does not come through Mālik b. Anas, even though Mālik transmits from Suhayl elsewhere. It comes through later non-Medinan lines: Kufan, Basran, and routes tied to Suhayl’s wider post-Medina circulation. That is precisely the layer where the report becomes movable as an eschatological unit.

<ContextNote>
The point is methodological. If a report has a respectable common link, that does not make it Prophetic. A common link can be the systematic disseminator of a report that formed in his family, school, or social world. The isnād tells us where the report becomes recoverable. The matn and historical setting tell us why it would have been meaningful there.
</ContextNote>

## Why Abū Ṣāliḥ matters more than Suhayl

The real improvement in the argument is to move the motive one generation upstream.

Abū Ṣāliḥ Dhakwān al-Sammān al-Zayyāt, Suhayl’s father, is the figure whose profile most closely fits the report. He was a major transmitter from Abū Hurayra, and the chain **Abū Ṣāliḥ → Abū Hurayra** was one of the most useful ways to give Medinan authority to a report. But his biography adds details that make the “meadows and rivers” clause unusually intelligible.

First, he was called **al-Sammān**, the ghee-seller, and **al-Zayyāt**, the oil-seller. Ibn Abī Ḥātim says he used to bring ghee and oil from Medina to Kufa. These are not random products. Ghee presupposes pastoral abundance. Oil presupposes agricultural and irrigated production. The report’s central imagery is precisely the world of pasture and irrigated land.

Second, he moved along the Medina-Kufa corridor. That matters because the Umayyad and early Marwānid period was an age of estates, canals, land grants, and reclamation projects across Iraq, the Jazīra, Syria, and the Hijaz. A transmitter who regularly moved between Medina and Kufa did not need a modern satellite image to imagine “Arab land” becoming productive. He lived in the commercial orbit of that transformation.

Third, he was remembered as an ʿUthmānī. al-Mizzī reports that when ʿUthmān was mentioned, Abū Ṣāliḥ wept until his beard trembled. Some reports also place him among those who witnessed the siege of ʿUthmān’s house. This does not prove that he was a Marwānid propagandist. It does mean that his political temperament was not anti-Umayyad in the way a Kūfan anti-Marwānid rebel’s would be. A providential reading of agrarian abundance under the Umayyad order fits his world better than it fits a neutral Bedouin memory from the Prophet’s lifetime.

The report therefore makes sense as an al-Sammān household tradition. Abū Ṣāliḥ supplies the Abū Hurayra authority and the commercial-ideological setting. Suhayl supplies the later hadith bundle.

## The matn is not about hidden paleoclimate

Modern apologetics tends to read the phrase **“returns to meadows and rivers”** through prehistoric climate studies. But the pre-modern reception points in another direction. The commentators did not need to invoke a lost green Arabia tens of thousands of years in the past. They read the report as a sign of settlement, cultivation, and irrigation.

Al-Qurṭubī’s explanation is especially useful. He says the Arabs would turn away from their old habit of seeking rain-pastures and moving for war and raids, and would instead occupy themselves with planting the land, developing it, and channeling its waters. He even says this had already been seen in many of their lands and circumstances.

That matters because it destroys the modern claim that the report is impressive only if interpreted as geological or paleoclimatic foresight. A major medieval commentator read it as social and agrarian transformation, not a scientific prediction about deep-time climate cycles.

Ibn Kathīr also files the report among the signs of the Hour as a sign of abundance. In the hadith families themselves, the greening clause sits beside wealth overflowing, zakāt becoming hard to distribute, and the earth producing treasures. The point is not meteorology. The point is ominous abundance.

## The historical setting: reviving dead land

  caption="The motive-layer behind the report. The phrase “meadows and rivers” fits the commercial and agrarian world of Abū Ṣāliḥ and Suhayl better than a seventh-century climate miracle."
  ariaLabel="Motive diagram showing late Umayyad land reclamation, iḥyāʾ al-mawāt, canals, the Sawad and Syrian steppe, Abu Salih’s ghee and oil trade, and Suhayl’s dissemination of the Green Arabia report."
/>

The late Umayyad period had a legal and fiscal language for making land productive: **iḥyāʾ al-mawāt**, the reviving of dead land. In legal theory, someone who revived dead land could acquire it, and such land could be taxed more lightly. The imam could also grant land. In practice, this created both opportunity and grievance. Reclamation could reward loyal elites and increase revenue, while also concentrating land and putting pressure on cultivators.

This historical setting gives the report a motive. The phrase “the land of the Arabs returns to meadows and rivers” is not just a climate image. It is a religiously charged version of a real social process: canals, reclaimed land, estates, produce, taxation, and the transformation of frontier or marginal land into productive property.

That also explains why the report is ambivalent. It is framed as a sign of the Hour, so abundance is ominous. But the imagery itself is positive: pasture, water, fertility, wealth. It sacralizes an agrarian transformation while keeping it inside apocalyptic warning.

## What does “the land of the Arabs” mean?

A major weakness in the modern miracle reading is that it quietly assumes **arḍ al-ʿarab** means the modern Arabian Peninsula in a narrow geographical sense. That is not how the phrase always worked.

Classical geographers could define the land or peninsula of the Arabs elastically. al-Aṣmaʿī’s definition, preserved by al-Bakrī, runs from the farthest south to the marches of Syria, while other boundary descriptions reach toward the Sawād of Iraq and the Baṭāʾiḥ. This matters because the documented agrarian transformations of the Umayyad period were not limited to the interior of today’s Saudi Arabia. They were especially visible in Iraq, the Jazīra, Syria, and the frontier zones.

So even if one takes the report literally, it does not have to mean “Riyadh becomes Switzerland.” In the world of early Arabic geography, “Arab land” could include the very regions where reclamation and irrigation were historically active.

This is why the paper’s reconstruction is stronger than both modern apologetics and simplistic skepticism. It does not deny that landscapes changed. It asks whose world produced a prophecy in which Arab land became irrigated, pastoral, and productive.

## Why the surrounding clauses look borrowed

The Bazzār note matters because the greening clause does not float alone. It gets attached to other eschatological motifs.

The **overflowing wealth** motif has wider attestation. So does the image of a man offering zakāt and finding no one to accept it. The **harj** motif, defined as killing, is also widely known. The **safe travel between Iraq and Mecca** motif belongs to a recognizable political-security imagination. These motifs could circulate independently and be joined to new local material.

What the Suhayl bundle appears to have done is attach the distinctive agrarian image to familiar eschatological furniture. The report becomes more credible because it sounds like other end-times reports. But the distinctive line still points back to one family bundle.

This is how retrospective prophecy often works. A new historical concern is framed with older recognized motifs so that it sounds like inherited sacred memory.

## Why it is not enough to say “Muslim narrated it”

The report is in Muslim, and that matters for later Sunni reception. But canonical placement is not a time machine. Muslim’s version is still a later collection-level witness to a report whose transmission history runs through Suhayl. al-Ḥākim’s grading also does not solve the problem, because his own cited route through Sufyān contains hesitation over the raised attribution.

The issue is not whether the report can be made formally acceptable within later Sunni hadith grading. The issue is whether it is a clean miracle claim. It is not. A clean miracle claim would need an early, broad, independent transmission profile and a matn that cannot be better explained by the transmitter’s world. Here we have the opposite: a recoverable bottleneck, a family profile that fits the imagery, and a historical setting that explains the motive.

## The revised verdict

The older blog focused too much on whether Suhayl’s narrations are “weak.” That is not the best case. The best case is that the report is historically located.

Suhayl is the common link and disseminator. Abū Ṣāliḥ is the probable origin-layer. The surrounding clauses are standard eschatological pieces. The distinctive greening clause fits the al-Sammān family’s commercial world and the late Umayyad reclamation order. Classical commentary reads the report through settlement and irrigation, not modern paleoclimate. The phrase “land of the Arabs” itself is geographically elastic enough to include the very regions undergoing transformation.

<VerdictBox>
The “Green Arabia” report is best understood as an eschatological interpretation of late Umayyad agrarian transformation. It is not a clean prophecy of modern climate change. The distinctive clause enters the recoverable tradition through Suhayl b. Abī Ṣāliḥ, while the motive points to Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Sammān al-Zayyāt’s Medina-Kufa commercial world of pasture, oil, irrigation, and ʿUthmānī political memory. A land-reclamation world became an end-times sign.
</VerdictBox>
