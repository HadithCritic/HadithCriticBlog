::: passage 88:1-1 | 4:741-743

{L|The Overwhelming|al-ghāshiya} the calamity that overwhelms people with its hardships and wraps them in its terrors. He means the Resurrection, from His saying {p|742}“*The day will come when the retribution overwhelms them*” (Q 29:55). It is also said: the Fire, from His saying “*and fire will overwhelm their faces*” (Q 14:50) and “*they will have barriers above them*” (*wa-min fawqihim ghawāshin*, “and above them coverings,” Q 7:41).

{L|On that day|yawmaʾidhin} on the day when it overwhelms. “*Shamed*” (*khāshiʿa*, “humbled”): abased. “*Laboring and exhausted*”: they work in the Fire at work that wearies them, which is dragging the chains and fetters, wading through the Fire as camels wade through mud, climbing without rest up a slope of fire and going down a descent of it. It is also said: they did evil works in this world and took pleasure and delight in them, and so are in weariness from them in the Hereafter. It is also said: they worked and wearied themselves in works that will be of no use to them in the Hereafter, from His saying “*We will look at all the works they have done, and render them null and void*” (Q 25:23), “*but they think that they are doing good. Such are the ones who disbelieved in the revelations of their Lord and in meeting Him. Therefore, their works are in vain*” (Q 18:104–105). It is also said: they are the men of the hermitages, and its meaning is that they humbled themselves to God and worked and wearied themselves in their works, of unending fasting and unbroken night vigil. *ʿĀmilatan nāṣibatan* is also read, in the accusative as a term of reviling.

“*Suffering*” (*taṣlā*, “will burn”): *taṣlā* is read, with *fatḥa* on the *tāʾ*; *tuṣlā*, with *ḍamma*; and *tuṣallā*, with doubling. It is said that *al-muṣallā* among the Arabs is when they dig a pit, gather many embers in it, then take a sheep and bury it in the middle. What is roasted on top of embers, or in a pan, or in an oven, is not called *muṣallā*.

{L|Flaming|āniya} at the utmost of heat, as in His saying “*They will circulate between it and an intolerable inferno*” (*wa-bayna ḥamīmin ān*, “and between scalding water, boiling,” Q 55:44).

*Al-ḍarīʿ* is dried *shibriq*, a kind of thorn that camels graze while it is fresh; when it dries the camels shun it, and it is a deadly poison. Abū Dhuʾayb said: “*It grazed the moist shibriq until, when it withered and turned to ḍarīʿ, the barren she-camels kept away from it*.” And [another] said: “*They were penned in the crumbling ḍarīʿ, and every one of them is hunched, bloody in the forelegs, dry*.” {p|743}

{Q} How is it said “*They will have no food except the useless variety*” (*laysa lahum ṭaʿāmun illā min ḍarīʿ*), while in al-Ḥāqqa it is “*Nor any food, except the bitter variety*” (*illā min ghislīn*, Q 69:36)? {A} The punishment is of many colors, and those punished are in ranks: some of them eat *zaqqūm*, some eat *ghislīn*, and some eat *ḍarīʿ*: “*Each gate will get a specific share of them*” (Q 15:44).

{L|It never nourishes|lā yusminu} in the place of a nominative or a genitive, as a description of “*food*” or of *ḍarīʿ*. He means that their food is of something that is not among the foods of humans. It is thorns, and thorns are among what camels graze and are fond of, yet this is a kind of them that camels shy from and do not go near. The two uses of food are both absent from it: driving off hunger, and giving the body strength and fat. Or what is meant is that they have no food at all, because *ḍarīʿ* is not food for beasts, let alone for humans, since food is what satisfies or fattens, and it is far from both; as you say “So-and-so has no shade but the sun,” meaning to deny shade emphatically. It is said that the unbelievers of Quraysh said, “Our camels grow fat on *ḍarīʿ*,” and “*It never nourishes*” was revealed. Then either they were lying and being obstinate in this, which is the plain sense, and their saying is answered by denying both the fattening and the satisfying; or they were telling the truth, and the meaning is that their food is of a *ḍarīʿ* not of the kind of your *ḍarīʿ*, but of a *ḍarīʿ* that neither nourishes nor avails against hunger.

::: passage 88:2-16 | 4:743-744

{L|Full of joy|nāʿima} full of joy and beauty, as in His saying “*You recognize in their faces the joy of bliss*” (Q 83:24); or living in bliss. “*Satisfied with their work*”: pleased with their work when they see the honor and reward it has brought them to. “*Exalted*”: high in place or in rank.

{L|No nonsense is heard|lā tasmaʿu} you who are addressed; or the faces. “*Nonsense*” (*lāghiya*, “idle talk”) (*lāghiya*): that is, idle speech (*laghw*), or a word that has idleness in it, or a soul that talks idly. The people of Paradise speak only wisdom and praise of God for the lasting bliss He has given them. *Lā tusmaʿu* is also read, passive, with *tāʾ* and with *yāʾ*.

{L|In it, a spring flows|fīhā ʿaynun jāriya} He means springs beyond counting, {p|744}as in His saying *ʿalimat nafsun* (“a soul will know,” Q 81:14).

{L|Luxurious|marfūʿa} high in rank or in height, so that the believer, sitting on it, may see all the dominion and bliss his Lord has given him. It is also said: stored up for them, from *rafaʿa l-shayʾa*, when one stores a thing away.

{L|Made available|mawḍūʿa} whenever they want them they find them set before them, ready and at hand, with no need to call for them; or set on the edges of the springs, ready for drinking. It may also mean: set below the size of the large ones, middling between small and large, as in His saying “*they rightly deserved all this*” (*qaddarūhā taqdīran*, “they measured them exactly,” Q 76:16).

{L|In rows|maṣfūfa} one beside another: cushions for leaning and lying on; wherever he wants to sit, he sits on one cushion and leans on another. “*And carpets*” (*zarābiyy*): rich, wide rugs; it is also said, the rugs that have a fine pile; the plural of *zarbiyya*. “*Throughout*” (*mabthūtha*, “spread out”): laid out; or scattered about the sitting places.

{L|Why do they not reflect on the camels|a-fa-lā yanẓurūna ilā l-ibil} with a look that takes a lesson, “*how they are created*”: a wondrous creation, pointing to the measuring of One who measures and bearing witness to the planning of One who plans. For He created them to rise up with heavy loads and carry them to distant lands; made them kneel, so that they are loaded from close by and with ease, then rise with what they carry; and made them docile to anyone who leads them by their reins: they do not resist the weak or refuse the small. He made them long-necked so that they can rise with their burdens. From one of the sages: he was told about the camel and its wonderful making, having grown up in a land with no camels, and he thought and said, “*They must be long-necked*.” And since He wished them to be the ships of the land, He made them patient in bearing thirst, so that the spans between their waterings rise to ten days and more, and made them graze every growing thing in the wilderness and the deserts, of what no other beast grazes. From Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: “*I met Shurayḥ the judge and said, ‘Where are you going?’ He said, ‘{p|745}I am going to al-Kunāsa.’ I said, ‘What will you do there?’ He said, ‘Look at the camels, how they were created.’*”

{Q} How is it fitting to mention the camels together with the sky, the mountains and the earth, when there is no kinship between them? {A} The gaze of the Arabs in their valleys and deserts took these things in together, so the mention took them in together as their gaze did. Nothing but the search for kinship moved the one who claimed that *al-ibil* is the clouds to say so. Perhaps he did not mean that *ibil* is one of the names of the clouds, like *al-ghamām*, *al-muzn*, *al-rabāb*, *al-ghaym*, *al-ghayn* and the rest, but saw clouds likened to camels often in their poetry, and allowed that clouds might be meant by way of likeness and figure.

{L|How it is raised|kayfa rufiʿat} raised to a far height, held up without pillars. “*How they are constructed*”: set firm, so that they are rooted and do not lean or move. “*How it is built*” (*kayfa suṭiḥat*, “how it was spread out”): spread out, smoothed and leveled, so that it is a bed for those who move about on it. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib ﵁ read *khalaqtu*, *rafaʿtu*, *naṣabtu*, *saṭaḥtu*, active, with the *tāʾ* of the pronoun; the implied sense is “I did it,” and the object is left out. From Hārūn al-Rashīd: he read *suṭṭiḥat*, with doubling. The meaning: do they not look at these created things that bear witness to the Creator’s power, so as not to deny His power to raise the dead, and so listen to the warning of the Messenger ﷺ, believe in it and make ready to meet Him? That is: they do not look; so remind them and do not press them, and let it not trouble you that they do not look or take heed.

{L|Your mission is to deliver this reminder|innamā anta mudhakkir} as in His saying “*Your sole mission is delivering the message*” (Q 42:48). “*You have no power over them*” (*bi-muṣayṭir*): one with power over them, as in His saying “*while you have no power over them*” (Q 50:45). It is said that in the dialect of Tamīm it has *fatḥa* on the *ṭāʾ*, since *sayṭara* is transitive for them; their saying *tasayṭara* points to this.

{L|As for those who turn away|illā man tawallā} an exception that stands apart: that is, you have no mastery over them, but whoever of them turns away “*and disbelieve*,” to God belong mastery and overpowering, and He will punish him with “*the great retribution*,” which is the punishment of Hell. It is also said that it is an exception from His saying “*You shall remind*”: that is, remind, except the one whose faith you have lost hope of, who has turned away and so deserves the greatest punishment; and what is between them is a parenthesis. *A-lā man tawallā* is also read, as a call to attention. In the reading of Ibn Masʿūd: *fa-innahu yuʿadhdhibuhu*. Abū Jaʿfar al-Madanī read *iyyābahum*, with doubling. Its explanation is that it is *fayʿāl*, the verbal noun of *ayyaba*, a *fayʿala* form from *iyāb*; or that its root is *iwwāb*, a *fiʿʿāl* form from *awwaba*, which was then said *iywāb*, as *dīwān* is from *diwwān*, and then what was done to the roots of *sayyid* and *mayyit* was done to it.

{Q} What is the meaning of putting the adverbial phrase first? {A} Its meaning is to make the threat harsher: that their return is to none but the Almighty who has power to take revenge, and that their reckoning is binding on none but Him, and He is the one who reckons for the speck on a date stone and its thin skin. The meaning of “binding” is binding in wisdom.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat al-Ghāshiya, God will reckon with him an easy reckoning*.”
