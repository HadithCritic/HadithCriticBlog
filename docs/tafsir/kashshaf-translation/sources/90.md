revelation: Meccan. Twenty verses. [Revealed after Qāf (50).]

::: passage 90:1-7 | 4:753-755

He swore, glory be to Him, by the Sacred Town and what comes after it, that man was created plunged in enduring hardships and adversities; and He put between the oath and what is sworn to His saying “*The town where you live*” (*wa-anta ḥillun bi-hādhā l-balad*, “and you are ḥill in this town”). He means: part of the hardship is that one like you, for all your great sanctity, is treated as fair game in this sacred town, as game is outside the sanctuary. From Shuraḥbīl: they hold it forbidden to kill game in it or cut its trees, yet hold it lawful to drive you out and kill you. In this is a steadying of the Messenger of God ﷺ, an urging to bear what he was enduring from the people of Mecca, and a cause for wonder at their state in their enmity to him.

Or He consoled the Messenger of God ﵌ by swearing {p|754}by his town that man is never free of suffering hardships, and put between them His promise to him of the conquest of Mecca, to complete the consolation and the relief. So He said *wa-anta ḥillun bi-hādhā l-balad*, meaning: you will be free in it in the future, doing in it what you wish of killing and taking captive. That is because God opened Mecca to him and made it lawful for him; it was not opened to anyone before him or made lawful for him. So he made lawful what he willed and forbade what he willed. He killed Ibn Khaṭal while he clung to the curtains of the Kaʿba, and Miqyas ibn Ṣubāba and others, and made the house of Abū Sufyān inviolable. Then he said: “*God made Mecca sacred on the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it is sacred until the Hour comes. It was not lawful for anyone before me and will not be lawful for anyone after me, and it was lawful for me only for an hour of a day. So its trees are not to be cut, its fresh grass is not to be mown, its game is not to be startled, and what is lost in it is not lawful except to one who announces it*.” Al-ʿAbbās said, “*Messenger of God, except the idhkhir, for it is for our smiths, our graves and our houses*.” He ﷺ said, “*Except the idhkhir*.”

{Q} Where is a parallel to His saying *wa-anta ḥillun* in the sense of the future? {A} His saying, mighty and majestic is He, “*You (Muhammad) will surely die, just like they will die*” (Q 39:30). The like of it is common in human speech: you say to one you promise honor and gifts, “You are honored and gifted.” It is more common still in the speech of God, because future states are with Him like present, seen ones. A decisive proof that it refers to the future, and that explaining it as the present is impossible, is enough for you: the sūra is Meccan by agreement, and how far is the Emigration from the time of its revelation, let alone the Conquest?

{Q} What is meant by “*The begetting and the begotten*” (*wa-wālidin wa-mā walad*, “a father and what he begot”)? {A} The Messenger of God ﵌ and those who begot him. He swore by his town, which is his birthplace, the sanctuary of his father Abraham and the place where his father Ishmael grew up, and by those who begot him, and by him.

{Q} Why is it indefinite? {A} For the vagueness that by itself conveys praise and wonder.

{Q} Why was it not said “and whom he begot”? {A} In it is what is in His saying “*GOD was fully aware of what she bore*” (Q 3:36), that is, what thing she bore; He means a child of wondrous standing. It is also said that they are Adam and his children; and it is also said, every father and child.

*Al-kabad*: its root is from your saying *kabida l-rajulu kabadan, fa-huwa akbadu*, when his liver (*kabid*) ached and swelled. Then its use was widened until it was used of every toil and hardship. From it *al-mukābada* (“enduring hardship”) was derived, as it is said *kabatahu* in the sense “he destroyed him,” its root being *kabadahu*, when he struck his liver. Labīd said: “*O eye, why did you not weep for Arbad, when we stood and the adversaries stood in kabad?*” {p|755}that is, in the stress of the matter and the difficulty of the affair.

{L|Does he think|a-yaḥsabu} the pronoun is for one of the chiefs of Quraysh, from whom the Messenger of God ﷺ endured what he endured. The meaning: does this chief, strong among his people and treating the believers as weak, think that there will be no Resurrection, and that no one will have power to take revenge on him and repay him for what he is about? Then He mentioned what he says on that day: that he says “*I spent so much money!*” (*ahlaktu mālan lubadan*, “I have squandered wealth in heaps”), meaning how much he spent on what the people of the Jāhiliyya called noble deeds and claimed as high merits and glories. “*Does he think that no one sees him*” when he spent what he spent to be seen by people and to boast among them? He means that God saw him and was watching over him.

The pronoun may also be for man, with the meaning: I swear by this noble town, and part of its nobility is that you, dwelling in it, are scrupulous and free of the sins its people commit; so it deserves that I honor it by swearing by it. “*We created the human being to work hard*” (*fī kabad*): in sickness, which is sickness of the heart and corruption within; He means those of whom God knew, when He created them, that they would not believe or do righteous deeds. It is also said that the one who thinks that no one will have power over him is Abū l-Ashadd. He was strong: an ʿUkāẓ hide would be spread out for him, and he would stand on it and say, “*Whoever moves me off it shall have such-and-such*,” and it could only be pulled out from under him in pieces, while the place of his feet remained. It is also said: al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra.

{L|So much money|lubadan} read with *ḍamma* and with *kasra* (*libadan*), the plural of *lubda* and *libda*, which is what is matted together; He means abundance. *Lubudan* is also read, with two *ḍamma*s, the plural of *lubūd*; and *lubbadan*, with doubling, the plural of *lābid*.

{L|Did we not give him two eyes|a-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn} with which he sees what is to be seen; “*A tongue*” with which he gives voice to what is in his mind; “*and two lips*” which he closes over his mouth and which help him in speaking, eating, drinking, blowing and the like. “*Did we not show him the two paths?*” (*wa-hadaynāhu l-najdayn*, “and guided him to the two high roads”): that is, the two ways of good and evil. It is also said: the two breasts.

{L|He should choose the difficult path|fa-lā qtaḥama l-ʿaqaba} He means: he did not give thanks for those gifts and favors with righteous deeds, freeing slaves and feeding orphans and the poor, and then with faith, {p|756}which is the root of all obedience and the foundation of all good; rather, he made light of the favors and was ungrateful to the One who gave them. The meaning: spending in this way is the spending that is pleasing and profitable with God, not squandering wealth in heaps on show and boasting, so that his likeness would be “*like a violent wind that hits the harvest of people*” (*ka-mathali rīḥin fīhā ṣirrun*, “the likeness of a wind with frost in it,” Q 3:117).

{Q} *Lā* entering on the past is seldom found unrepeated, and the like of the saying “*and what evil deed did he not do*” hardly occurs. So why is it not repeated in the most eloquent speech? {A} It is repeated in meaning, because the meaning of “*He should choose the difficult path*” (*fa-lā qtaḥama l-ʿaqaba*, “but he did not attempt the steep path”) is: he did not free a slave, and he did not feed a poor man. Do you not see that attempting the steep path is explained by that? Al-Zajjāj said: His saying “*And being one of those who believe*” (*thumma kāna mina lladhīna āmanū*) points to the meaning “he did not attempt the steep path, and he did not believe.”

*Al-iqtiḥām* is entering and crossing with force and hardship, and *al-quḥma* is hardship. He made the righteous deed a steep path, and doing it an attempting of it, because of the hardship to be borne and the struggle with oneself in it. From al-Ḥasan: “*A steep path, by God, and a hard one: a man’s struggle with himself, his desire and his enemy, Satan*.”

{L|The freeing of slaves|fakku raqaba} freeing a neck is releasing it from slavery or something else. In the ḥadīth: a man said to the Messenger of God ﷺ, “*Point me to a deed that will bring me into Paradise*.” He said, “*Set a soul free and free a neck*.” He said, “*Are they not the same?*” He said, “*No. Setting it free is freeing it on your own; freeing it is helping to release it from retaliation or a debt*.” Setting free and alms are among the best of deeds. From Abū Ḥanīfa ﵁: setting free is better than alms; and according to his two companions, alms is better. The verse points more to the view of Abū Ḥanīfa, since setting free is put before alms. From al-Shaʿbī, about a man who has spending money left over, whether he should give it to a relative or free a slave: he said, “*The slave is better, because the Prophet ﷺ said: ‘Whoever frees a neck, God frees for every limb of it a limb of his from the Fire.’*” *Fakku raqabatin aw iṭʿāmun* is read, meaning “it is the freeing of a neck or feeding”; and *fakka raqabatan aw aṭʿama* is also read, as a substitute for *iqtaḥama l-ʿaqaba*. His saying “*Which one is the difficult path?*” is a parenthesis; its meaning is that you have not grasped the true measure of its difficulty for the self, or of its reward with God.

*Al-masghaba*, *al-maqraba* and *al-matraba* are *mafʿala* forms: from *saghiba*, when he was hungry; from *qaruba*, in kinship, as one says “So-and-so is a man of my kinship” and “a man of my *maqraba*”; and from *tariba*, when he became poor, its meaning being that he clung to the dust (*turāb*). As for *atraba*, it means he became rich, that is, became {p|757}a man of wealth like dust in its abundance, as is said *athrā*. From the Prophet ﷺ on His saying “*the poor who is in need*” (*dhā matraba*): the one whose shelter is the rubbish heaps. Describing the day as “*during the time of hardship*” (*dhī masghaba*, “of hunger”) is like what the grammarians say about their saying *hammun nāṣibun*, that it means “of toil.” Al-Ḥasan read *dhā masghabatin*, in the accusative through “*feeding*”; its meaning is: or feeding, on one of the days, one who is hungry.

{L|And being one of those who believe|thumma kāna mina lladhīna āmanū} He brought *thumma* (“then”) because faith lies far beyond freeing slaves and alms in rank and merit, not in time, since faith is what comes first and before all else, and no righteous deed is established without it. *Al-marḥama* is mercy: that is, they enjoined one another to patience in faith and to holding firm to it; or to patience in keeping from sins and in obedience and the trials the believer is tried with; and to being merciful and kind to one another; or to what leads to God’s mercy.

{L|Happiness … misery|al-maymana … al-mashʾama} the right hand and the left; or good fortune and ill: that is, those who bring good fortune on themselves and those who bring ill fortune on themselves. *Mūṣada* is read, with *wāw*, and with *hamza* (*muʾṣada*), from *waṣadtu l-bāba* and *āṣadtuhu*, when you close and shut the door. From Abū Bakr ibn ʿAyyāsh: “*We have an imam who reads muʾṣada with hamza, and I long to stop my ears when I hear him*.”

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites ‘No, I swear by this town,’ God will give him safety from His anger on the Day of Resurrection*.”
