*Arayta* is also read, with the *hamza* dropped. It is not the preferred reading, because dropping it belongs to the imperfect only, and *rayta* is not soundly attested from the Arabs; what made it easier here is that the interrogative particle comes at the head of the speech. Like it is: “*Friend, have you seen (rayta) or heard of a herdsman who put back into the udder what he had gathered in the milking pail?*” {p|804}Ibn Masʿūd read *a-raʾaytaka*, with the particle of address added, as in His saying “*Since You have honored him over me*” (*a-raʾaytaka hādhā lladhī karramta ʿalayya*, Q 17:62).

The meaning: do you know who the one is who denies the recompense? If you do not know him, then “*that*” one who denies the recompense is the one “*who mistreats the orphans*” (*yaduʿʿu l-yatīm*, “who repels the orphan”), that is, pushes him away violently, with harshness and harm, and turns him back in an ugly way, with scolding and roughness. *Yadaʿu* is also read, that is, he leaves him and neglects him. “*And does not advocate*”: he does not urge his household to give food to the poor.

He made withholding kindness and daring to harm the weak the sign of denying the recompense. That is, if he believed in the recompense and were certain of the threat, he would fear God Most High and His punishment and would not dare to do it; so when he dares to do it, it is known that he denies it. How severe a speech this is, how fearful a station, and how forceful a warning against disobedience, showing that it may fairly be taken as evidence of weak faith and a slack hold on certainty!

Then He joined to it His saying “*And woe to those who observe the contact prayers (Salat)*,” as if He said: since the matter is so, woe to those who pray, who are heedless of the prayer, caring little for it, so that it escapes them or its time runs out; or who do not pray it as the Messenger of God ﷺ and the early generations prayed it, but peck at it without humility or submission, and without avoiding what is disliked in it, such as toying with the beard and the clothes and much yawning and looking around, so that one of them does not know after how many [*rakʿa*s] he finished or which sūras he recited. So you see the prayer of most of those you see, whose habit is to show off their deeds and withhold what is due from their wealth. The meaning: such people have the better claim to have their heedlessness of the prayer (which is the pillar of the religion and the line between faith and unbelief), their showing off (which is a branch of associating partners with God), and their withholding of the *zakāt* (which is the sister of the prayer and the bridge of Islam) taken as a sign that they deny the religion. How many you see of those who bear the name of Islam, indeed of the scholars among them, who fit this description. O what a calamity!

Another way: “*that*” is joined to “*who really rejects the faith*” (*alladhī yukadhdhibu*, “the one who denies”), either as one person joined to another or as one description joined to another, and the answer to “*Do you know*” (*a-raʾayta*, “have you seen”) is left out because what follows points to it, as if it were said: tell me, what do you say of one who denies the recompense, and of one who harms the orphan and does not feed the poor? Is what he does good? Then He said “*And woe to those who observe the contact prayers (Salat)*,” that is, since it is known that he does wrong, woe to those who pray, in the sense of woe to them; only He put their description in the place of their pronoun, because along with denial and what is ascribed {p|805}to them they were heedless of the prayer, showing off, and not paying the *zakāt* on their wealth.

{Q} How did you make “*those who observe the contact prayers*” (*al-muṣallīn*, “those who pray”) stand in the place of the pronoun of *alladhī yukadhdhibu*, when that is singular? {A} Its meaning is plural, because the kind is meant.

{Q} What is the difference between His saying “*of their prayers*” (*ʿan ṣalātihim*) and your saying “*in their prayer*” (*fī ṣalātihim*)? {A} The sense of *ʿan* is that they are heedless of it, with the heedlessness of leaving it and paying it little attention, and that is what hypocrites do, or the dissolute rascals among the Muslims. The sense of *fī* is that lapses come over them in it, through the whispering of a devil or the talk of the self, and hardly any Muslim is free of that. The Messenger of God ﷺ had lapses in his prayer, let alone anyone else, and that is why the jurists set down a chapter on the prostration of forgetfulness in their books. From Anas ﵁: “*Praise be to God that He did not say ‘in their prayer.’*” Ibn Masʿūd read *lāhūn* (“distracted”).

{Q} What does showing off (*murāʾāh*) mean? {A} It is the pattern *mufāʿala* from *irāʾa* (“showing”), because the show-off shows people his deed and they show him their praise of him and their admiration. A man is not a show-off for displaying a good deed if it is obligatory, for it is the right of obligations to be announced and made known, because of his saying ﵊: “*There is no concealment in the obligations of God*”; for they are the marks of Islam and the rites of the religion, and the one who leaves them deserves blame and contempt, so suspicion has to be removed by displaying them. If it is voluntary, its right is to be hidden, because no one is blamed for leaving it and there is no suspicion in it; and if he displays it meaning to be followed in it, that is good. Showing off is only to mean, by displaying it, that eyes should see him, so that he is praised for his righteousness. From one of them: he saw a man in the mosque who had made a prostration of thanks and drawn it out, and he said, “*How good this would be if it were in your house!*” He said this only because he saw signs of showing off and seeking a name in him. Yet avoiding showing off is hard except for those trained in sincerity. That is why the Messenger of God ﷺ said: “*Showing off is more hidden than the creeping of a black ant on a black haircloth on a dark night*.”

{L|Charity|al-māʿūn} the *zakāt*. Al-Rāʿī said: “*A people firm in Islam, who have not withheld their māʿūn or neglected the tahlīl*.” {p|806}From Ibn Masʿūd: what people commonly lend one another, such as the ax, the pot, the bucket, the fire-steel and the like. From ʿĀʾisha: water, fire and salt. Withholding these things may be forbidden by the law when they are borrowed out of need, and ugly as a matter of decency when there is no need.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat a-raʾayta, God will forgive him, if he paid the zakāt*.”
