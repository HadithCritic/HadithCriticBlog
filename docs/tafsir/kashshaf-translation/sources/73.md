::: passage 73:1-4 | 4:634-637

{L|O you cloaked one|al-muzzammil} is *al-mutazammil*, one who has wrapped himself (*tazammala*) in his clothes, that is, rolled himself in them, with the *tāʾ* assimilated to the *zāy*; and like it is *al-muddaththir* for *al-mutadaththir*. *Al-mutazammil* is also read, on the origin, and *al-muzammil*, with the *zāy* light and the *mīm* with *fatḥa* and *kasra*, as an active or passive participle from *zammalahu*: the one whom another wrapped, or who wrapped himself. The Messenger of God ﷺ was asleep at night, wrapped in a velvet cloak; and he was woken and called by what made the state he was in look ugly to him, of being wrapped in his cloak and making ready to sink into sleep, as one does whom no matter concerns and no affair occupies. Do you not see the saying of Dhū l-Rumma:

> How many a desert has my she-camel crossed, / and how many a sleeper, wrapped up, away from its night!

{p|635}He means the lazy one who holds back and does not rise to great matters and the meeting of crises, and does not lay hardships and toils on himself. Like it is:

> She bore him wild of heart, lean of belly, / wakeful when the dullard sleeps the night.

And in their proverbs:

> Saʿd brought them to water while Saʿd was wrapped up; / not so, Saʿd, are camels brought to water.

{p|636}So he blamed him for wrapping himself in his cloak, and made that the opposite of hardiness and briskness. And he was commanded to choose night vigil over sleeping, and over wrapping up, girding himself and lightening himself for worship and striving in God. No wonder, then, that the Messenger of God ﷺ girded himself for that with his companions as he should, and they turned to bringing their nights to life, refused sleep and ease for it, and strove in it until their feet swelled, their colors grew pale, the mark showed in their faces, and their matter reached a point at which their Lord had mercy on them for it and lightened it for them.

It is also said: he was wrapped in a woolen wrap of ʿĀʾisha’s, praying. On this, it is not a making ugly but a praise of him and a commending of the state he was in, and he was commanded to keep at it and hold to it. From ʿĀʾisha ﵂: she was asked what his wrapping was. She said, “*It was a wrap fourteen cubits long, half of it on me while I slept and half of it on him while he prayed*.” She was asked, “*What was it?*” She said, “*By God, it was not silk-wool, nor raw silk, nor goat’s down, nor silk thread, nor wool: its warp was hair and its weft camel’s hair*.” It is also said: he came in to Khadīja, frightened with fear, when Gabriel first came to him, with the flesh between his shoulders and neck trembling, and he said, “*Wrap me up, wrap me up*,” and he thought something had befallen him; and while he was in that state Gabriel called him, “*O you wrapped one*.” From ʿIkrima: the meaning is, O you who have taken up (*zammala*) a great matter, that is, carried it; *al-zaml* is carrying, and *izdamalahu* is “he carried it.”

*Qumu l-layla* is also read, with *ḍamma* and with *fatḥa* on the *mīm*. ʿUthmān ibn Jinnī said: the aim of this vowel is to get by with it, escaping the meeting of two quiescent letters; so whichever vowel it is moved with, the aim has been met. “*Half of it*” is a substitute for “*the night*,” and “*except rarely*” (*illā qalīlan*, “except a little”) is an exception from the half, as though He said: stand less than half the night. The pronoun in “*from it*” and “*to it*” is for the half. And the meaning is a choice between two matters: between standing less than half the night outright, and choosing one of two matters, which are taking from the half and adding to it. And if you wish, you make {p|637}“*half of it*” a substitute for “*a little*,” and it is a choice among three: between standing the half in full, standing less than it, and standing more than it; and the half was only described as little in relation to the whole. And if you wish you say: since the meaning of “*Meditate during the night, except rarely. Half of it*,” when you make the half a substitute for the night, is “stand less than half the night,” the pronoun in “*from it*” and “*to it*” goes back to the less than half; as though it were said: stand less than half the night, or stand less than that less, or a little more than it. Then the choice is in what is below the half, between it and the third. When you make “*half of it*” a substitute for “*a little*” and explain it by it, you may also make the second “*a little*” in the sense of half of the half, which is the quarter, as though it were said: or take from it a little, half of it. And you make what is added to this little, I mean the quarter, half of the quarter, as though it were said: or add to it a little, half of it. You may also make the addition, because it is unrestricted, the completion of the third; so it is a choice among the half, the third and the quarter.

{Q} Was the standing an obligation or a supererogation? {A} From ʿĀʾisha ﵂: God made it voluntary after it had been an obligation. It is also said: it was an obligation before the five prayers were made obligatory, then it was abrogated by them, except what they did voluntarily. From al-Ḥasan: standing a third of the night was an obligation, and they kept to that for a year. It is also said: it was required, and the choice fell only in the amount; then it was abrogated after ten years. From al-Kalbī: a man would stand until morning for fear of not keeping what is between the half, the third and the two thirds. And some of them said it was a supererogation, by proof of the choice in the amount, and for the saying of God Most High “*During the night, you shall meditate for extra credit*” (Q 17:79).

{L|From cover to cover|tartīl} of the Qurʾān is reciting it in an unhurried and deliberate way, making the letters clear and giving the vowels their full value, so that what is recited of it comes like a well-spaced row of teeth (*thaghr murattal*), which is gapped and likened to the camomile flower; and that he does not race through it nor run it on, as ʿUmar ﵁ said: “*The worst going is the hard driving, and the worst reciting is the gabbling*”; so that what is recited, in its running together, is like close-set teeth. ʿĀʾisha ﵂ was asked about the recitation of the Messenger of God ﷺ, and she said, “*Not like this running-on of yours. If the listener wanted to count its letters, he could count them*.” “*From cover to cover*” (*tartīlan*) is an emphasis in making the command to it binding, and that it is something the reciter cannot do without.

::: passage 73:5-5 | 4:637-638

This verse is a parenthesis. By the heavy word He means the Qurʾān and what is in it of commands and prohibitions, which are {p|638}hard and heavy obligations on those charged; especially on the Messenger of God ﷺ, because he carries them himself and makes his community carry them, so they are heavier on him and more burdensome to him. By this parenthesis He meant that what He charged him with, of standing at night, is among the heavy and hard obligations that the Qurʾān came with; because the night is the time of sleep, rest and stillness, so whoever brings it to life must go against his nature and strive against himself. From Ibn ʿAbbās ﵁: when revelation came down on him, it was heavy on him and his skin grew dark with it. From ʿĀʾisha ﵂: “*I saw revelation coming down on him on a day of hard cold, and it would leave him, and his forehead would be streaming with sweat*.” From al-Ḥasan: heavy in the balance. It is also said: heavy on the hypocrites. It is also said: speech that has weight and worth, not of the trivial.

::: passage 73:6-6 | 4:638-639

{L|The meditation at night|nāshiʾata l-layli} the soul that rises by night, that rises from its bed to worship, that is, gets up and lifts itself; from *nashaʾati l-saḥābatu*, when the cloud rose; and *nashaʾa min makānihi* and *nashaza*, when he got up. He said:

> We rose to hollow-eyed camels whose fat the night journey had pared, / and which it had worn down to their jutting napes.

Or standing at night, on the view that *al-nāshiʾa* is a verbal noun from *nashaʾa*, when he stood and got up, on the pattern *fāʿila*, like *al-ʿāqiba*. What is related from ʿUbayd ibn ʿUmayr points to it: I said to ʿĀʾisha, “*A man who stood from the beginning of the night, would you say of him that he stood a nāshiʾa?*” She said, “*No; the nāshiʾa is only standing after sleep*.” So she explained the *nāshiʾa* as standing from the bed. Or the worship that arises at night, that is, comes about and goes up. It is also said: it is all the hours of the night, because they come about one after another. It is also said: its first hours. From ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn ﵄: he used to pray between the sunset and the evening prayers and say, “*Have you not heard the saying of God Most High, ‘The rising by night’? This is the rising of the night*.” “*more effective*” (*ashaddu waṭʾan*, “firmer in tread”): it alone, and not the rising of the day, is firmer in agreement, its heart agreeing with its tongue, if you mean the soul; or the heart of the one standing agrees in it with {p|639}his tongue, if you mean the standing, or the worship, or the hours. Or firmer in agreeing with what is wanted of humility and sincerity. From al-Ḥasan: firmer in agreement between the secret and the open, because the sight of creatures is cut off. *Ashaddu waṭāʾan* is also read, with *fatḥa* and with *kasra*. And the meaning is: firmer of footing and farther from slipping; or heavier and harder on the one who prays than the prayer of the day, from his saying ﵇, “*O God, make Your tread hard on Muḍar*.” “*and more righteous*” (*wa-aqwamu qīlan*, “more upright in speech”): and sounder in saying and steadier in reciting, for the stillness of voices. From Anas ﵁: he read *wa-aṣwabu qīlan*. It was said to him, “*Abū Ḥamza, it is only wa-aqwamu*.” He said, “*Aqwam, aṣwab and ahyaʾ are one*.” Abū Zayd al-Anṣārī related from Abū Sirār al-Ghanawī that he used to read *fa-ḥāsū*, with an undotted *ḥāʾ*. It was said to him, “*It is only fa-jāsū* (Q 17:5), *with jīm*.” He said, “*Jāsū and ḥāsū are one*.”

::: passage 73:7-7 | 4:639-639

{L|A lot of time|sabḥan} moving about and turning over in your concerns and occupations; and you are free only at night, so keep to the intimate speech with God that requires an easy mind and the absence of occupations. As for the reading with *khāʾ*, it is a borrowing from *sabkh al-ṣūf*, which is carding wool and spreading its parts, for the spreading of care and the scattering of the heart by occupations. He charged him with standing at night, then mentioned the wisdom in what He charged him with of it: which is that the night is more helpful to agreement and firmer for reciting, because the man is still and the voice low; and that it gathers the heart more and draws together scattered care more than the day, because that is the time when cares scatter, thoughts are divided, and one turns about in the needs of living and of the return. It is also said: free time and room for your sleep and your going about your needs. It is also said: if something of the night escapes you, you have free time in the day in which you can make it up.

::: passage 73:8-10 | 4:639-640

{L|You shall commemorate the name of your Lord|wa-dhkuri sma rabbika} and keep at His remembrance in your night and your day, and be eager for it. The remembrance of God takes in everything of good remembrance: glorifying, saying there is no god but God, magnifying, extolling, declaring Him one, prayer, reciting the Qurʾān, studying knowledge, and other things with which the Messenger of God ﷺ used to fill the hours of his night and his day. “*To come ever closer and closer to Him*” (*wa-tabattal ilayhi*, “and devote yourself to Him”): and cut yourself off for Him.

{Q} How was it said *tabtīl* (“a devoting”) in place of *tabattul*? {A} Because the meaning of *tabattal* is “he devoted himself”; so it was brought according to its meaning, keeping the right of the verse endings. “*Lord of the east and the west*” is read in the nominative, as praise; and in the genitive, as a substitute for “*your Lord*.” From Ibn ʿAbbās: as an oath, with the particle {p|640}of the oath implied, as in your saying, “By God, I will surely do it”; and its answer is “*there is no other god besides Him*,” as you say, “By God, there is no one in the house but Zayd.” Ibn ʿAbbās read *rabbu l-mashāriqi wa-l-maghāribi*. “*You should choose Him as your advocate*” follows as a result on the declaration that there is no god but God; because it is He alone to whom, for His being alone in lordship, matters must be entrusted. It is also said: “*advocate*,” guarantor of what He promised you of help and victory. The fair shunning is that he keeps away from them in his heart and inclination, and goes against them with good dealing, courtesy, overlooking, and leaving off repaying. From Abū l-Dardāʾ ﵁: “*We grin in the faces of a people and laugh with them, while our hearts loathe them*.” It is also said: it is abrogated by the verse of the sword.

::: passage 73:11-14 | 4:640-641

When a man knows of his companion that he is anxious over an affair he wants to be spared, or an enemy he longs to have vengeance taken on for him, while he himself is equal to that and able for it, he says, “Leave me and him”; that is, you need nothing to attain what you want and long for except to leave me alone with him, by entrusting his matter to me and asking me to suffice you against him; for in me is what will ease your mind and clear your care. And there is no hindrance there, so that he should be asked to leave him and him, except leaving off asking him to suffice and entrusting to him; as though, when he does not entrust his matter to him, he has kept him from it, and when he entrusts it to him he has removed the hindrance and left him and him. In it is proof of confidence that he is able to fulfill the furthest that the wish of the one addressed turns around, and more than it.

*Al-naʿma*, with *fatḥa*, is living in ease; with *kasra*, bestowing favor; and with *ḍamma*, gladness: one says *nuʿm* and *nuʿmat ʿayn*. They are the great men of Quraysh, and they were people of ease and luxury. “*We have*” (*ladaynā*) what is the opposite of their ease: of fetters, which are the heavy shackles, from al-Shaʿbī: when they rise they lift with them; the singular is *nikl* and *nakl*; and of Hell, which is the fire hard in heat and blaze; and of food that chokes, which is what sticks in the throats and is not swallowed, meaning the *ḍarīʿ* and the tree of Zaqqūm; and of painful punishment, of the other kinds of punishment. So you see none to whom their matter is entrusted, left alone with them, who takes vengeance on them with the like of that vengeance. It is related that the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse and fell in a swoon. From {p|641}al-Ḥasan: he came to the evening fasting and was brought food, and this verse came to him, and he said, “*Take it away*.” It was set before him the second night, and it came to him, and he said, “*Take it away*”; and likewise the third night. Thābit al-Bunānī, Yazīd al-Ḍabbī and Yaḥyā al-Bakkāʾ were told, and they came and kept at him until he drank a draught of barley-meal gruel. “*The day will come when the earth and the mountains will quake*” is put in the accusative by what is in “*We have*.” *Al-rajfa* is the quake and the hard shaking. *Al-kathīb* is gathered sand, from *kathaba l-shayʾa*, when he gathered it; as though it were *faʿīl* in the sense of *mafʿūl* in its origin. From it is *al-kuthba* of milk; the ewe said, “*I am shorn of much wool and milked in quick small measures*.” That is: they were like gathered sand that was poured, *hīla haylan*, that is, scattered and made to flow.

::: passage 73:15-16 | 4:641-641

The address is to the people of Mecca. *Shāhidan ʿalaykum* (“a witness against you”): who bears witness against you on the Day of Resurrection to your unbelief and your denying.

{Q} Why did He make “*messenger*” indefinite and then definite? {A} Because He meant: We sent to Pharaoh one of the messengers. Then when He repeated it, as one known by mention, He brought in the *lām* of definiteness, pointing to the one mentioned himself. “*Severely*” (*wabīlan*, “ruinous”): heavy, harsh; from their saying, “*kalaʾun wabīlun*,” unwholesome pasture that is not easy to digest for its heaviness. *Al-wabīl* is the thick staff; and from it is *al-wābil* for heavy rain.

::: passage 73:17-18 | 4:641-642

{L|A day|yawman} is an object, that is: how will you guard yourselves on the Day of Resurrection and its terror, if you remain in unbelief and do not believe and do righteous work? It may also be an adverbial, that is: how will you have guarding on the Day of Resurrection if you disbelieved in this world? It may also be put in the accusative by “*you disbelieve*,” interpreted as “you deny,” that is: how will you fear God and dread Him if you deny the Day of Resurrection and the repayment? Because fearing God is fearing His punishment. “*It makes the infants gray-haired*” is a proverb for hardness. It is said of a hard day: a day that grays the forelocks of children. Its origin is that when cares and griefs pile up on a man, graying comes on him quickly. Abū l-Ṭayyib said:

> Care wastes the stout man to thinness, / grays the forelock of the child, and makes him old.

{p|642}I came across in one of the books that a man went to bed with his hair coal black like a raven’s throat, and rose in the morning with his head and beard white as the *thaghāma* plant. He said, “*I was shown the Resurrection, Paradise and the Fire in my sleep, and I saw people being led in chains to the Fire; and from the terror of that I have become as you see*.” It may also be that the day is described as long, and that children reach in it the time of old age and graying. “*The heaven will shatter therefrom*” is also a description of the day by hardness, and that the heaven, for all its greatness and firmness, splits in it; so what do you think of the other creatures? *Munfaṭirun* and *mutafaṭṭirun* are read; and the meaning is: possessed of splitting; or on interpreting the heaven as the roof; or on interpreting the heaven as a thing split. The *bāʾ* in “*therefrom*” (*bihi*) is like that in your saying, “I split the stick with the adze, and it split by it.” He means that it splits by the hardness of that day and its terror, as a thing splits by what splits it. It may also be meant: the heaven is weighed down by it with a weighing that leads to its splitting, for its greatness on it and its fear of its happening; as in His saying “*Heavy it is, in the heavens and the earth*” (Q 7:187). “*His promise*” is of annexing the verbal noun to the object, and the pronoun is for the day. It may also be annexed to the doer, which is God, Mighty and Exalted; and He was not mentioned, because He is known.

{L|This|inna hādhihi} these verses that speak the hard threat, “*is a reminder*,” an admonition. “*Whoever wills*” takes admonition from it, and takes a way to God by fear and dread. The meaning of taking a way to Him is drawing near and seeking access by obedience.

{p|643}{L|Two-thirds of the night|adnā min thuluthayi l-layli} fewer than them. “Nearer” was only borrowed for “less” because when the distance between two things is near, the spaces between them are few, and when it is far, that is many. *Wa-niṣfahu wa-thuluthahu* is read in the accusative, on: that you stand less than the two thirds, and stand the half and the third. And that fits what went before at the start of the sūra, of the choice between standing the half in full, standing less than it, which is the third, and standing more than it, which is less than the two thirds. *Wa-niṣfihi wa-thuluthihi* is read in the genitive, that is: you stand less than the two thirds, and less than the half and the third. And that fits the choice among the half, which is less than the two thirds; the third, which is less than the half; and the quarter, which is less than the third; and it is the last face. “*And so do some of those who believed with you*”: and a group of your companions stand that. “*GOD has designed the night and the day*” (*wa-llāhu yuqaddiru l-layla wa-l-nahāra*, “God measures the night and the day”): none is able to measure the night and the day and know the amounts of their hours but God alone. Putting His name, Mighty and Majestic, first as subject, with “*designed*” (*yuqaddiru*) built on it, is what points to the meaning of His being singled out for measuring; and the meaning is that you are not able for it. The pronoun in “*you cannot always do this*” (*lan tuḥṣūhu*, “you will not count it”) is for the verbal noun of *yuqaddiru*, that is: He knew that fixing the times is not possible for you, nor can their reckoning be had by evening out and making equal, unless you take the wider for caution; and that is hard on you and wearing for you.

{L|He has pardoned you|fa-tāba ʿalaykum} is an expression for the allowance to leave off the measured standing; as in His saying “*He has redeemed you, and has pardoned you. Henceforth, you may have intercourse with them*” (Q 2:187). And the meaning is that He lifted from you the consequence of leaving it, as He lifts the consequence from the one who repents. He expressed the prayer by recitation, because it is one of its pillars, as He expressed it by standing, bowing and prostration. He means: so pray what is easy for you and is not hard, of the night prayer. And this abrogates the first; then both were abrogated together by the five prayers. It is also said: it is reciting the Qurʾān itself. It is said: he recites a hundred verses; and whoever recites a hundred verses in a night, the Qurʾān will not argue against him. It is also said: whoever recites a hundred verses is written among the devout. It is also said: fifty verses. He has made clear the wisdom in the abrogation, which is the standing being hard for the sick, for those traveling the earth for trade, and for those striving in the way of God. It is said: God made equal those who strive and those who travel to earn the lawful. From ʿAbd Allāh ibn Masʿūd ﵁: “*Any man who brings something to one of the cities of the Muslims, patient and seeking reward, and sells it at the price of its day, is with God among the martyrs*.” From ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar: “*God has not created a death I might die, after being killed in the way of God, dearer to me than to die between the two sides of a saddle, traveling the earth seeking of God’s bounty*.” “*He knew*” {p|644}is a fresh start, on the estimate of a question about the reason for the abrogation.

{L|Observe the contact prayers (Salat)|wa-aqīmū l-ṣalāta} He means the obligatory one, and the binding alms. It is also said: the alms of the breaking of the fast, because there were no alms at Mecca; they were only made binding after that; and whoever explains it as the binding alms makes the end of the sūra Medinan. “*Lend GOD a loan of righteousness*”: He may mean the other charities; and He may mean paying the alms in the best way: bringing out the best of wealth and the most useful of it to the poor, attending to intention and seeking God’s face, and giving to the one who deserves it; and He may mean everything of good that is done that concerns the self and wealth. “*Better*” is the second of the two objects of “*find*,” and *huwa* is a separator. It is allowed, though it does not fall between two definites, because *afʿal min* resembles the definite in refusing the article. Abū l-Sammāl read *huwa khayrun wa-aʿẓamu ajran*, in the nominative, as subject and predicate.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat al-Muzzammil, God will keep hardship from him in this world and the Hereafter*.”
