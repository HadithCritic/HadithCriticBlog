{L|NuN, the pen|nūn wa-l-qalami} *nūn wa-l-qalami* is read with the *nūn* pronounced plainly and assimilated, and with the *nūn* quiescent and with *fatḥa* and *kasra*, as in *ṣād*. What is meant is this letter of the letters of the alphabet. As for their saying that it is the inkwell, I do not know whether it is a usage of the language or of the law. And if it is a name for the inkwell, it must be either generic or a proper name. If generic, where is the case ending and the *tanwīn*? If a proper name, where is the case ending? And whichever it is, it must have a place in the build of the speech.

{Q} It is a thing sworn by. {A} Then, if generic, you must put it in the genitive with *tanwīn*, and the oath would be by an indefinite, unknown inkwell, as though it were said, “By an inkwell and the pen”; and if a proper name, you must decline it and put it in the genitive, or not decline it and give it *fatḥa*, for its being a proper name and feminine. Likewise the explanation as the fish: either one fish of the fishes is meant, or it is made a proper name for the Bahamūt they make claims about. The explanation as a tablet of light or of gold, and as a river in Paradise, is like that. He swore by the pen to make it great, because of what is in its creation and its forming of proof of great wisdom, and because of what is in it of uses and benefits that description cannot take in. “*And what they (the people) write*”: and what is written of books. It is also said: what the recording angels write. *Mā* is relative or verbal. By the pen its people may also be meant, so the pronoun in “*they (the people) write*” is for them, as though it were said: by the people of the pen and what they write, or their writing; and by them is meant everyone who writes, or the recording angels.

{Q} With what is the *bāʾ* in “*a great blessing from your Lord*” (*bi-niʿmati rabbika*, “by the blessing of your Lord”) connected, and what is its place? {A} It is connected with “*crazy*” as denied, as it is connected with “*sane*” as affirmed in your saying, “You are, by the blessing of God, sane”; affirming and denying being equal in that, as they are equal in your saying, “Zayd struck ʿAmr,” and “Zayd did not strike ʿAmr”: you make the verb govern in one way, affirmed and denied. {p|585}Its place is the accusative as a circumstantial clause, as though He said: you are not mad, being blessed with that. The *bāʾ* did not keep “*crazy*” from governing what is before it, because it is extra, to stress the negation. The meaning is to rule out what the unbelievers of Mecca used to attribute to him out of enmity and envy, and that, because of God’s blessing him with soundness of mind and the nobility that being made fit for prophethood requires, he is in another station. “*And for you*,” for bearing that, swallowing the bitterness in it and being patient with it, “*is a reward*,” a recompense, “*well deserved*” (*ghayra mamnūn*, “unfailing”): not cut off, as in His saying “*an everlasting reward*” (Q 11:108). Or not held over you as a favor, because it is a reward you earn by your work and not a favor from the outset; and only favors are held over one, not wages for work.

{L|Condemned|al-maftūnu} the mad, because he has been afflicted (*futina*), that is, tried with madness; or because the Arabs claim it is from the jinn’s deranging, and they are the afflicters, the bold ones among them. The *bāʾ* is extra. Or *al-maftūn* is a verbal noun, like *al-maʿqūl* and *al-majlūd*, that is: in which of you is {p|586}madness? Or: in which of your two parties is the mad one, in the party of the believers or the party of the unbelievers? That is: in which of the two is there one who deserves this name? It is an allusion to Abū Jahl ibn Hishām, al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra and their like. It is like the saying of God Most High “*They will find out tomorrow who the flagrant liar is*” (Q 54:26).

{L|Your Lord is fully aware|inna rabbaka huwa aʿlamu} the mad in reality, who are those who strayed from His way; “*and He knows best*” the sane, who are the guided. Or it is a threat and a promise, and that He knows best the repayment of both parties. “*Do not obey the rejectors*”: a stirring up and kindling to make him firm in going against them. They had wanted him to worship God for a time and their gods for a time, and they would hold back their mischief from him. “*They wish that you compromise*”: if you would soften and flatter, “*so they too can compromise*.”

{Q} Why was “*so they too can compromise*” put in the nominative and not in the accusative with an implied *an*, when it is the answer to the wish? {A} It was turned to another way, which is that He made it the predicate of a dropped subject, that is: then they compromise, as in the saying of God Most High “*Anyone who believes in his Lord will never fear*” (Q 72:13); on the meaning: they wished you would compromise, and then they would compromise at that time. Or: they wished for your compromising, and so they compromise now, out of their hope for your compromising. Sībawayh said: Hārūn claimed that in one of the codices it is *waddū law tudhinu fa-yudhinū*.

{L|Swearer|ḥallāfin} one who swears much, in truth and falsehood. That is enough of a restraint for one used to swearing; like it is the saying of God Most High “*Do not subject GOD’s name to your casual swearing*” (Q 2:224). “*Lowly*” (*mahīn*): from contemptibleness, which is smallness and meanness; He means smallness in judgment and discernment. Or He meant the liar, because he is mean in people’s eyes. “*Slanderer*”: one who finds fault and taunts. From al-Ḥasan: he twists the corners of his mouth at the backs of people’s heads. “*A backbiter*” (*mashshāʾin bi-namīm*): an instigator who carries talk from people to people by way of informing and making trouble among them. *Al-namīm* and *al-namīma* are informing. One of the Arabs recited to me:

> {p|587}Flare up as tale-bearing flares up, / that Zahrāʾ carries to Tamīma.

“*Forbidder of charity*” (*mannāʿin li-l-khayr*): a miser; and good is wealth. Or he keeps his family from the good, which is Islam; so He mentioned what is kept from and not those kept from it, as though He said: one who keeps from good. It is said: he is al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra al-Makhzūmī. He was well off, and had ten sons, and used to say to them and to his kin, “*Whoever of you becomes Muslim, I will keep my support from him*”; from Ibn ʿAbbās. And from him: he is Abū Jahl. From Mujāhid: al-Aswad ibn ʿAbd Yaghūth. From al-Suddī: al-Akhnas ibn Sharīq; his origin was in Thaqīf, and he was counted among Zuhra; and for that reason he was called *zanīm*. “*A transgressor*”: going past the limit in wrongdoing; “*a sinner*”: of many sins; “*Unappreciative*” (*ʿutull*): harsh, rough; from *ʿatalahu*, when he led him with violence and roughness. “*And greedy*” (*baʿda dhālika zanīm*, “after that, an impostor”): after the faults and defects He counted against him; *Zanīm*: of false lineage. Ḥassān said:

> You are a hanger-on, fastened onto the family of Hāshim, / as the single cup is fastened behind the rider.

Al-Walīd was a false claimant in Quraysh, not of their stock; his father claimed him eighteen years after his birth. It is also said: his mother played the harlot, and it was not known until this verse was revealed. He made his harshness and his false lineage the worst of his faults, because when one is harsh and his nature rough his heart hardens and he dares every sin; and because, as a rule, when the seed is foul what grows from it is foul. For this reason the Messenger of God ﷺ said, “*The child of adultery will not enter Paradise, nor his child, nor his child’s child*.” “*After that*” is like “*then*” in His saying “*And being one of those who believe*” (Q 90:17). Al-Ḥasan read *ʿutullun*, {p|588}in the nominative, as blame; and this reading strengthens what “*after that*” points to. *Al-zanīm* is from *al-zanama*, the bit of a she-goat’s skin that is cut and left hanging at her throat; because he is an addition hanging onto people not his own.

{L|Even though he possessed enough money|an kāna dhā mālin} is connected with His saying “*Do not obey*.” He means: do not obey him with these faults because he has wealth, that is, for his ease and his share of this world. It may also be connected with what is after it, on the sense: because he is wealthy and backed by sons, he denies Our signs. “*He says*,” which is the answer to *idhā*, does not govern it, because what is after the condition does not govern what is before it; but the meaning of denial that the clause points to does. *A-an kāna?* is also read, as a question, on: is it only because he has wealth and sons that he denies? Or: do you obey him because he has wealth? Al-Zubayrī related from Nāfiʿ *in kāna*, with *kasra*, the condition being for the one addressed, that is: do not obey every swearer, making his ease a condition; because if he obeys the unbeliever for his wealth, it is as though he made wealth a condition for obedience. Like turning the condition to the one addressed is turning the hope to him in the saying of God Most High “*he may take heed*” (Q 20:44).

The face is the noblest place in the body, and the nose the noblest place in the face, because it is out in front of it. For that reason they made it the place of might and of pride, and derived *anafa* (disdain) from it. They said, “Disdain is in the nose,” “He guarded his nose,” and “So-and-so is high of nose.” And they said of the abased, “His nose was cut off,” and “His nose was rubbed in the dust.” So He expressed by branding on the snout the utmost humiliation and abasement, because a brand on the face is a disgrace and a degradation, so how much more on the noblest place in it! Al-ʿAbbās branded his camels on their faces, and the Messenger of God ﷺ said to him, “*Honor the faces*,” and he branded them on their haunches. In the word *khurṭūm*, “snout,” is contempt and disdain for him. It is also said: its meaning is, We will mark him on the Day of Resurrection with a disfiguring mark by which he stands out from the other unbelievers, as {p|589}he was hostile to the Messenger of God ﷺ with a hostility by which he stood out from them. It is also said: he was struck on the nose by the sword on the day of Badr, and the mark stayed on his snout. It is also said: We will make him notorious for this abuse in both abodes together, so it will not be hidden, as a brand on the snout is not hidden. From al-Naḍr ibn Shumayl: *al-khurṭūm* is wine, and its meaning is, We will give him the prescribed punishment for drinking it; and that is far-fetched. Wine was called *al-khurṭūm* as it was called *al-sulāfa*, which is what flows first of the pressed juice of grapes; or because it flies up into the nostrils.

We have tried the people of Mecca with drought and hunger, by the prayer of the Messenger of God ﷺ against them, “*like we tested the owners of the garden*.” They were a people of the people of prayer. Their father had this garden two parasangs from Ṣanʿāʾ. He {p|590}used to take from it his provision for the year and give the rest in alms. He used to leave for the poor what the sickle missed, what was at the bottom of the heaps, what the picking missed of the grapes, and what remained on the mat spread under the palm when it was cropped; and a great deal would gather for them. When he died his sons said, “*If we do what our father did, things will be tight for us, and we have families*.” So they swore to crop it in the morning, in the dark, hidden from the poor, and they made no exception in their oath; and God burned their garden. It is also said: they were of the Children of Israel. “*In the morning*”: entering on the morning, early. “*They were so absolutely sure*” (*wa-lā yastathnūn*, “and they made no exception”): they did not say “if God wills.”

{Q} Why was it called an exception, when it is only a condition? {A} Because it does the work of an exception, in that the meaning of your saying “I will surely go out, if God wills,” and “I will not go out unless God wills,” is one.

{L|A passing (storm) from your Lord passed by it|fa-ṭāfa ʿalayhā} a trial, or destruction, “*a visitation*,” as in the saying of God Most High “*his crops were wiped out*” (Q 18:42). *Ṭayfun* is also read. “*By morning, it was barren*” (*fa-aṣbaḥat ka-l-ṣarīm*): like a cropped one, for the destruction of its fruit. It is also said: *al-ṣarīm* is the night, that is, it was burned and turned black. It is also said: the day, that is, it dried and its green went; or nothing was left in it, from their saying “He whitened the vessel,” when he emptied it. It is also said: *al-ṣarīm* is the sands. “*Let us harvest the crop*” (*ṣārimīn*): harvesting.

{Q} Why was it not said “go early to your crop,” and what is the meaning of *ʿalā*? {A} Since going early to it was to crop it and cut it, it was a going early against it, as you say, “The enemy came against them early.” Going early may also be made to contain the meaning of coming toward, as in their saying, “The great bowl is brought to him in the morning and the evening,” that is: come toward your crop early. “*They confided to each other*”: telling secrets among themselves. *Khafiya*, *khafata* and *khafada*, all three are in the sense of hiding; from it is *al-khafdūd* for the bat. “*That from then on, none of them would be poor*” (*an lā yadkhulannahā*, “that none shall enter it”): *an* is explanatory. Ibn Masʿūd read it dropped, with a saying implied, that is: they whisper, saying, “None shall enter it.” Forbidding the poor man to enter is forbidding them to let him; that is, do not let him enter, so that he enters; as in your saying, “Let me not see you here.”

{L|They were so absolutely sure of their harvest|ʿalā ḥardin} *al-ḥard* is from *ḥaradati l-sanatu*, when the year withheld its good, and *ḥāradati l-ibilu*, when the camels withheld their milk. The meaning is: they went early able to do mischief only, not able to do good. He means that they resolved to do mischief to the poor and deprive them while they were able to benefit them; so they went in a state of poverty and loss of wealth in which they were able only to do mischief and deprive. That is because they sought to deprive the poor, and deprivation and poverty were hastened to them. Or: they went early, able, on the withholding of their garden and the loss of its good, instead of being able to get {p|591}its good and its benefits; that is, they went early arriving at deprivation in place of benefit. Or: when they said, “Go early to your crop,” their intention having grown foul, God punished them in that their garden withheld and they were deprived of its good; so they did not go early to a crop, but went early to a withholding. “*Able*” is of reversing speech for mockery, that is: able to do what they had resolved, of cropping and depriving the poor; and “*on withholding*” is not linked to “*able*.” It is also said: *al-ḥard* has the sense of *al-ḥarad*, anger; and *ʿalā ḥaradin* is also read, that is, they were able only to rage and be angry with one another, as in the saying of God Most High “*to blame each other*.” It is also said: *al-ḥard* is purpose and speed; one says *ḥaradtu ḥardaka*, “I made for where you made for.” He said:

> A flood came forth from God’s command, / making for the fruitful garden.

And *qaṭan ḥirād*: swift sandgrouse. He means: they went early making for their garden with speed and eagerness, able in their own eyes, saying, “We are able to crop it and keep its benefit from the poor.” It is also said: *Ḥard* is a proper name for the garden, that is, they went early to that garden, able in their own eyes to crop it; or reckoning that their aim of cropping and depriving would be fulfilled for them.

{L|They said|qālū} at their first arrival, “*We were so wrong!*” (*innā la-ḍāllūn*): that is, we have lost our way to our garden, and this is not it, because of the destruction they saw. When they reflected and knew that it was it, they said, “*Now, we have nothing!*” (*bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn*): we have been deprived of its good for our offense against ourselves. “*The righteous among them*” (*awsaṭuhum*): the most just and best of them, from their saying, “He is of the middle of his people,” and “Give me of the middle of your wealth”; and from it is the saying of God Most High “*an impartial community*” (Q 2:143). “*If only you had glorified (GOD)!*”: why do you not remember God and repent to Him of your foul intention? As though the most moderate of them said to them when they resolved on that: remember God and His vengeance on the criminals, repent of this foul resolve at once, and hurry to cut off its evil before the vengeance comes down. They disobeyed him, and he reproached them. The proof of it is their saying “*Glory be to our Lord. We have transgressed*”: they spoke what he had called them to speak, right after committing the sin; but after Baṣra was ruined. It is also said: by glorifying is meant the exception, because they meet in the meaning of magnifying God; for the exception is leaving the matter to Him and glorifying is declaring Him far above, and each of leaving to Him and declaring Him far above is magnifying. From al-Ḥasan: it is prayer, as though they were slack in prayer; otherwise it would have forbidden them indecency and wrong, and would have been a favor moving them to make the exception and not deprive. “*Glory be to our Lord*”: they glorified God and declared Him far above wrongdoing and every ugly thing, then confessed their wrongdoing in withholding kindness and leaving out the exception. “*They started to blame each other*”: some of them blamed others, because among them were some who made it fair, some who accepted, some who commanded holding back and gave warning, {p|592}some who disobeyed the command, and some who kept silent and were content. “*To give us in exchange*” is read heavy and light. “*We repent to our Lord*” (*ilā rabbinā rāghibūn*): asking good of Him, hoping for His pardon. “*Such was the requital*”: like that punishment with which We tried the people of Mecca and the owners of the garden is the punishment of this world; “*But the retribution of the Hereafter is far worse*,” more severe and greater than it. Qatāda was asked about the owners of the garden, whether they are of the people of Paradise or the people of the Fire, and he said, “*You have laid a weary task on me*.” From Mujāhid: they repented and were given a better one in exchange. It is related from Ibn Masʿūd ﵁: “*It has reached me that they became sincere, and God knew their truthfulness, and gave them in exchange a garden called al-Ḥayawān, in which are grapes, a bunch of which a mule carries*.”

The chiefs of Quraysh saw how full their share of this world was and how small the shares of the Muslims in it. When they heard talk of the Hereafter and what God promised the Muslims, they said: “*If it is true that we will be raised, as Muḥammad and those with him claim, their state and ours will be only as it is in this world; otherwise they will not be above us nor excel us, and the most they can hope is to be equal to us*.” So it was said: shall We be unjust in judgment and make the Muslims like the unbelievers? Then it was said to them by way of turning: “*What is the matter with you? How do you judge*” this crooked judgment, as though the matter of repayment were left to you so that you judge in it as you wish? “*Do you have another book*” from heaven, “*to uphold*” that what you choose and desire is yours? As in the saying of God Most High “*Do you have any proof? Show us your book*” (Q 37:156–157). The origin is “you study that (*anna*) you have what you choose,” with *fatḥa* on *anna*, because it is what is studied; and when the *lām* came, it took *kasra*. It may also be the relating of what is studied as it is, as in His saying “*we preserved his history for subsequent generations. Peace be upon Noah among the peoples*” (Q 37:78–79). *Takhayyara l-shayʾa* and *ikhtārahu*: he took the best of it; like it are *tanakhkhalahu* and *intakhalahu*, when he took what was sifted of it. “So-and-so has an oath on me for such-and-such”: when you have guaranteed it to him and sworn to him to fulfill it. He means: or have We guaranteed to you and sworn to you with heavy oaths, reaching the utmost in emphasis?

{p|593}{Q} With what is “*on the Day of Resurrection*” connected? {A} With what is implied in the adverbial, that is: they stand fixed for you on Us until the Day of Resurrection; We do not come out of Our obligation for them until that day, when We let you judge and give you what you judge. It may also be connected with “*reaching*,” on the view that they reach that day and end at it in full, no oath of them having become void, until what was sworn on, of letting them judge, comes about. Al-Ḥasan read *bālighatan*, in the accusative, as a circumstantial clause from the pronoun in the adverbial. “*That you shall have what you judge*” is the answer to the oath, because the meaning of “*or have you oaths on Us*” is: or have We sworn to you?

{L|Who|ayyuhum} for that judgment “*is guarantor*,” that is, stands up for it and for arguing its soundness, as the guarantor stands up, who speaks for the people and takes on their affairs. “*Do they have idols?*” (*am lahum shurakāʾ*): that is, people who share with them in this saying, agree with them on it and go their way in it? “*Let their idols help them*” them, “*if they are truthful*” in their claim. Baring the shank and showing the anklets is a proverb for the hardness of a matter and the difficulty of an affair. Its origin is in alarm and rout, and the women kept behind curtains rolling up their skirts from their shanks in flight and showing their anklets at that. Ḥātim said:

> The brother of war: if war bites him, he bites it; / and if war bares its shank, he rolls up his sleeves.

{p|594}And Ibn al-Ruqayyāt said:

> It makes the old man forget his sons, and bares / the anklets of the noble virgin.

So the meaning of “*The day will come when they will be exposed*” (*yawma yukshafu ʿan sāq*, “the day the shank is bared”) is: the day the matter grows hard and terrible; and there is no baring there and no shank. As you say of a miser with no hands, “His hand is fettered,” and there is no hand there and no fetter; it is only a proverb for avarice. As for the one who likens God to creatures, it is from the narrowness of his range and how little he has looked into the science of eloquence. What deceived him in it is the ḥadīth of Ibn Masʿūd ﵁: “*The Merciful will bare His shank; as for the believers, they will fall down prostrating; and as for the hypocrites, their backs will be one rigid plate, as though there were spits in them*.” Its meaning is: the matter of the Merciful will grow hard and its terror terrible, which is the greatest terror on the Day of Resurrection. Further, the shank ought to be made definite on what the likener holds, because it is a particular, known shank with him, which is the shank of the Merciful.

{Q} Then why did it come indefinite in the likeness? {A} To point to its being a matter vague in its hardness, strange, out of the ordinary, as in His saying “*the day will come when the caller will announce a terrible disaster*” (Q 54:6); as though it were said: the day a dreadful, fearful matter happens. This likening is related from Muqātil. From Abū ʿUbayda: two men came out of Khurāsān. One of them likened God to creatures until he gave Him a form, and that is Muqātil ibn Sulaymān; and the other denied until he emptied Him of attributes, {p|595}and that is Jahm ibn Ṣafwān. Whoever senses how great the harm of lacking this science is, knows how great its benefits are.

*Yawma nakshifu* is also read, with *nūn*; and *takshifu*, with *tāʾ*, built for the active and the passive both; the verb is for the Hour or for the state, that is: the day the state or the Hour grows hard, as you say, “War bared its shank,” by figure. *Tukshifu* is also read, with the *tāʾ* with *ḍamma* and *kasra* on the *shīn*, from *akshafa*, when he entered into baring; from it is *akshafa l-rajulu fa-huwa mukshif*, when his upper lip is turned back. What puts the adverbial in the accusative is “*let them bring*”; or an implied “remember”; or: on the day the shank is bared, such-and-such will be; and it was dropped for telling terror, and that there are such happenings there as cannot be described for their greatness. From Ibn Masʿūd ﵁: their backbones are made rigid, that is, turned into bones without joints that do not bend in rising and lowering. In the ḥadīth: “*and their backbones remain one plate*,” that is, one vertebra.

{Q} Why are they called to prostrate, when there is no obligation? {A} They are not called to it as worship and obligation, but as rebuke and reproach for their leaving prostration in this world; with their backbones made rigid and their being kept from the ability, to grieve them and make them regret what they neglected when they were called to prostrate while their backbones and joints were sound, they were able, and their excuses had been removed in what they were bound to worship with.

::: passage 68:44-45 | 4:595-596

One says, “Leave me and him,” meaning: leave him to me, and I will suffice you against him. As though He says: it is enough of striking at him that you leave his matter to Me and leave Me alone with him, for I will act on him as he should be acted on, and am able for him. What is meant is: I am enough as repayer for whoever denies the Qurʾān; so do not busy your heart with his matter, and rely on Me in taking vengeance on him. It is a consolation for the Messenger of God ﷺ and a threat to the deniers. *Istadrajahu ilā kadhā*: he brought him down to it step by step, until he entangled him in it. God’s drawing on the disobedient is that He provides them health and blessing, and they make God’s provision a means and a ladder to more unbelief and sin. “*Whence they never perceive*”: that is, from the side by which they do not perceive that it is drawing on, which is the blessing on them; because they think it is a preferring of them and a favoring over the believers, while it is the cause of their destruction. “*I will give them enough rope*”: I give them time, as in the saying of God Most High “*We only lead them on to confirm their sinfulness*” (Q 3:178). Health, provision and length of life are a kindness and a favor from God, which binds them to thanks and obedience; but they make it, by their own choice, a cause of unbelief. So when they went down step by step by it to destruction, the Giver of blessing was described by drawing on. It is said: how many are drawn on by being treated well! How many tempted by being praised! How many deceived by being covered! {L|In carrying out the commands of your Lord|li-ḥukmi rabbika} which is His giving them respite and putting off your victory over them. “*Do not be like (Jonah) who called from inside the fish*”: He means Jonah ﵇, “*who called*” in the belly of the fish, *wa-huwa makẓūm* (“while he was filled with distress”): full of anger, from *kaẓama l-siqāʾa*, when he filled the water-skin. The meaning is: let there not come from you what came from him, of impatience and anger, so that you are tried with his trial. Making the verb masculine is good because the pronoun in “*overtook him*” comes between. Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd read *tadārakathu*. Al-Ḥasan read *taddārakuhu*, that is, *tatadārakuhu*, relating a past state; meaning: had it not been that it was said of him, “it overtakes him”; as one says, “Zayd was going to stand, and So-and-so kept him from it,” that is, it was said of him, “he is going to stand,” and the meaning is that his standing was expected. His Lord’s blessing is that He blessed him with success in repenting and turned to him. He leaned, in the answer to *lawlā*, on the circumstantial clause, I mean His saying “*while he was blamed*.” He means that his state was the opposite of blame when he was cast on the bare shore, and had it not been for his repentance his state would have been one of blame. It is related that it was revealed at Uḥud, when there came on the Messenger of God ﷺ what came on him, and he wanted to pray against those who had been routed. It is also said: when he wanted to pray against Thaqīf. *Raḥmatun min rabbihi* is also read. “*But his Lord blessed him*” (*fa-jtabāhu rabbuhu*, “then his Lord chose him”): He drew him to Himself and brought him near by turning to him, as He said: “*Subsequently, his Lord chose him, redeemed him, and guided him*” (Q 20:122). “*And made him righteous*”: that is, of the prophets. From Ibn ʿAbbās: God gave him back revelation and let him intercede for himself and his people.

{p|597}*In* is the lightened form of the heavy one, and the *lām* is its mark. *Layuzliqūnaka* is read with *ḍamma* and *fatḥa* on the *yāʾ*. *Zalaqahu* and *azlaqahu* have one meaning; and one says *zalaqa l-raʾsa* and *azlaqahu* when he shaved it. *Layuzhiqūnaka* is also read, from *zahiqat nafsuhu* and *azhaqahā*. He means that from how hard they stare and look at you askance with eyes of enmity and hatred, they almost make your foot slip, or destroy you; from their saying, “He gave me a look that almost threw me down,” and “that almost ate me,” that is, had he been able to throw me down or eat me by his look, he would have done it. He said:

> When they meet somewhere they trade / looks that make feet slip from their footing.

It is also said: the evil eye was among the Banū Asad. A man of them would go hungry three days, and nothing would pass by him but he would say of it, “I have never seen the like of it as today,” and he would strike it with the eye. One of those who struck with the eye was wanted to say the like of that about the Messenger of God ﷺ, and he said, “*I have never seen a man as today*,” and God protected him. From al-Ḥasan: the remedy for being struck by the eye is to recite this verse. “*When they hear the message*”: that is, the Qurʾān, they could not master themselves for envy at the prophethood you were given. “*And say, ‘He is crazy!’*,” in bewilderment at his matter and to drive people from him; otherwise they knew he was the most sensible of them. The meaning is that they called him mad because of the Qurʾān. “*And it is nothing but a reminder*” and an admonition “*to the worlds*”; so how is one called mad who brought the like of it?
