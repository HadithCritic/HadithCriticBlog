title: Sūrat al-Masad (111)
revelation: Meccan. Five verses. [Revealed after al-Fātiḥa (1).]

::: passage 111:1-5 | 4:813-817

*Al-tabāb* is perishing. From it is their saying *a-shābba am tābba?* (“young, or perishing?”), that is, perishing from old age and infirmity. The meaning: his hands perished, because, as is related, he picked up a stone to throw at the Messenger of God ﷺ. “*And he is condemned*” (*wa-tabba*, “and he perished”): he perished entirely. Or his hands were made to perish, and what is meant is that the whole of him perished, as in the saying of God Most High “*This is what your hands have sent ahead for you*” (Q 22:10). The sense of “*and he is condemned*” is: and so it was, and it came about, as in the line: “*He repaid me (may God repay him with the worst repayment) with the repayment of howling dogs; and He has done it*.” {p|814}The reading of Ibn Masʿūd supports this: *wa-qad tabba* (“and he has perished”).

It is related that when “*You shall preach to the people who are closest to you*” (Q 26:214) was revealed, he climbed al-Ṣafā and called out, “*Alarm, alarm!*” (*yā ṣabāḥāh*), and people gathered to him from every side. He said, “*Sons of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, sons of Fihr, if I told you that there were horsemen at the foot of this mountain, would you believe me?*” They said, “*Yes*.” He said, “*Then I am a warner to you ahead of the Hour*.” Abū Lahab said, “*May you perish! Is this what you called us for?*” So it was revealed.

{Q} Why did He call him by his *kunya*, when calling someone by his *kunya* honors him? {A} There are three answers. The first is that he was well known by his *kunya* rather than his name, for a man may be known by either, which is why the *kunya* may be put after the name, or the name after the *kunya*, as an explanatory apposition. So when He meant to make him notorious by the curse and have it stay on him as a brand, He named him by the better known of his two names. The reading of those who read *yadā abū lahab* supports this, just as people said “ʿAlī ibn Abū Ṭālib” and “Muʿāwiya ibn Abū Sufyān,” so that nothing in it is changed and the hearer is not confused. Fulayta ibn Qāsim, the emir of Mecca, had two sons, one called ʿAbdi llāh, with the genitive, and the other ʿAbda llāh, with the accusative. There was a man in Mecca called ʿAbdi llāh, with the *dāl* in the genitive, who was known by no other form. The second is that his name was ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā, so He turned from it to his *kunya*. The third is that, since he was one of the people of the Fire and his end is a fire with flame (*lahab*), his state matched his *kunya*, and so he deserved to be called by it. One says “Abū Lahab” as one says Abū l-Sharr (“father of evil”) of an evil man and Abū l-Khayr (“father of good”) of a good one, and as the Messenger of God ﷺ gave Abū l-Muhallab the *kunya* Abū Ṣufra for a yellowness (*ṣufra*) in his face. It is also said that he was given that *kunya* because his cheeks were flushed and glowing, and so he may be called by it to mock him and his pride in it. *Abī lahb* is also read, with *sukūn*; this is one of the alterations of proper names, like their saying *Shums ibn Mālik*, with *ḍamma*.

{L|Will never help him|mā aghnā} an interrogative meant as a denial, in the accusative position; or a negation. “*And whatever he has accomplished*” (*wa-mā kasaba*, “and what he earned”) is nominative, and *mā* is either a relative pronoun or forms a verbal noun, meaning “what he earned” or “his earning.” The meaning: neither his wealth nor what he earned with his wealth did him any good, that is, the capital and the profits; or his livestock and what he gained from their young and their uses, for he had large and growing herds; or the wealth he inherited {p|815}from his father and what he earned himself; or his old wealth and his new. From Ibn ʿAbbās: what he earned is his children. It is told that the sons of Abū Lahab came to him to settle a dispute and began to fight. He got up to separate them, one of them pushed him and he fell, and he said in anger, “*Get this foul earning away from me!*” From this is his saying ﵇: “*The best that a man eats is from his own earning, and his child is part of his earning*.” From al-Ḍaḥḥāk: his wealth and his foul deeds, meaning his scheming in his enmity to the Messenger of God ﷺ, will not help him. From Qatāda: his deeds, by which he thought he had something to stand on, as in His saying “*We will look at all the works they have done*” (Q 25:23). It is related that he used to say, “*If what my nephew says is true, I will ransom myself from it with my wealth and my children*.”

{L|He has incurred|sa-yaṣlā} it is read with *fatḥa* on the *yāʾ*, and with *ḍamma*, both light and doubled. The *sīn* is for the threat, that is: it will certainly come about, however long it is put off.

{L|Also his wife|wa-mraʾatuhu} she is Umm Jamīl bint Ḥarb, the sister of Abū Sufyān. She used to carry a bundle of thorns, *ḥasak* and *saʿdān*, and scatter it by night on the path of the Messenger of God ﷺ. It is also said that she went about with slander. Of one who goes about with tales and makes trouble between people it is said that he carries firewood among them, that is, he kindles strife between them and leaves evil behind. He said: “*Of the spotless women; she was never caught on the back of a reproach, and never walked among the tribe with green firewood*.” He made it green to point to the smoke, which adds to the evil.

It is nominative, joined to the pronoun in “*He has incurred*” (*sa-yaṣlā*, “he will burn”), that is: he will burn, and his wife; and “*around her neck*” is in the position of a circumstantial clause. Or it is the subject of a nominal clause, with “*around her neck*” as its predicate. *Ḥammālata l-ḥaṭab* is also read, in the accusative, as a term of reviling, and I like this reading: one who loves to revile Umm Jamīl has sought the favor of the Messenger of God ﷺ with a fine deed. *Ḥammālatun li-l-ḥaṭab* and *ḥammālatan li-l-ḥaṭab* are also read, with *tanwīn*, in the nominative and the accusative; and *wa-muraytuhu* is read, as a diminutive.

{L|A rope of thorns|al-masad} rope twisted hard, whether of palm fiber, hide or anything else. He said: {p|816}“*and a masad twisted tight from she-camels*.” A man *mamsūd al-khalq* is one firmly knit. The meaning: around her neck is a rope of the hard-twisted kind, and she carries that bundle of thorns and ties it around her neck as firewood gatherers do. This is to debase her state and belittle her and paint her in the likeness of the serving women who gather firewood, so that she and her husband would smart at it, when the two of them were in a house of might and nobility and a place of wealth and plenty. Some people taunted al-Faḍl ibn al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿUtba ibn Abī Lahab with “*who led the persecution*” (*ḥammālata l-ḥaṭab*, “the carrier of firewood”), and he said: “*What did you mean by reviling me and running me down? Or what do you find to taunt in the carrier of firewood? A bright one, her blaze spread wide in glory, she was the daughter of an elder of noble line*.”

{p|817}The meaning may also be that her state in the fire of Hell will be the form she was in when she carried the bundle of thorns: she will never be without a bundle of the Fire’s firewood on her back, from the tree of Zaqqūm or from *ḍarīʿ*, and around her neck a rope of the twisted chains of the Fire, just as every wrongdoer is punished with what matches his state in his crime.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat tabbat, I hope that God will not bring him together with Abū Lahab in one abode*.”
