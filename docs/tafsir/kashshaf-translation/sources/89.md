::: passage 89:1-5 | 4:746-747

{L|By the dawn|wa-l-fajr} He swore by the dawn as He swore by the morning in His saying “*And the morning as it shines*” (Q 74:34) and “*And the morn as it breathes*” (Q 81:18). It is also said: by the dawn prayer. By the ten nights He meant the ten [nights] of Dhū l-Ḥijja.

{Q} Then why are they indefinite, among all the things He swore by? {A} Because they are particular nights among the kind “nights,” the ten being some of them; or particular in a merit that others do not have.

{Q} Then why were they not made definite with the article of what is known, since they are known and familiar nights? {A} Had He done so, they would not carry on their own the sense of merit that lies in the indefiniteness; and because it is better that the articles be of one kind, so that the speech is further from riddle and obscurity.

{L|By the even and the odd|wa-l-shafʿi wa-l-watr} either all things, their even and their odd; or the even and the odd of these nights. Their even may be the Day of Sacrifice and their odd the Day of ʿArafa, because that is the ninth of its days and this the tenth; and it is related from the Prophet ﷺ that he explained them so. They have said so much about the even and the odd that they have almost gone through every kind of thing in which they occur; that is of little use and better left alone. After swearing by the particular nights, He swore by the night in general.

{L|As it passes|idhā yasri} when it goes on, as in His saying “*And the night as it passes*” (Q 74:33) and “*By the night as it falls*” (Q 81:17). *Wa-l-watri* is also read, with *fatḥa* on the *wāw*; the two are dialect forms, like *ḥibr* and *ḥabr*, in the sense of number, while in the sense of blood-revenge only the *kasra* is used. *Wa-l-watiri* is also read, with *fatḥa* on the *wāw* and *kasra* on the *tāʾ*; Yūnus related it from Abū ʿAmr. *Wa-l-fajrin*, *wa-l-watrin* and *yasrin* are also read, with *tanwīn*, the *tanwīn* that comes in place of the letter of prolongation. From Ibn ʿAbbās: *wa-layālī ʿashrin*, with annexation, meaning “and the nights of ten days.” The *yāʾ* of *yasri* is dropped in connected speech, the *kasra* being enough in its place; in the pause it is dropped together with {p|747}the *kasra*. It is also said that the meaning of *yasrī* is “it is traveled in.”

{L|A profound oath|hal fī dhālika} that is, in these things I swore by, “*an oath*,” that is, something sworn by, “*for one who possesses intelligence*”: He means, is it right in his view that they be honored by being sworn by? Or: is there in My swearing by them [enough] for one who has understanding; that is, is it a great oath, by the like of which what is sworn to is confirmed? *Al-ḥijr* is the mind, because it restrains (*yaḥjuru*) from rushing headlong into what is not fitting, as it is called *ʿaql* and *nuhya* because it holds back (*yaʿqilu*) and forbids (*yanhā*); and *ḥaṣāt*, from *iḥṣāʾ*, which is keeping hold. Al-Farrāʾ said: one says “He is a man of *ḥijr*” when he masters himself and keeps himself in hand. What is sworn to is left out. It is “He will surely punish them,” and His saying “*Have you noted*” up to His saying “*Consequently, your Lord poured upon them a whipping retribution*” points to it.

::: passage 89:6-14 | 4:747-749

The descendants of ʿĀd ibn ʿAwṣ ibn Iram ibn Sām ibn Nūḥ were called ʿĀd, as the Banū Hāshim are called Hāshim. Then the first of them were called the first ʿĀd and Iram, naming them after their ancestor, and those after them the last ʿĀd. Ibn al-Ruqayyāt said: “*An ancient glory that his forebears built, that reached back to ʿĀd, and before it to Iram*.” So *Iram*, in His saying “*bi-ʿĀdin Iram*,” is an explanatory apposition to ʿĀd, and a sign that they are the first, ancient ʿĀd. It is also said that *Iram* is their town and the land they lived in; the reading of Ibn al-Zubayr, *bi-ʿĀdi Iram*, with annexation, points to this, the implied sense being “ʿĀd, the people of Iram,” as in His saying “*You may ask the community*” (*wa-sʾali l-qaryata*, “and ask the town,” Q 12:82). It is not fully inflected, whether it is a tribe or a land, because it is definite and feminine. Al-Ḥasan read *bi-ʿĀdin Aram*, with two *fatḥa*s. *Bi-ʿĀdin Irm* is also read, with *sukūn* on the *rāʾ*, to lighten it, as *bi-warqikum* is also read (Q 18:19). *Bi-ʿĀdin Irami dhāti l-ʿimād* is also read, with *Iram* annexed to *dhāt al-ʿimād*; *al-iram* is a landmark, meaning: ʿĀd, the people of landmarks with pillars; and *Dhāt al-ʿimād* is the name of the city. *Bi-ʿĀdin aramma dhāta l-ʿimād* is also read: that is, God made the One with Pillars rotten dust (*ramīm*), the verb being a substitute for “*your Lord did*.”

{L|The town with tall buildings|dhāti l-ʿimād} if it is a description of the tribe, the meaning is that they were desert people, people of tent poles; or tall in body, their statures being likened to pillars. From this is their saying *rajul muʿammad* and *ʿumdān*, when a man is tall. It is also said: of lofty building. If it is a description of {p|748}the town, the meaning is that it has columns.

It is related that ʿĀd had two sons, Shaddād and Shadīd, who ruled and conquered. Then Shadīd died and the rule was Shaddād’s alone. He ruled the world and its kings submitted to him. He heard mention of Paradise and said, “*I will build its like*.” So he built Iram in one of the deserts of Aden over three hundred years, and his life was nine hundred years. It is a great city whose palaces are of gold and silver and whose columns are of chrysolite and ruby, with every kind of tree and flowing rivers. When its building was finished he set out for it with the people of his kingdom, and when he was a day and a night’s journey from it, God sent upon them a cry from heaven, and they perished. From ʿAbd Allāh ibn Qilāba: he went out in search of a camel of his and came upon the city, and carried off what he could of what was there. News of him reached Muʿāwiya, who summoned him, and he told him his story. Muʿāwiya sent for Kaʿb and asked him, and he said: “*It is Iram of the Pillars. A man of the Muslims will enter it in your time, ruddy, fair and short, with a mole on his eyebrow and a mole on his heel, going out in search of a camel of his*.” Then he turned, saw Ibn Qilāba, and said, “*By God, this is that man*.”

{L|There was nothing like it|lam yukhlaq mithluhā} the like of ʿĀd “*anywhere*” (*fī l-bilād*, “in the lands”), for size of body and strength: a man of them was four hundred cubits tall, and he would come to a huge rock, carry it, throw it on a tribe and destroy them. Or: the like of Shaddād’s city was not created in all the lands of the world. Ibn al-Zubayr read *lam yakhluq mithlahā*, that is, God did not create its like.

{L|Who carved the rocks|jābū l-ṣakhra} they cut the rock of the mountains and made houses in it, as in His saying “*You carve out of the mountains luxurious mansions*” (Q 26:149). It is said that the first to hew the mountains, rocks and marble were Thamūd, and that they built one thousand seven hundred cities, all of stone.

{L|Who possessed might|dhī l-awtād} he was called *dhī l-awtād* (“of the stakes”) for his many troops and the tents they pitched when they halted; or for his torturing with stakes, as he did to his daughter’s hairdresser and to Āsiya.

{L|They all transgressed|alladhīna ṭaghaw} the best way with it is that it stand in the accusative as a term of censure. It may also be nominative, as in “they are the ones who transgressed”; or genitive, as a description of those mentioned, ʿĀd, Thamūd and Pharaoh.

{L|A whipping retribution|sawṭa ʿadhāb} one says “he poured the whip on him,” and “covered him with it,” and “cloaked him with it.” Mentioning the whip points out that the great punishment He brought on them in this world, compared with what He has prepared for them in the Hereafter, is like the whip compared with everything else one is punished with. From ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd: when al-Ḥasan came to this verse he would say, “*God has many whips, and He took them with one of them*.”

{L|Ever watchful|al-mirṣād} the place where the watcher waits; *mifʿāl* from *raṣadahu*, as *mīqāt* is from *waqqatahu*. This is a likeness of His lying in wait for the disobedient with punishment, and of their not escaping Him. From one of the Arabs: he was asked, “Where is your Lord?” and said, “At the lookout.” From ʿAmr ibn ʿUbayd ﵀: he recited this sūra in the presence of one of the oppressors until he reached this verse, and said, “*Your Lord is at the lookout, So-and-so*,” hinting by this address that he was one of the tyrants threatened with it. How excellent he was! What a lion, {p|749}ever mauling, was between his two garments, crushing the oppressors with his rebuke and humbling the people of whims and innovations with his arguments!

{Q} With what does His saying “*When the human being*” (*fa-ammā l-insānu*, “as for man”) connect? {A} With His saying “*Your Lord is at the lookout*,” as if it were said: God wants nothing from man but obedience and striving for the outcome, and He is lying in wait with punishment for the disobedient; but as for man, he does not want that, and cares only for the present life and what gives him pleasure and comfort in it.

{Q} How do His saying “*When the human being is tested by his Lord*” (*fa-ammā l-insānu idhā mā btalāhu rabbuhu*) and His saying “*But if He tests him*” balance, when balance requires that what follows the two *ammā*s match? You say: “As for man, he is ungrateful; as for the angel, he is grateful”; and “As for when you do good to Zayd, he does good to you; as for when you do him harm, he does you harm.” {A} They balance in that the implied sense is “and as for him, when his Lord tries him.” That is because His saying “*he says, ‘My Lord is generous towards me’*” is the predicate of the subject “*the human being*,” the *fāʾ* entering because of the sense of condition in *ammā*, and the adverb standing between subject and predicate is understood as coming later; as if it were said: “As for man, he says ‘My Lord has honored me’ at the time of trial.” So the second “*he says*” must be the predicate of a subject that has to be supplied.

{Q} How is each of the two, the widening of provision and its stinting, called a trial? {A} Because each of them is a testing of the servant. When He widens it for him, He tests his state: will he give thanks or be ungrateful? When He stints it, He tests his state: will he be patient or lose heart? The wisdom in both is one. Like it is the saying of God Most High “*after we put you to the test through adversity and prosperity*” (Q 21:35).

{Q} Why did He not say “and He humiliated him and stinted his provision,” as He said “*through blessings and joy*” (*fa-akramahu wa-naʿʿamahu*, “and He honored him and favored him”)? {A} Because the widening is God’s honoring of His servant by favoring him out of grace, with no prior claim; but the stinting is not a humiliation of him, because holding back a grace is not humiliation, only a leaving off of honor. A master may be honoring his servant, or humiliating him, or neither honoring nor humiliating him. If Zayd gives you a gift you say, “He honored me with a gift,” but you do not say “He humiliated me” {p|750}or “He did not honor me” when he gives you none.

{Q} He said *fa-akramahu*, so He affirmed and established his being honored; then He disowned his saying “*My Lord is generous towards me*” and blamed him for it, as He disowned his saying “*humiliating me*” and blamed him for it. {A} There are two answers. One: He disowned his saying “*My Lord is generous towards me*” and blamed him for it only because he said it meaning something other than what God had affirmed and established for him, namely that God gave him what He gave as an honor he was owed and had earned, after their way of boasting and of their high standing in their own eyes, as in His saying “*I attained all this because of my own cleverness*” (Q 28:78). But God gave it to him as a grace, with no earning of it on his part and no prior claim of the only kind God counts, which is piety, not the lineages and pedigrees they boasted of and thought made them deserve honor. The second: the disowning and blame go to his saying “*My Lord is humiliating me!*”; that is, when He favors him with good and honors him with it, he acknowledges God’s grace and honoring, and when He does not favor him, he calls the absence of favor humiliation when it is not humiliation. The mention of honoring in His saying *fa-akramahu* supports this view. *Fa-qadara* is also read, without doubling and with it; and *akraman* and *ahānan*, with *sukūn* on the *nūn* in the pause, by those who leave out the *yāʾ* in connected speech, the *kasra* being enough in its place.

{L|Wrong!|kallā} a rebuke to man for his saying. Then He said: rather, there is worse than the saying, namely that God honors them with much wealth, and they do not do what it binds them to, of honoring the orphan with care and kindness, and urging its people {p|751}to feed the poor; and they devour it as cattle eat, and love it and so are stingy with it. *Yukrimūna* and what follows it are also read, with *yāʾ*, as well as with *tāʾ*. *Taḥāḍḍūna* is also read, that is, you urge one another; and in the reading of Ibn Masʿūd *wa-lā tuḥāḍḍūna*, with *ḍamma* on the *tāʾ*, from *muḥāḍḍa*.

{L|Consuming the inheritance|aklan lammā} with *lamm*, which is lumping together the lawful and the unlawful. Al-Ḥuṭayʾa said: “*If eating that lumps all together brings blame on its owner, then may the Merciful not bless those molars*.” He means that they lump together in their eating their own share of the inheritance and the share of others. It is said that they did not let women and children inherit, and ate their inheritance along with their own. It is also said: they eat what the dead man gathered by wrongdoing, knowing it, and so lump together its lawful and unlawful in their eating. It may also be blame of the heir who got the wealth easily and without effort, with no sweat on his brow for it, and so is wasteful in spending it and eats it freely, lumping together every kind of food, drink and fruit he desires, as idle heirs do.

{L|Loving the money too much|ḥubban jammā} much and intense, with greed and craving and the holding back of what is due.

::: passage 89:21-26 | 4:751-752

{L|Indeed|kallā} a rebuke to them for that and a denial of what they did. Then He brought the threat, and mentioned their regret for what they neglected, at a time when regret will not help. *Yawmaʾidhin* is a substitute for “*when the earth is crushed*,” and what puts both in the accusative is “*will remember*.” “*crushed, utterly crushed*”: pounding after pounding, as in the saying “I went over his account chapter by chapter”; that is, the pounding is repeated on it until it becomes scattered dust.

{Q} What is the meaning of ascribing coming to God, when movement and passing from place to place are possible only for one who is in a direction? {A} It is an image of the appearing of the signs of His power and the showing forth of the traces of His overpowering and His authority. His state in that is pictured as the state of a king who, when he comes in person, there appears at his coming such awe and command as do not appear at the coming of all his armies, his ministers and his courtiers to the last man.

{L|Row after row|ṣaffan ṣaffā} the angels of every heaven come down and draw up row after row, surrounding the jinn and humans.

{L|On that day, Gehenna will be brought forth|wa-jīʾa yawmaʾidhin bi-jahannam} as in His saying “*Hell will be brought into existence*” (Q 79:36). It is related that when it {p|752}was revealed, the face of the Messenger of God ﷺ changed, and it showed in his face until it weighed heavily on his Companions. They told ʿAlī ﵁, and he came, embraced him from behind and kissed him between his shoulders, then said: “*Prophet of God, my father and mother be your ransom, what has happened today, and what has changed you?*” He recited the verse to him. ʿAlī said, “*How will it be brought?*” He said: “*Seventy thousand angels will bring it, leading it by seventy thousand reins, and it will bolt once; had it been let go, it would have burned up the people gathered*.”

{L|The human being will remember|yatadhakkaru l-insān} that is, he will remember what he neglected, or he will take heed. “*But what a remembrance*” (*wa-annā lahu l-dhikrā*, “but whence for him the remembrance?”): from where will he have the benefit of the remembrance? A left-out annexed word must be supplied; otherwise there would be a contradiction between “*On that day, the human being will remember*” and “*but what a remembrance*”

{L|I prepared for my (eternal) life|qaddamtu li-ḥayātī} this life, which is the life of the Hereafter; or the time of my life in this world, as you say “I came to him when ten nights of Rajab had passed.” This is the clearest proof that the choice was in their hands and depended on their purpose and will, and that they were not barred from acts of obedience and compelled to acts of disobedience, as is the teaching of the people of whims and innovations; otherwise, what would the regret mean?

{L|No retribution could be worse than His retribution|lā yuʿadhdhibu ʿadhābahu aḥad} *yuʿadhdhabu* and *yūthaqu* are read, with *fatḥa*; this is the reading of the Messenger of God ﷺ, and it is related from Abū ʿAmr that he came back to it at the end of his life. The pronoun is for the man described; it is said that he is Ubayy ibn Khalaf. That is: no one will be punished as he is punished, or bound in chains and fetters as he is bound, for how far he went in unbelief and obstinacy; or no one will bear the punishment of man, as in His saying “*none bears the burden of another*” (Q 6:164). It is also read with *kasra*, and the pronoun is for God Most High: that is, no one will carry out God’s punishment, because the command on that day is God’s alone. Or it is for man: that is, none of the keepers of Hell will punish anyone as they punish him.

{L|O content soul|yā ayyatuhā l-nafsu} with “saying” understood; that is, God says to the believer, “*O content soul*,” either speaking to him Himself to honor him, as He spoke to Moses ﵊, or by the tongue of an angel. *Al-muṭmaʾinna* is the secure soul, which no fear or grief unsettles, which is the believing soul; or the soul at rest in the truth, in which the coolness of certainty has settled so that no doubt stirs in it. The reading of Ubayy ibn Kaʿb, *yā ayyatuhā l-nafsu l-āminatu l-muṭmaʾinna*, bears witness to the first explanation.

{Q} When will this be said to it? {A} Either at death, or at the raising, or on entering Paradise; in the sense: return to the promise of your Lord, “*pleased*” with what you were given, “*pleasing*” to God. “*Welcome into My servants*”: in the company of My righteous servants, and take your place in their line; “*Welcome into My Paradise*” with them. It is also said that the soul is the spirit, {p|753}and its meaning is: enter into the bodies of My servants. Ibn ʿAbbās read *fa-dkhulī fī ʿabdī* (“into My servant”); Ibn Masʿūd read *fī jasadi ʿabdī* (“into the body of My servant”); Ubayy read *iʾtī rabbaki rāḍiyatan marḍiyyatan, udkhulī fī ʿabdī*. It is said that it was revealed about Ḥamza ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib; it is also said, about Khubayb ibn ʿAdī, whom the people of Mecca crucified with his face toward Medina. He said, “*O God, if I have any good with You, turn my face toward Your qibla*,” and God turned his face toward it, and no one could turn it back. The plain sense is general.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat al-Fajr in the ten nights is forgiven, and whoever recites it on the other days, it will be a light for him on the Day of Resurrection*.”
