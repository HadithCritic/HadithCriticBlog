{L|Is it not a fact|hal atā} *hal* is in the sense of *qad*, in questions only; its origin is *a-hal*, by proof of his saying, “*Have they* (a-hal) *seen us at the foot of the hillocky plain?*” So the meaning is: has there indeed come? By way of confirming and bringing near together; that is, there came over man, a short time before, “*there was a time when the human being was*” in it “*nothing to be mentioned*”: that is, he was a thing forgotten, not mentioned, a drop of sperm in the loins. What is meant by man is the race of the sons of Adam, by proof of His saying “*We created the human from a liquid mixture*.” “*a time*” (*ḥīnun mina l-dahr*, “a span of time”): a stretch of long, drawn-out time.

{Q} What is the place of “*the human being was nothing to be mentioned*”? {A} Its place is the accusative, as a circumstantial clause from “*man*,” as though it were said: has there come over him a span of time, unmentioned? Or the nominative, as a description of *ḥīn* (“span”), as in His saying “*a day when a father cannot help his own child*” (Q 31:33). From one of them: it was recited in his presence, and he said, “*Would that it had been completed*”; he meant: would that that state had been completed, which is his being a thing unmentioned, not created and not charged.

{L|A liquid mixture|nuṭfatin amshājin} like *burmatu aʿshārin*, a pot in pieces, and *burdu akyāshin*; and they are singular words, not plurals; for that reason they fall as descriptions of singulars. One also says *nuṭfatun mashijun*. Al-Shammākh said:

> She folded the insides of a closed womb at its time / over a mingled drop whose issue is despised.

*Amshāj* cannot be a broken plural of it; rather they are two alike in being singular, because a singular is described by both. *Mashajahu* and *mazajahu* have one meaning. And the meaning is: from a drop in which the two waters have mingled. From Ibn Masʿūd: they are the veins of the drop. From Qatāda: *amshāj* are colors and stages; he means that it is a drop, then a clot, then a lump of flesh. “*Testing him*” is in the place of a circumstantial clause, that is: We created him, testing him, in the sense of meaning to test him; as in your saying, “I passed a man with a hawk, hunting with it tomorrow,” by which you mean: intending to hunt with it tomorrow. It may also be meant: moving him from state to state; and that was called testing by way of borrowing. From Ibn ʿAbbās: We turn him about in his mother’s belly, a drop, then a clot. It is also said: it is on the estimate of being put after, meaning: “and We made him hearing, seeing,” that We might test him; and that is forced.

“*Grateful*” and “*ungrateful*” are two circumstantial clauses from the *hāʾ* in “*We guided him*,” that is: We gave him power and ability in both his states together. Or: We called him to Islam by the proofs of reason and hearing, whether it was known of him that he would believe or disbelieve, to make the argument binding. They may also be two circumstantial clauses from “*the way*,” that is: We made known to him the way, either a grateful way or an ungrateful way; as in His saying “*Did we not show him the two paths?*” (Q 90:10). Describing the way by gratitude and ingratitude is a figure. Abū l-Sammāl read with *fatḥa* on the *hamza* {p|667}in *immā*, and it is a good reading. And the meaning is: as for grateful, then by Our granting success; and as for ungrateful, then by his bad choice.

{L|The virtuous|al-abrār} is the plural of *barr* or *bārr*, like *rabb* and *arbāb*, and *shāhid* and *ashhād*. From al-Ḥasan: they are those who do not hurt the ants. *Al-kaʾs* is the glass when there is wine in it; and the wine itself is called *kaʾs*. “*Spiced with*” (*mizājuhā*, “its mixture”): what it is mixed with. “*Nectar*” (*kāfūran*, “camphor”): water of camphor; and it is the name of a spring in Paradise whose water is in the whiteness of camphor, its scent {p|668}and its coolness. “*A spring*” is a substitute for it. From Qatāda: it is mixed for them with camphor and sealed for them with musk. It is also said: the scent of camphor, its whiteness and its coolness are created in it, so it is as though it were mixed with camphor. On these two sayings “*a spring*” is a substitute for the place of “*from cups*,” on the estimate of a dropped annexed word, as though it were said: they drink in it a wine, the wine of a spring. Or it is accusative of specification.

{Q} Why was the verb of drinking joined with the particle of beginning (*min*) first, and with the particle of attaching (*bi-*) last? {A} Because the cup is the start of their drinking and the first of its limit; as for the spring, with it they mix their drink; so the meaning is: the servants of God drink the wine with it, as you say, “I drank the water with honey.” “*it will gush out as they will*” (*yufajjirūnahā*, “they make it gush forth”): they make it run where they wish from their dwellings, *tafjīran* (“a gushing”) easy, not refusing them.

{L|They fulfill|yūfūna} is an answer to one who may say: what is with them, that they are given that? Fulfilling the vow is emphasis in describing them as devoted to carrying out what is required; because one who fulfills what he made binding on himself for God’s face is more fulfilling of what God made binding on him. “*Extremely difficult*” (*mustaṭīran*, “spreading far”): widespread, scattered, reaching the furthest reach; from *istaṭāra l-ḥarīqu*, the fire spread, and *istaṭāra l-fajru*, the dawn spread; and it is from *ṭāra* as *istanfara* is from *nafara*. “*Their favorite food*” (*ʿalā ḥubbihi*, “for love of it”): the pronoun is for the food, that is, with their craving it and needing it. Like it is “*they give the money, cheerfully*” (*wa-ātā l-māla ʿalā ḥubbihi*, Q 2:177), “*You cannot attain righteousness until you give to charity from the possessions you love*” (Q 3:92). From al-Fuḍayl ibn ʿIyāḍ: for love of God. “*And the captive*”: from al-Ḥasan: a captive would be brought to the Messenger of God ﵌ and he would give him to one of the Muslims and say, “*Treat him well*”; and he would be with him two or three days, and he would prefer him to himself. And with most of the scholars: it is allowed to do good to unbelievers in the abode of Islam, but the required dues are not paid to them. From Qatāda: their captive at that time was the idolater; and your Muslim brother has more right to be fed by you. From Saʿīd ibn Jubayr and ʿAṭāʾ: it is the captive from the people of the qibla. From Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī: it is the slave and the prisoner. The Messenger of God ﵌ called the debtor a captive, and said: “*Your debtor is your captive, so treat your captive well*.”

{L|We feed you|innamā nuṭʿimukum} on the intent of saying. It may also be a saying with the tongue, to keep them from repaying it with its like or with thanks, because their doing good is done for God’s face, so there is no sense in being recompensed by creatures. And it may be that their saying it to them is a kindness, a teaching and an alerting to what one who is sincere to God ought to be in. From ʿĀʾisha ﵂: she used to send charity to a household, then ask the messenger what they had said; and when he mentioned a prayer, she prayed for them the like of it, so that the reward of the charity would remain hers, pure, with God. It may also be that that is a setting out and disclosing of their belief and the soundness of their intention, even if they said nothing. From Mujāhid: {p|669}“*As for them, they did not speak it; but God knew it of them, and praised them*.” *Al-shukūr* and *al-kufūr* are verbal nouns like *al-shukr* and *al-kufr*. “*We fear*”: it may mean that our doing good to you is from fear of the hardness of that day, not from wanting your recompense; and that we do not want recompense from you, for fear of God Most High’s punishment for seeking recompense for charity.

Describing the day as frowning is a figure in two ways. One is that it is described by the description of its people, of the wretched; as in their saying, “Your day is fasting.” It is related that the unbeliever frowns that day until there flows from between his eyes a sweat like pitch. The other is that it is likened in its hardness and harm to the frowning lion or the fierce brave man. *Al-qamṭarīr* is the one who frowns hard, who gathers what is between his eyes. Al-Zajjāj said: one says *iqmaṭarrati l-nāqatu*, when the she-camel raised her tail, drew in her sides and lifted her nose; so he derived it from *al-quṭr*, the side, and made the *mīm* extra. Asad ibn Nāʿiṣa said:

> And I have warmed myself at wars on every day / fierce in evil, grim of morning.

{p|670}{L|And rewards them with joy and contentment|wa-laqqāhum naḍratan wa-surūran} that is: He gave them, in place of the frowning of the wicked and their grief, radiance in the faces and joy in the hearts. And this points to the day being described by the frowning of its people. “*For their steadfastness*”: for their patience in preferring others. From Ibn ʿAbbās ﵁: al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn fell sick, and the Messenger of God ﷺ visited them among people who were with him. They said, “*Abū l-Ḥasan, what if you made a vow for your children?*” So ʿAlī, Fāṭima and Fiḍḍa, a slave woman of theirs, vowed that if the two were cured of what was on them they would fast three days. The two were cured, and they had nothing. ʿAlī borrowed three ṣāʿs of barley from Shamʿūn of Khaybar, the Jew. Fāṭima ground a ṣāʿ and baked five loaves, for their number, and they set them before them to break the fast. A beggar stood at them and said, “*Peace be on you, household of Muḥammad. A poor man of the poor of the Muslims; feed me, and God will feed you from the tables of Paradise*.” They preferred him, and spent the night tasting nothing but water, and rose fasting. When evening came and they set the food before them, an orphan stood at them, and they preferred him; and a captive stood at them on the third, and they did the like of that. When morning came, ʿAlī ﵁ took al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn by the hand, and they came to the Messenger of God ﷺ. When he saw them trembling like chicks from the hardness of hunger, he said, “*How hard it is on me, what I see in you!*” And he rose and went with them, and saw Fāṭima in her prayer niche, her back stuck to her belly and her eyes sunken, and that grieved him. Then Gabriel came down and said, “*Take it, Muḥammad: God congratulates you on your household*”; and he recited the sūra to him.

{Q} What is the meaning of mentioning silk with the garden? {A} The meaning is: and He repaid them, for their patience in preferring others and what it led to of hunger and nakedness, with an orchard in which is wholesome eating, and silk in which is splendid dress. He means that its air is even: no heat of a sun that burns, and no hardness of cold that hurts. In the ḥadīth: “*The air of Paradise is mild* (sajsaj), *neither hot nor cold*.” It is also said: *al-zamharīr* is the moon. From Thaʿlab: it is in the dialect of Ṭayyiʾ; and he recited:

> And many a night whose darkness had grown thick / I crossed, while the moon was not shining.

{p|671}And the meaning is that Paradise is light, so neither sun nor moon is needed in it.

{Q} To what is “*The shade covers them therein*” (*wa-dāniyatan ʿalayhim ẓilāluhā*, “and near over them are its shades”) joined? {A} To the sentence before it, because that is in the place of a circumstantial clause from those repaid; and this is a circumstantial clause like it, from them, because the pronoun in it goes back to them in *ʿalayhim*. Only, this is a single noun, and that is a sentence in the rule of a single word, its estimate being: not seeing in it sun nor cold, and near over them its shades. The *wāw* came in to point to the two matters being joined for them, as though it were said: and He repaid them with a garden, joining in it being far from heat and cold and the nearness of the shades over them. *Wa-dāniyatun* is read in the nominative, on the view that *ẓilāluhā* is the subject and *dāniyatan* the predicate, and the sentence is in the place of a circumstantial clause. And the meaning is: they do not see in it sun nor cold, while its shades are near over them. You may also make *muttakiʾīna* (“reclining”), *lā yarawna* (“they do not see”) and *dāniyatan* all descriptions of “*Paradise*.” And *wa-dāniyatan* may be joined to “*Paradise*,” that is: and another garden whose shades are near over them, on the view that they were promised two gardens, as in His saying “*For those who reverence the majesty of their Lord, two gardens*” (Q 55:46); because they were described by fear: “*We fear from our Lord*.”

{Q} Then to what is “*and the fruits are brought within reach*” (*wa-dhullilat*, “and are made low”) joined? {A} When you put *dāniyatan* in the nominative, it is a verbal sentence joined to a nominal sentence. When you put it in the accusative as a circumstantial clause, it is a circumstantial clause from “*near*,” that is: its shades are near over them in the state of its clusters being made low for them; or it is joined to it, on: and near over them its shades, and made low its clusters. When you put *dāniyatan* in the accusative as a description, it is a description like it; do you not see that if you said, “a garden whose clusters are made low,” it would be sound? Making the clusters low is that they are made tractable, not refusing those who pick them however they wish; or made low for them, submissive and drawn in, from their saying, “a low wall,” when it was short.

{L|Translucent|qawārīrā qawārīrā} both are read without *tanwīn*, with *tanwīn* on the first, and with *tanwīn* on both. This *tanwīn* is a substitute for the *alif* of release, because it is a verse ending; and in the second for its following the first. The meaning of “*Translucent cups, though made of silver*” (*qawārīra min fiḍḍa*, “glass of silver”) is that it is created of silver, and it is, with the whiteness of silver and its beauty, in the clearness of glass and its translucence.

{Q} What is the meaning of *kānat* (“was”)? {A} It is from the *kun* (“be”) in His saying “*Be, and it is*”: that is, it came into being as glass by God’s bringing into being; to make great that creation of wondrous matter, joining the qualities of the two different substances. And from it is the *kānat* (“was”) in His saying “*spiced with nectar*” (*kāna mizājuhā kāfūran*). *Qawārīru min fiḍḍatin* is read in the nominative, on: they are glass. “*They rightly deserved all this*” (*qaddarūhā taqdīran*, “they have measured them”) is a description of “*Translucent cups, though made of silver*.” The meaning of their measuring them is that they measured them in themselves, that they be of sizes and shapes according to their desires, and they came as they measured. It is also said: the pronoun is for those who go round with them, whom His saying “*They are served*” points to; on the view that they measured their drink to the measure of quenching, and that is most delightful for the drinker, because it is to the measure of his need, not more than it nor short of it. From Mujāhid: they do not overflow nor fall short. *Quddirūhā* is also read, built for the passive. Its face is that it is from *qudira*, carried over from *qadara*. You say, “I measured the thing, and So-and-so *qaddaranīhi*,” when he made you able to measure it; and its meaning is: they were made able to measure them as they wished, and they were left free to measure according to what they desired.

The spring was called ginger for the taste of ginger in it; and the Arabs find it delightful and pleasant. {p|672}Al-Aʿshā said:

> As though clove and ginger / had spent the night in her mouth, and gathered honey.

And al-Musayyab ibn ʿAlas said:

> As though the taste of ginger were in it / when you tasted it, and the first pressing of wine.

{L|Salsabeel|salsabīlan} for the smoothness of its running down in the throat and the ease of swallowing it. He means that it is in the taste of ginger, and has not its sting, but the opposite of the sting, which is smoothness. One says, “a *salsal* drink,” *salsāl* and *salsabīl*; the *bāʾ* was added in the build until the word became of five letters, and pointed to the utmost smoothness. Al-Zajjāj said: *al-salsabīl* in the language is a description of what is of the utmost smoothness. *Salsabīla* is read as indeclinable, for the coming together of being a proper name and feminine. They have attributed to ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib ﵁ that its meaning is “ask a way (*sal sabīlan*) to it.” And this is not right on its face; unless it is meant that the whole of one’s saying, “ask a way,” was made a proper name for the spring, as was said, *Taʾabbaṭa Sharran* and *Dharrā Ḥabban*; and it was called that because none drinks from it but one who asked a way to it by righteous work. And that, while it is right in Arabic, is forced and an innovation; and attributing it to the like of ʿAlī ﵁ is a greater innovation. In the poetry of one of the moderns:

> Ask a way (sal sabīlan) in it to the soul’s ease, / with a wine as though it were salsabīl.

“*A spring*” is a substitute for “*ginger*.” It is also said: their cup is mixed with ginger itself, or God creates its taste in it; and “*a spring*” on this saying is a substitute for “*a cup*,” as though it were said: and they are given to drink in it a cup, the cup of a spring. Or it is accusative of specification.

They were likened, in their beauty, the clearness of their colors and their being spread through their sitting places and dwellings, to scattered pearls. {p|673}From al-Maʾmūn: on the night Būrān bint al-Ḥasan ibn Sahl was brought to him as a bride, he was on a carpet woven of gold, and the women of the caliphal palace had scattered pearls on it. He looked at them scattered on that carpet, found the sight beautiful, and said, “*God bless Abū Nuwās! It is as though he saw this when he says:*

> As though the small and great of its bubbles / were pebbles of pearl on a ground of gold.”

It is also said: they were likened to moist pearls when scattered from their shells, because that is finer and has more water.

{L|You see|raʾayta} has no object, expressed or implied, so that it may spread and be general; as though it were said: and when seeing comes about there. And its meaning is that wherever the sight of the one who sees falls, its perceiving fastens only on much bliss and great dominion. “*There*” is in the place of the accusative as an adverbial; He means: in Paradise. Whoever said its meaning is “what is there” has erred, because “*there*” would be the complement of *mā*, and it is not allowed to drop the relative and leave the complement. “*Wonderful*” (*kabīran*, “great”): wide and wholesome. It is related that the lowest of the people of Paradise in station looks in his dominion a journey of a thousand years, and sees its furthest part as he sees its nearest. It is also said: it has no ending. It is also said: when they want a thing, it is. It is also said: the angels greet them and ask leave to come in to them.

*ʿĀlīhim* is read with the *yāʾ* quiescent, on the view that it is a subject whose predicate is “*clothes of green velvet*” (*thiyābu sundusin*, “garments of fine silk”), that is: what is above them of their dress is garments of fine silk. And *ʿāliyahum*, in the accusative, on the view that it is a circumstantial clause from the pronoun in “*Serving them*,” or in “*they will look like*”; that is: there go round among them youths, there being garments over those gone round to; or you would reckon them pearls, there being garments over them. It may also be meant: you see people of bliss and dominion, over whom are garments. *ʿĀliyatuhum*, in the nominative and accusative, on that. And *ʿalayhim*. *Khuḍrun* and *istabraqun* in the nominative, carried on *thiyābu*; and in the genitive, on *sundusin*. *Wa-istabraqa* is read in the accusative in the place of the genitive, as indeclinable because it is foreign; and that is a mistake, because it is indefinite and takes the article: you say *al-istabraq*. Unless Ibn Muḥayṣin claims that it is sometimes made a proper name for this kind of garment. *Wa-stabraqa* is read with the *hamza* joining and with *fatḥa*, on the view that it is named by *istafʿala* from *al-barīq*, glitter; and that is not sound either, because it is a well-known Arabized word, and its origin is *istabrah*. “*And silver ornaments*” (*wa-ḥullū*, “and they are adorned”) is joined to “*Serving them*.”

{Q} He has mentioned here that their bracelets are of silver, and in another place that they are of {p|674}gold. {A} Suppose it were said, “and they are adorned with bracelets of gold and of silver”: this is sound, without difficulty, on the view that they are given bracelets of both kinds, either by turns or together; as the women of this world pair kinds of jewelry and join them. And how fine it is for the wrist to have two bracelets in it, a bracelet of gold and a bracelet of silver! “*Pure drinks*”: not filth like the wine of this world; because its being filth is by the law, not by reason, and the abode is not an abode of obligation. Or because it was not pressed, so that greasy hands touched it and dirty feet trod it, and it was not put in jars and jugs whose cleaning was not attended to. Or because it does not turn to impurity, since it oozes as sweat from their bodies with a scent like the scent of musk. That is, it is said to the people of Paradise, “*This*”: and “this” points to what went before of God’s giving to them, “*is*” what you have been repaid with for your works, and your striving has been thanked by it; and the thanks is a figure.

Repeating the pronoun after putting it as the noun of *inna* is emphasis on emphasis for the meaning of God’s being singled out for the sending down; so that it may be settled in the soul of the Messenger of God ﷺ that since He is the One who sends down, His sending down, in whatever way it comes down, is only wisdom and rightness. As though it were said: none sent down the Qurʾān on you, sent down in portions, in parts, but I, not another; and you have known Me as wise, doing all I do by the motives of wisdom. And a far-reaching wisdom has called Me to send down on you the command to hold back and endure, and I will send down on you the command to fight and take vengeance after a while. “*You shall steadfastly carry out your Lord’s commandments*,” which issues from wisdom and His making matters hang on benefits, and His putting off your victory over your enemies of the people of Mecca; and do not obey any of them out of little patience with their harm and weariness at the delay of victory. They, for all their going too far in enmity and harm to him and those with him, used to call him to turn back from his matter, and offered him their wealth and marriage to the noblest of their daughters if he answered them.

{Q} They were all unbelievers; so what is the meaning of the division in His saying “*a sinner or an ungrateful one*”? {A} Its meaning is: do not obey of them one who commits what is sin, calling you to it; or does what is unbelief, calling you to it. Because they either call him to help them in a deed that is sin, or unbelief, or neither sin nor unbelief; so he was forbidden to help them in the two and not the third. It is also said: the sinner is ʿUtba, and the ungrateful al-Walīd; because ʿUtba was given to committing sins, taking up kinds of wickedness, and al-Walīd went to extremes in unbelief, {p|675}hard-bitted in insolence.

{Q} The meaning of “*or*” is: do not obey either of them; so why was the *wāw* not brought, so that it would be a prohibition of obeying both together? {A} Had it been said, “do not obey both,” he might obey one of them. When it is said, “do not obey either of them,” it is known that one who forbids obeying either of them forbids obeying both together the more; as when he is forbidden to say to his parents “*uff*,” it is known that he is forbidden to strike them, by way of the more so.

{L|And commemorate the name of your Lord day and night|wa-dhkuri sma rabbika bukratan wa-aṣīlan} and keep at the dawn and afternoon prayers. “*During the night, fall prostrate before Him*”: and in part of the night pray to Him. Or He means the sunset and evening prayers. He brought *min* in on the adverbial for partition, as it came in on the object in His saying “*He will then forgive you your sins*” (*yaghfir lakum min dhunūbikum*, Q 71:4). “*Glorify Him many a long night*”: and keep vigil for Him a long part of the night: two thirds of it, or half of it, or a third of it.

{L|These|inna hāʾulāʾi} unbelievers “*are preoccupied with this fleeting life*”: they prefer it to the Hereafter, as in His saying “*Indeed, you are preoccupied with this first life*” (Q 87:16). “*Just ahead of them*” (*warāʾahum*, “behind them”): before them, or behind their backs, paying it no heed. “*A heavy day*”: heavy was borrowed for its hardness and its terror, from the heavy thing that weighs down the one who carries it. Like it is “*Heavy it is, in the heavens and the earth*” (Q 7:187). *Al-asr* is binding and making fast; and from it is *usira l-rajulu*, when he was bound with the thong, which is *al-isār*; and “a horse firmly knit (*maʾsūr*) of build,” and “a shield bound (*maʾsūr*) with sinew.” And the meaning is: We made fast the joining of their bones one to another, and bound their joints with the sinews. Like it is their saying, “a girl tight-knit of build,” and “well-twisted.” “*whenever we will*,” We destroy them and “*we can substitute others in their place*” in firmness of build; He means the other bringing into being. It is also said: its meaning is, We replace them with others who obey; and its right would be to come with *in*, not *idhā*, as in His saying “*If you turn away, He will substitute other people in your place*” (Q 47:38), “*If He wills, He can annihilate you*” (Q 4:133).

{p|676}{L|This|hādhihi} points to the sūra, or to the nearby verses. “*Whoever wills*”: so whoever chooses good for himself and a fair outcome. Taking the way to God is an expression for drawing near to Him and seeking access by obedience. “*Whatever you will*” of obedience “*is in accordance with GOD’s will*” (*wa-mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa llāhu*), by compelling them to it. “*GOD is Omniscient*” of their states and what will be from them, “*Wise*” in that He created them with His knowledge of them. *Tashāʾūna* is read with *tāʾ*.

{Q} What is the place of *illā an yashāʾa llāhu* (“unless God wills”)? {A} The accusative, as an adverbial; its origin is “except at the time of God’s willing.” And likewise the reading of Ibn Masʿūd, *illā mā yashāʾu llāhu*, because *mā* with the verb is like *an* with it. “*He admits whomever He wills*”: they are the believers. *Al-ẓālimīna* is put in the accusative by a verb that “*He has prepared for them*” explains, like “He threatened” and “He repaid,” and the like of that. Ibn Masʿūd read *wa-li-l-ẓālimīna*, on: and He has prepared for the wrongdoers. Ibn al-Zubayr read *wa-l-ẓālimūna*, as subject. Other readings are better, for the match between the joined sentence and the one it is joined to being lost in it, along with its going against the codex.
