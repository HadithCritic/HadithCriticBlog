{L|O you hidden secret|al-muddaththir} is the one who wears the *dithār*, which is what is over the *shiʿār*, and that is the garment next to the body. From it is his saying ﵊: “*The Anṣār are the inner garment, and people are the outer garment*.” It is said: it is the first sūra revealed. Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh related from the Messenger of God ﷺ: “*I was on Mount Ḥirāʾ, and I was called: ‘Muḥammad, you are the Messenger of God.’ I looked to my right and my left and saw nothing; then I looked above me and saw something*.” In {p|645}the account of ʿĀʾisha: “*I looked above me, and there he was sitting on a throne between the heaven and the earth*,” meaning the angel who had called him, “*and I was terrified, and went back to Khadīja and said, ‘Cover me, cover me.’ And Gabriel came down and said, ‘O you cloaked one.’*” From al-Zuhrī: the first that was revealed was the sūra “*Read, in the name of your Lord*” up to His saying “*what he never knew*” (Q 96:1–5). The Messenger of God ﷺ grieved and began climbing the heights of the mountains; and Gabriel came to him and said, “*You are the prophet of God*.” He went back to Khadīja and said, “*Cover me, and pour cold water on me*”; and “*O you cloaked one*” was revealed. It is also said: he heard from Quraysh what he disliked and was distressed, and covered himself with his garment, thinking, as one in distress does; and he was commanded not to leave off warning them, even if they spoke ill to him and harmed him. From ʿIkrima: he read it as the passive participle, from *daththarahu*, and said: you have been cloaked with this matter, and it has been bound on you; as He said in al-Muzzammil.

{L|Come out|qum} from your bed; or rise with a rising of resolve and determination. “*And warn*”: then caution your people against the punishment of God if they do not believe. What is right is that the meaning is: then do the warning, without restricting it to anyone. “*Extol your Lord*”: and single out your Lord for magnifying, which is describing Him by greatness, and saying, “God is greatest.” It is related that when it was revealed, the Messenger of God ﷺ said, “*God is greatest*”; and Khadīja said it after him and rejoiced, and was sure it was revelation. It may also be taken as the magnifying of the prayer. The *fāʾ* came in for the meaning of the condition, as though it were said: whatever happens, do not leave off magnifying Him. “*Purify your garment*”: he was commanded that his garments be clean of impurities, because cleanness of garments is a condition in prayer, which is not sound without it; and it is better and more loved outside prayer; and it is ugly for the good believer to carry filth. It is also said: it is a command to shorten them, and to go against the Arabs in their lengthening their garments and trailing the skirts; and that is something with which hitting impurities cannot be guarded against. It is also said: it is a command to purify the self from deeds that are found filthy and habits that are found ugly. One says, “So-and-so is clean of garments,” and “clean of pocket, skirt and sleeves,” when they describe him as free of faults and of things that soil character; and “So-and-so is soiled of garments,” of the betrayer. That is because the garment is worn by the man and wraps around him, so he was alluded to by it. Do you not see their saying, “Zayd’s garment pleased me,” as they say, “Zayd’s mind and character pleased me”? And they say, “Glory is in his garment, and nobility under his robe.” And because, as a rule, whoever purifies his inside and cleans it takes care to purify the outside and clean it, and will have nothing but avoiding filth and preferring purity in everything. “*And the abomination*” is read with *kasra* and *ḍamma*, and it is the punishment. And its meaning is: shun what leads to it, of the worship of idols and other sins. And the meaning is to stay firm in shunning it, because he was free of it.

Al-Ḥasan read *wa-lā tamunna*. *Tastakthiru* is in the nominative, its place being in the accusative as a circumstantial clause, that is: and do not give, seeking much, seeing what you give as much, or seeking much. It is a prohibition of seeking increase, which is that he gives something while hoping to get back from the one given more than the gift; and this is allowed, and from it is the ḥadīth, “*The one who seeks increase is repaid for his gift*.” There are two faces in it. One of them: that it is a prohibition particular to the Messenger of God ﷺ, because God Most High chose for him the noblest manners and the best character. The second: that it is a prohibition of keeping clear, not of forbidding, for him and his community. Al-Ḥasan read *tastakthir*, quiescent. There are three faces in it: a substitute for “*do not grant favors*,” as though it were said, “Do not grant favors, do not count much,” on the view that it is from the reproachful reminding of favors (*mann*) in the saying of the Mighty and Majestic “*then do not follow their charity with insult or harm*” (Q 2:262); because the way of one who reproaches for what he gives is to count it much, that is, to see it as much and set store by it. Or that *tastakthiru* is likened to *ʿaḍud*, and made quiescent to lighten it. Or that the state of the pause is taken account of. Al-Aʿmash read it in the accusative, with *an* implied, as in his saying, “*O you who chide me for attending* (aḥḍura) *the battle*.” The reading of Ibn Masʿūd supports it: *wa-lā tamnun an tastakthira*. In the nominative, *an* may be dropped and its working voided, as *aḥḍuru l-waghā* is related in the nominative.

{L|Steadfastly commemorate your Lord|wa-li-rabbika fa-ṣbir} and for God’s face use patience. It is also said: under the harm of the idolaters. It is also said: in carrying out the obligations. From al-Nakhaʿī: in your giving; as though he joined it to what was before it, and made it patience in giving without counting much. The right face is that it is a command to the act itself, and that it takes in generally everything one is patient in bearing and patient in holding back from; and patience under the harm of the unbelievers is meant, because it is one of the things the general takes in.

The *fāʾ* in His saying “*Then, when the horn is blown*” is for cause, as though He said: be patient under their harm, for before them is a hard day on which they will meet the outcome of their harm, and you will meet the outcome of your patience under it. The *fāʾ* in “*then that*” is for the answer.

{Q} By what is *idhā* put in the accusative, and how was it sound for *yawmaʾidhin* (“on that day”) to be an adverbial for “*a difficult day*”? {A} *Idhā* is put in the accusative by what {p|647}the answer points to, because the meaning is: then when the trumpet is sounded, the matter becomes hard for the unbelievers. What allowed *yawmaʾidhin* (“on that day”) to be an adverbial for “*a difficult day*” is that the meaning is: then that time of sounding is the befalling of a hard day; because the Day of Resurrection comes and befalls when the trumpet is sounded. They differ whether it is the first blast or the second. It may also be that *yawmaʾidhin* is indeclinable, in the place of the nominative, a substitute for “*then that*,” and “*a difficult day*” the predicate; as though it were said: then the day of sounding is a hard day.

{Q} Then what is the benefit of His saying “*not easy*,” when “*hard*” makes it needless? {A} When He said “*for the unbelievers*,” and restricted the hardness to them, He said “*not easy*” to give notice that it will not be easy and light for them as it will be for the believers; so that He joins the threat to the unbelievers and the increase of their rage with good news to the believers and their consolation. It may also be meant that it is hard, with no hope of its turning easy, as the easing of hard things in the matters of this world is hoped for.

{L|As an individual|waḥīdan} is a circumstantial clause from God, Mighty and Majestic, on two meanings. One of them: leave Me alone with him, for I will suffice you in taking vengeance on him in place of every avenger. The second: I created him alone; no one shared with Me in creating him. Or a circumstantial clause from the one created, on the meaning: I created him while he was alone, single, with no wealth and no child; as in His saying “*You have come back to us as individuals, just as we created you the first time*” (Q 6:94). It is said: it was revealed about al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīra al-Makhzūmī, and he was nicknamed among his people “the Unique” (*al-waḥīd*). Perhaps he was nicknamed that after the verse came down. If he was nicknamed it before, then it is a mockery of him and of his nickname, and a turning of it from the aim they intended, of praising him and lauding him as the unique one of his people for his leadership, his wealth and his precedence in this world, to the face of blame and fault: which is that he was created alone, with no wealth and no child, and God gave him that, and he was ungrateful for God’s blessing, associated others with Him and mocked His religion. “*Lots of money*” (*mālan mamdūdan*, “extended wealth”): spread out, much; or supplied with growth, from “the river *madda*,” and “another river *maddahu*.” It is said: he had crops, herds and trade. From Ibn ʿAbbās: it is the kinds of wealth he had between Mecca and al-Ṭāʾif. It is also said: he had an orchard at al-Ṭāʾif whose fruit did not stop, summer and winter. It is also said: he had a thousand *mithqāl*s; it is also said four thousand; it is also said nine thousand; it is also said a thousand thousand. From Ibn {p|648}Jurayj: an income month after month.

{L|And children to behold|wa-banīna shuhūdan} present with him at Mecca, not leaving him to go about some work or trade; because they were provided for, for the abundance of their father’s blessing and their having no need of earning and seeking a living themselves. So he took comfort in them; his heart was not busied by their absence and the fear of the perils of travel for them, and he did not grieve at parting from them and longing for them. It may also be that its meaning is that they were men who attended assemblies and gatherings with him; or whose testimony was heard in what was brought to judgment. From Mujāhid: he had ten sons. It is also said: thirteen. It is also said: seven, all of them men: al-Walīd ibn al-Walīd, Khālid, ʿUmāra, Hishām, al-ʿĀṣ, Qays and ʿAbd Shams. Three of them became Muslim: Khālid, Hishām and ʿUmāra. “*I made everything easy for him*”: and I spread out for him broad standing and leadership among his people; so I completed on him My two blessings of wealth and standing, and their coming together is perfection with the people of this world. From it is people’s saying, “May God keep up your support and your smoothing,” by which they mean more standing and respect. Al-Walīd was among the notables of Quraysh and their great men; for that reason he was nicknamed “the Unique” and “the sweet basil of Quraysh.” “*Then he hopes*” is thinking his hope and greed far-fetched and disavowing them. He means there is no more than what he has been given, in breadth and abundance. It is also said: he used to say, “*If Muḥammad is truthful, Paradise was created only for me*.” *Kallā* (“no”): a restraint of him, and a cutting off of his hope and his greed. “*He has been stubborn toward Our signs*” gives the reason for the restraint by way of a fresh start, as though someone said, “Why is he not given more?” And it was said: he was stubborn toward the signs of the Giver of blessing, and by that was ungrateful for His blessing; and the ungrateful does not deserve more. It is related that after this verse came down he went on losing his wealth until he died.

{L|I will increasingly punish him|sa-urhiquhu ṣaʿūdan} I will cover him with a mountain road hard of ascent; and it is a proverb for the hard, climbing punishment he will meet, which cannot be borne. From the Prophet ﷺ: “*He will be made to climb a mountain road in the Fire. Whenever he puts his hand on it, it melts, and when he lifts it, it comes back; and when he puts his foot, it melts, and when he lifts it, it comes back*.” And from him ﵇: “*The ascent is a mountain of fire which he climbs for seventy autumns, then falls down it in the same way, forever*.” “*He thought*” gives the reason for the threat, as though God Most High hastened poverty after riches and abasement after might on him in this world for his stubbornness, and he will be punished in the Hereafter with the hardest and most hideous punishment, for his reaching by stubbornness its utmost limit in his thinking and his calling the Qurʾān sorcery. It may also be that the word of restraint is followed by His saying “*I will make him climb an ascent*,” {p|649}as a refutation of his claim that Paradise was created only for him, and a telling that he is of the people of the Fire with the hardest punishment; and the reason for that is given by his stubbornness. And His saying “*he thought*” is then a substitute for His saying “*he has been stubborn toward Our signs*,” making clear the essence of his stubbornness. And its meaning is: he thought what he would say about the Qurʾān, “*and he measured*” in himself what he would say and got it ready. “*Perish he, how he measured!*” is a wondering at his measuring and his hitting the joint in it, and his shooting at the mark Quraysh were aiming for. Or a praise of him by way of mocking him. Or it is the relating of what they repeated of their saying, “Perish he, how he measured!”, mocking them and their delight in his measuring and their thinking his saying great. The meaning of one’s saying, “God kill him, how brave he is!” and “God shame him, how good a poet he is!” is to give a sign that he has reached the point at which he deserves to be envied, and his envier calls that down on him.

It is related that al-Walīd said to the Banū Makhzūm: “*By God, I have just heard from Muḥammad speech that is not of the speech of humans nor of the speech of the jinn. It has sweetness, and grace is on it; its top is fruitful and its bottom abundant; it rises above and is not risen above*.” Quraysh said, “*By God, al-Walīd has turned Sabian; by God, all of Quraysh will turn Sabian*.” Abū Jahl said, “*I will deal with him for you*.” He sat by him, sorrowful, and spoke to him with what heated him. He got up and came to them and said, “*You claim that Muḥammad is mad: have you seen him choke? And you say he is a soothsayer: have you ever seen him practice soothsaying? And you claim he is a poet: have you ever seen him take up poetry? And you claim he is a liar: have you ever found any lying in him?*” They said to all of that, “*By God, no*.” Then they said, “*Then what is he?*” He thought, and said, “*He is nothing but a sorcerer. Have you not seen him part a man from his family, his children and his clients? And what he says is nothing but sorcery he hands down from Musaylima and from the people of Babylon*.” The assembly shook with joy, and they parted delighted with his saying and wondering at it.

{L|He looked|thumma naẓara} into the faces of the people; then he knit his brow; then he crept back, turning away, and looked askance, proud, when the hideous word came to his mind and he was about to fling it. He described the shapes he took on until he worked out what he worked out, in mockery of him. It is also said: he measured what he would say, then looked into it, then frowned when the devices failed him and he did not know what to say. It is also said: he frowned in the face of the Messenger of God ﷺ. “*Then he turned away*” from the truth “*arrogantly*” toward it, and said what he said. “*He looked*” is joined to “*he reflected, then decided*,” and the imprecation is a parenthesis between them.

{Q} What is the meaning of the *thumma* that comes in the repeating of the imprecation? {A} To point to the second round being stronger than the first; like it is his saying, “*O be safe, then be safe, then be safe*.”

{Q} {p|650}What is the meaning of the one in the middle, between the verbs after it? {A} To point to his having taken his time in reflecting and gone slowly; as though between the verbs set in order there were a lapse and a distance.

{Q} Then why was *fa-qāla* (“and he said”) said with *fāʾ*, after what was before it had been joined with *thumma*? {A} Because when the word came to his mind after the search, he could not hold back from speaking it without delay.

{Q} Then why was the particle of joining not put between the two sentences? {A} Because the second runs from the first as the emphasis from the emphasized.

{Q} He has made the unbelievers’ being tried by the number of the guards of Hell a cause of the certainty of the people of the Book, the increase of the believers’ faith, and the mocking of the unbelievers and the hypocrites; so what is the face of the soundness of that? {A} He did not make their being tried by the number a cause of that; it is only the number itself that was made the cause. That is because what is meant by His saying “*we assigned their number (19) … to disturb the disbelievers*” (*wa-mā jaʿalnā ʿiddatahum illā fitnatan li-lladhīna kafarū*, “and We have made their number only a trial for those who disbelieve”) is: and We have made their number only nineteen. So He put *fitnatan li-lladhīna kafarū* (“a trial for those who disbelieve”) in the place of “*nineteen*,” because the state of this number, falling one short of the tens of twenty, is that one who does not believe in God and His wisdom is tried by it, and objects and mocks, and does not submit as the believer submits even if the face of the wisdom is hidden from him. As though it were said: We have made their number a number whose way is that one is tried by it, for the sake of the certainty of the believers, the bewilderment of the unbelievers, and the certainty of {p|652}the people of the Book, because their number is nineteen in the two Books, so when they hear its like in the Qurʾān they are sure it is sent down from God; and the believers’ increase in faith, for their affirming that as they affirmed everything else sent down, and for what they saw of the people of the Book submitting and affirming that it is so.

{Q} Why did He say “*to remove all traces of doubt from the hearts of Christians, Jews, as well as the believers*,” when certainty and increase of faith point to the absence of doubt? {A} Because when establishing certainty and denying doubt are joined for them, it is stronger and more forceful in describing them by peace of soul and coolness of breast; and because in it is a hint at the state of those besides them, as though He said: and that their state may differ from the state of the doubting, the wavering, of the people of hypocrisy and unbelief.

{Q} How did He mention those in whose hearts is a sickness, who are the hypocrites, when the sūra is Meccan, and there was no hypocrisy at Mecca; it only sprouted at Medina? {A} Its meaning is: and that the hypocrites who will sprout in time to come at Medina after the Hijra, “*and the unbelievers*” at Mecca, may say “*What did GOD mean by this allegory?*” And there is nothing in that but telling of what will be, like the other tellings of unseen things; and that does not go against the sūra’s being Meccan. It may also be that by sickness doubt and wavering are meant, because most of the people of Mecca were doubting and some of them sure it was a lie.

{Q} He has given as the reason for making them nineteen the certainty, the absence of doubt, and the saying of the hypocrites and the unbelievers what they said. Grant that certainty and the absence of doubt can be aims; how can the saying of the hypocrites and the unbelievers be an aim? {A} The *lām* gave the meaning of reason and cause; and it is not necessary that a reason be an aim. Do you not see your saying, “I left the town for fear of evil”? You have made the fear the reason for your leaving, and it is not your aim.

{L|Allegory|mathalan} is specification for “*this*,” or a circumstantial clause from it, as in His saying “*here is GOD’s camel, to serve as a sign for you*” (Q 7:73).

{Q} Why did they call it a likeness? {A} It is a borrowing from the coined proverb, because it is of what is strange in speech and novel; they found this number strange and novel. And the meaning is: what thing did God mean by this odd number, and what aim did He intend in making the angels nineteen, not an even twenty? And what they meant was to deny it at the root, and that it is not from God, and that had it been from God it would not have come with this short number.

The *kāf* in “*thus*” is in the accusative, and “that” points to what was before it, of the meaning of leading astray and guiding; that is: like that mentioned leading astray and guiding, He leads the unbelievers astray and guides the believers. He means: He does a good deed built on wisdom and rightness, and the believers see it as wisdom and submit to it, because of their belief that God’s acts are all good and wise, and so it increases them in faith; and the unbelievers deny it and doubt it, and so it increases them in unbelief and straying.

And the meaning is: every soul is held in pledge with God for its earning, not redeemed, “*Except for those on the right*”: for they have redeemed their necks from it by what they made good of their earning, as the pledger frees his pledge by paying the due. From ʿAlī ﵁: he explained the companions of the right as the children, because they have no works to be held in pledge for. From Ibn ʿAbbās ﵁: they are the angels. “*While in Paradise*”: that is, they are in gardens whose description cannot be reached. “*They will ask*” “*about the guilty*” (*yatasāʾalūna ʿani l-mujrimīn*): some of them ask others about them; or they ask others about them, as in your saying, “I called him,” and “we called one another.”

{Q} How does His saying “*What brought you to this retribution?*”, which is a question to the criminals, fit His saying “*they will ask*” “*about the guilty*,” which is a question about them? It would only fit if it were said, “They ask the criminals, ‘What brought you?’” {A} “*What brought you*” is not the setting out of the asking about them; it is only the relating of the saying of those asked about them. For those asked put to the askers what passed between them and the criminals, and say, “We said to them, ‘*What brought you into Saqar?*’ They said, ‘*We were not of those who prayed*.’” But the speech was brought with dropping and shortening, as is the way of the revelation in the strangeness of its arrangement. *Al-khawḍ* is plunging into falsehood and what is not proper.

{Q} Why do they ask them, when they know that? {A} To rebuke them and make them grieve; and so that God’s relating of that in His Book may be a reminder to the hearers. Some have supported the explaining of the companions of the right as the children by this: that they only asked them because they were children who did not know what makes the Fire due.

{Q} Do they mean that each one of them entered the Fire for all four of these together, or that some of them entered it for this and some for that? {A} It may be both together.

{Q} Why did they put the denying last, when it is the greatest of them? {A} They meant that after all that, they were deniers of the Day of Judgment, making the denying great; as in His saying “*And being one of those who believe*” (Q 90:17). “*Certainty*” is death and what comes before it. That is: if all the intercessors interceded for them, of the angels, the prophets and others, their intercession would not profit them; because intercession is for whom God has approved, and they are those He is angry with. And in it is proof that intercession profits on that day, because it increases the degrees of the approved.

{L|To this reminder|ʿani l-tadhkirati} from the reminding, which is admonition; He means the Qurʾān or other admonitions. “*Turning away*” is in the accusative as a circumstantial clause, as in your saying, “What is the matter with you, standing?” *Al-mustanfira* is the one very much given to bolting, as though it sought bolting from itself, in its gathering itself for it and driving itself to it. *Mustanfara* is read with *fatḥa*, and it is the one made to bolt, driven to bolting. *Al-qaswara* is a band of archers who hunt them. It is also said: the lion; one says, “lions *qasāwir*”; and it is *faʿwala* from *al-qasr*, which is force and overpowering; and of its pattern is *al-ḥaydara*, among the names of the lion. From Ibn ʿAbbās: the low noise of people and their voices. From ʿIkrima: the darkness of the night. He likened them, in their turning away from the Qurʾān and from listening to the reminder and the admonition, and their fleeing from it, to asses that strain in bolting from what has frightened them. In likening them to asses there is plain blame and a clear making ugly of their state, as in His saying “*like the donkey carrying great works of literature*” (Q 62:5), and a witness against them of stupidity and little sense. You do not see the like of the bolting of wild asses and their running on in flight when something alarms them; for that reason most of the Arabs’ likenings, in describing camels and how hard they go, are to the asses and their running when they came down to water and sensed a hunter at it.

{L|The scripture|ṣuḥufan munashsharatan} sheets that are spread and read, like the letters people write to one another.
