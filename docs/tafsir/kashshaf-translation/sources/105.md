surah: 105
title: Sūrat al-Fīl (105)
revelation: Meccan. Five verses. [Revealed after al-Kāfirūn (109).]

::: passage 105:1-5 | 4:797-800

It is related that Abraha ibn al-Ṣabbāḥ al-Ashram, king of Yemen on behalf of Aṣḥama the Negus, built a church in Ṣanʿāʾ, named it al-Qullays, and meant to turn the pilgrims toward it. A man of Kināna went out and relieved himself in it by night, which enraged him. It is also said that a party of Arabs lit a fire and the wind carried it and burned the church. So he swore he would destroy the Kaʿba, and he set out with the Abyssinians. He had with him an elephant of his called Maḥmūd, which was strong and huge, and twelve other elephants; it is also said eight; it is also said he had a thousand elephants with him; and it is also said it was the only one.

When he reached al-Mughammas, ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib came out to him and offered him a third of the wealth of Tihāma to turn back. He refused, drew up his army and sent the elephant in front. Whenever they turned it toward the Sanctuary it knelt and would not move, and whenever they turned it toward Yemen or any other direction it broke into a trot. Then God sent birds, black ones (it is also said green, and also white), each bird with a stone in its beak and two stones in its feet, larger than a lentil and smaller than a chickpea. From Ibn ʿAbbās ﵄: he saw about a *qafīz* of them at the house of Umm Hāniʾ, streaked with red like the onyx of Ẓafār. The stone would fall on a man’s head and come out of his rear, and on each stone was the name of the one it would fall on. They fled and perished on every road and at every watering place. Abraha fell sick, his fingertips and limbs dropped off, and he did not die until his chest burst open over his heart. His vizier Abū Yaksūm got away, with his bird circling above him, until he reached the Negus and told him the story. When he finished it the stone fell on him, and he fell dead before him. It is also said that Abraha was the grandfather of {p|798}the Negus who lived in the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ, [and that this happened] forty years [before him]; it is also said, twenty-three years. From ʿĀʾisha ﵂: “*I saw the elephant’s driver and its keeper, both blind and crippled, begging for food*.”

It is also related that Abraha seized two hundred camels belonging to ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, and he went out to him about them. Abraha was struck by his presence, for he was a large, handsome man, and it was said to him, “This is the chief of Quraysh and the master of the caravans of Mecca, who feeds people on the plain and the wild beasts on the mountaintops.” When he stated what he wanted, Abraha said, “*You have fallen in my eyes. I came to destroy the House that is your religion and the religion of your fathers, your protection and your honor from ancient times, and a few camels taken from you have made you forget it*.” He said, “*I am the owner of the camels, and the House has a Lord who will protect it*.” Then he went back, came to the door of the House, took hold of its ring and said: “*O God, a man protects his household, so protect Your household. Let their cross and their might never overcome Your might tomorrow. If You will leave them and our Kaʿba, then do as You see fit*.” {p|799}“*O Lord, I hope for no one but You against them. O Lord, keep them from Your sanctuary*.” He turned as he prayed, and there were birds coming from the direction of Yemen. He said, “*By God, these are strange birds, neither of the sea nor of Tihāma*.”

It is also related that the people of Mecca took possession of their goods, and ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib gathered sacks of their jewels and gold, and this was the source of his wealth. From Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī ﵁: he was asked about the birds and said, “*The doves of Mecca are of their kind*.” It is also said that they came in the evening and fell on them in the morning. From ʿIkrima: whoever was struck by them broke out in smallpox, and it was the first smallpox to appear.

*Alam tar* is also read, with *sukūn* on the *rāʾ*, to make the effect of the jussive fully plain. The meaning: you have seen the traces of what God did to the Abyssinians and heard the reports of it from so many that they took the place of seeing it yourself. *Kayfa* (“how”) is in the accusative through “*your Lord did*,” not through “*Have you noted*” (*alam tara*, “have you not seen”), because of the interrogative sense in *kayfa*.

{L|To backfire|fī taḍlīl} in loss and failure. One says *ḍallala kaydahu* when he makes it go astray and come to nothing. From this is the saying of God Most High “*the scheming of the disbelievers is always wicked*” (*wa-mā kaydu l-kāfirīna illā fī ḍalāl*, “and the plotting of the unbelievers is only in error,” Q 40:25). Imruʾ al-Qays was called “the king who went astray” (*al-malik al-ḍillīl*) because he lost (*ḍallala*) his father’s kingdom, that is, let it go to waste. He means that they plotted against the House first by building al-Qullays, intending to cancel its standing by turning the pilgrims’ faces toward the church, and He brought their plot to nothing by setting it on fire; and they plotted against it a second time by intending to destroy it, and He brought that to nothing by sending the birds upon them.

{L|Swarms|abābīl} groups; the singular is *ibāla*. Among their proverbs is “*a handful on top of an ibāla*,” the *ibāla* being a large bundle; the flock of birds, packed together, is likened to the bundle. It is also said that *abābīl* is like *ʿabādīd* and *shamāṭīṭ*, which have no singular. Abū Ḥanīfa ﵀ read *yarmīhim* (“He strikes them”), that is, God Most High, or the birds, since *ṭayr* is a masculine collective noun and is treated as feminine only by sense.

{L|Hard stones|sijjīl} as if it were the proper name of the register in which the punishment of the unbelievers is written, as *sijjīn* is the proper name of the register of their deeds; as if it were said: with stones from among the punishment written and recorded. It is derived from *isjāl*, which is sending, because the punishment is described in that way: “*He sent upon them swarms of birds*” (Q 105:3), “*we sent upon them {p|800}the flood*” (Q 7:133). From Ibn ʿAbbās ﵄: of baked clay, as bricks are baked. It is also said that it is Arabized from *sang-o-gil* (“stone and clay”). It is also said that it means of severe punishment, and they cite the verse of Ibn Muqbil, “*blows that heroes urged on one another, sijjīlan*”; but the word there is *sijjīnan*, and the poem is a well-known ode rhyming in *nūn* in his collected poems.

He likened them to the leaves of crops when they are eaten, that is, when blight (*al-akkāl*) falls on them, which is worms eating them; or to straw that beasts have eaten and then dropped as dung, though it is put in keeping with the decorum of the Qurʾān, as in His saying “*Both of them used to eat the food*” (Q 5:75). Or what is meant is that its grain was eaten and it was left empty of it.

{From|the Messenger of God ﷺ} “*Whoever recites Sūrat al-Fīl, God will spare him, for the days of his life, from being swallowed by the earth and from being transformed*.”
